The Credit Turn

The AI supercycle is shifting from the scarcity of computing capacity to the scarcity of capital capable of earning an adequate return.

FdK Francesco de Leo Kaufmann October 8, 2026 8 min read Capital

“The next economy will be won not by those who deploy the most capital, but by those who transform capital into the greatest productive advantage.”

Five developments reveal a structural shift in the global corporate economy. From technological acceleration to capital discipline, a new hierarchy of corporate winners is emerging — and the fundamental question is changing with it.

Samsung has projected 107.4 trillion won ($80.2 billion) in third-quarter operating profit, almost nine times its year-earlier result, as AI-driven memory shortages propel semiconductor earnings to extraordinary levels. These are preliminary estimates, with detailed results expected October 29.

Schneider Electric has agreed to acquire PTC for an implied $23.7 billion enterprise value, Broadcom will provide Anthropic with up to $42 billion in financing, Micron faces a 99% strike-authorization vote at its Taoyuan union, and the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield sits near 5.30%. These developments converge on one conclusion: the fundamental question is no longer simply which corporation is deploying AI fastest, but which corporation can sustain acceleration while improving the economics of every incremental dollar committed.

The AI supercycle is moving from the scarcity of computing capacity to the scarcity of capital capable of earning an adequate return.

The Five Corporate Signals That Matter

Where the evidence points

  1. Samsung's preliminary third-quarter results are the clearest immediate evidence of the earnings leverage available to manufacturers controlling scarce memory capacity. AMD's Lisa Su confirmed on October 7 that the company continues to explore memory and foundry cooperation with Samsung while emphasizing its dependence on Korean suppliers. The critical second-order effect: scarcity-driven pricing power can attract new capacity, competition and eventual margin normalization. signal: Reward durable manufacturing advantages, but distinguish structural profit growth from temporary scarcity rents.

  2. Schneider's acquisition of PTC combines energy management, automation, product design and industrial data. Management forecasts roughly €250 million in annual cost synergies by year three and €800 million in revenue synergies, financed with €5–6 billion of equity and €16–17 billion of new debt, with closing anticipated by Q3 2027. The capital-allocation case now depends on execution, synergy realization and financing cost. signal: HOLD / REPRICE — strategic acceleration is evident; acquisition economics remain to be demonstrated.

  3. Amazon's reported ~$8 billion chip sale-and-leaseback and Broadcom's Anthropic arrangements point to equipment ownership, compute consumption and infrastructure financing becoming increasingly separable. But legal separation does not eliminate economic risk — a provider may reduce recorded capital while retaining substantial lease obligations, demand exposure or guarantees. signal: Analyze cash obligations and recourse across the entire financing structure, not reported capex alone.

  4. Micron's Taoyuan union announced on October 7 that 99% of participating members authorized strike action; no date has been set. The October 5 Netlist settlement, involving $600 million over five years, separately reduces legal uncertainty at a financial cost. The economic value of scarce technology can increase labor's bargaining power. signal: ACCUMULATE ON EVIDENCE, with a heightened execution-risk test.

  5. Financing activity involving Broadcom, SpaceX and other major technology borrowers is becoming relevant to the broader bond market — particularly as sovereign financing conditions tighten simultaneously. The risk is that larger commitments become increasingly sensitive to interest rates, refinancing conditions and the timing of AI monetization. signal: Track credit spreads, financing costs, lease-adjusted leverage and the maturity structure of infrastructure obligations.

The Two Pillars That Matter Most Now

Capital Formation vs Resilience

12%

Capital Formation — accelerating

Companies are acquiring, building, leasing and financing infrastructure at extraordinary scale. Financing innovation and larger capital commitments are strengthening (↑↑).

9%

Resilience — harder to preserve

Larger fixed obligations, concentrated counterparties and higher borrowing costs reduce flexibility (↓). The divergence between these pillars is becoming one of the most consequential indicators in the benchmark.

The Evidence-to-Investment Bridge

The Five Forces Reshaping Corporate Competition

Second-order effects

01

Capital markets become part of the production system

Coordinated financing across infrastructure owners, suppliers, cloud providers and institutional investors creates opportunity — but also the risk that distress in one participant affects many. Counterparty concentration becomes a material component of technology valuation.

02

Industrial AI could trigger a new rotation

As deployment matures, investors will scrutinize realized productivity gains, potentially benefiting industrial software, engineering platforms and enterprise applications. Timing depends on adoption, realized savings and procurement cycles.

03

Energy and network access will determine returns

Electricity availability, permitting speed, transmission capacity and cooling influence both deployment schedules and financial returns. Infrastructure execution becomes a valuation variable.

04

Scarcity will produce its own counterforces

High margins incentivize new capacity; demand for specialist talent raises compensation; heavy borrowing raises financing costs. Distinguish durable barriers from advantages that attract their own competition.

05

Europe's challenge is becoming more selective

Schneider's industrial-software strategy illustrates a potential European advantage in connecting AI to physical production, while automotive restructuring illustrates the cost of simultaneous technological and commercial disruption.

What to watch

Five Decisive Takeaways

The economics of production and capital

  • The capital hurdle is rising — infrastructure investment must justify itself against higher financing costs and larger contractual obligations.
  • Semiconductor scarcity remains powerful — but extraordinary margins will attract competition and new operational pressures.
  • Industrial AI is becoming a distinct investment opportunity — integrating software, engineering and physical assets could create durable advantages.
  • Financial resilience is becoming a differentiator — stronger cash generation and strategic flexibility better sustain acceleration.
  • The next rotation will be driven by economic proof — sustained productivity, cash generation and returns on incremental capital.
