The Great Split Reaches the Architecture of Financial Trust

Three interventions in Bangkok on October 17 reveal that AI is accelerating finance faster than the supervisory and legal frameworks meant to govern it.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann October 18, 2026 5 min read Capital

“Intelligence without accountability is not financial progress.”

Three consequential developments from the October 17 international banking discussions in Bangkok sharpen the central-bank response to the AI Supercycle.

The Bank of England has elevated legal accountability for autonomous AI agents into a financial-stability concern. The IMF has called for coordinated AI-risk recommendations from the BIS, FSB and IMF. And China's central bank is seeking stronger international surveillance of sovereign-debt risks and capital-flow spillovers.

Together, these interventions expose a fundamental problem: AI is accelerating financial decision-making and capital formation faster than existing supervisory and legal frameworks are adapting. The Great Split is becoming a divergence not only in productivity and access to capital, but also in the institutional capacity to govern increasingly autonomous financial systems.

01

The Bank of England: Who Is Responsible When AI Moves Money?

At the Group of Thirty International Banking Seminar on October 17, Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey identified an emerging gap in financial regulation.

AI agents can increasingly execute financial transactions, but they cannot themselves bear legal responsibility in the way regulated institutions and individuals can.

Bailey called for stronger model testing, clearer accountability and fraud safeguards incorporated into financial infrastructure from the outset. This extends the supervisory debate beyond conventional model risk.

Transmission

AI agent autonomy → financial execution → accountability gaps → operational and legal risk. The second-order consequence could be substantial. As autonomous systems assume greater responsibility for payments, treasury operations and portfolio management, institutions must establish who authorises transactions, who can intervene and who bears responsibility when systems malfunction. This is particularly relevant to the emerging combination of agentic AI, tokenised deposits and programmable settlement infrastructure. Bailey's intervention reinforces Singapore's earlier work on runtime safeguards, but adds a critical dimension: technological autonomy cannot imply the disappearance of institutional accountability.

02

The BIS–IMF–FSB: Toward Coordinated AI Supervision

The most important institutional signal came from IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva.

During the same October 17 discussion, moderated by BIS General Manager Pablo Hernández de Cos, Georgieva called for the IMF, Financial Stability Board and BIS to develop joint recommendations by the next IMF–World Bank Spring Meetings. This was a proposal, not an announced agreement or binding international framework. Nevertheless, it is consequential.

The AI supervisory agenda is increasingly moving beyond individual institutions and national jurisdictions. Three risks require particular attention: autonomous agents operating across financial systems, common dependence on a small number of technology providers, and financial-market reactions that become synchronised during periods of stress.

Bank of Korea Governor Hyun Song Shin emphasised that traditional safeguards—including capital requirements, leverage limits and collateral standards—remain essential.

The deeper implication is that more sophisticated algorithms do not eliminate the financial constraints that produce market contagion. Different AI models can still generate similar financial behaviour when institutions confront common leverage and liquidity pressures.

03

China: The AI Capital Boom Becomes an International Surveillance Issue

A separate intervention from People's Bank of China Governor Pan Gongsheng adds an important monetary dimension.

On October 17, Pan supported stronger IMF surveillance of fiscal vulnerabilities in major advanced economies and their international spillovers. He called for greater attention to capital flows and financial factors when evaluating global imbalances. His intervention comes amid rising sovereign borrowing costs and exceptional AI investment requirements, both contributing to concerns about global financing conditions.

The significance is the growing international attention to the interaction between private AI capital formation and sovereign financing needs.

Mechanism

AI capital demand → competition for financing → higher borrowing costs → sovereign and corporate vulnerabilities. AI is not the only driver of higher yields. Fiscal deficits, inflation expectations, monetary policy and geopolitical uncertainty also matter. But the increasing scale of AI investment makes its contribution to global capital demand an issue that international financial institutions can no longer ignore.

04

The Monetary Consequence: Two Kinds of Velocity

The latest developments reinforce an important distinction. The AI Supercycle is accelerating both economic activity and financial transmission.

The first affects capital expenditure, labour demand, electricity consumption and ultimately productivity. The second affects financial transactions, portfolio adjustments, collateral requirements and the propagation of market disturbances. Central banks must manage both.

The tension between near-term AI investment inflation and eventual Industrial-AI deflation remains unresolved. No new policy-rate decision or formal neutral-rate revision was established by these latest interventions.

However, the emerging supervisory debate suggests that AI could transform financial stability before its full productivity effects become visible in macroeconomic statistics. That is a fundamental sequencing risk.

Five Takeaways for Investors and European Policymakers
  • Financial institutions: Effective accountability for autonomous AI agents will become increasingly important to operational resilience and institutional risk management.
  • Technology providers: Model testing, auditability, authorisation controls and secure transaction execution may become significant competitive differentiators.
  • Capital markets: Investors should monitor how AI infrastructure financing interacts with sovereign issuance, refinancing conditions and financial-market liquidity.
  • European supervision: The ECB and European banking authorities should connect AI governance, third-party technology concentration and liquidity stress testing rather than treating them as separate regulatory questions.
  • European competitiveness: Europe needs both faster Industrial-AI capital formation and reliable governance frameworks. Regulatory resilience is necessary, but it cannot substitute for productive investment, affordable energy and technological diffusion.
05

The Final Take — Intelligence Without Accountability Is Not Financial Progress

The October 17 interventions expose a new frontier in the AI Supercycle. The question is no longer simply whether autonomous systems can make financial decisions faster or more efficiently. It is whether financial institutions, central banks and supervisors can preserve accountability, liquidity and public trust as those decisions become increasingly automated.

The emerging international response combines traditional financial safeguards with new controls over autonomous execution. For investors, financial resilience will become an increasingly important determinant of sustainable returns. For Europe, the challenge is to build a financial system capable of financing the AI transition without becoming disproportionately exposed to its operational and capital-market risks.

The next Great Split will separate financial systems capable of governing intelligent capital at machine speed from those in which technological acceleration outpaces institutional resilience.
