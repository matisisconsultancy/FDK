The Great Split Is Now Capital Formation vs. Demand

AI investment is accelerating; consumption, credit formation and employment absorption are not.

FdK Francesco de Leo Kaufmann September 15, 2026 7 min read Macro

“The scarcity of the last decade was productive capacity. The scarcity of the next may be demand capable of absorbing it.”

The most consequential development of the week is no longer another record equity close or another AI infrastructure announcement. It is the widening divergence between where capital is being created and where demand is being absorbed.

Can the Next Economy sustain extraordinary capital formation if the broader economy progressively loses its capacity to absorb employment, credit and consumption?

U.S. retail sales unexpectedly fell 0.6% in July, the first decline in nine months and the largest in 14 months. The GDP-sensitive control group fell 0.4%. Consumer sentiment dropped to 51.0 in August. Yet the S&P 500 remains less than 0.2% below Thursday's record, while aggregate Q2 profits for S&P 500 companies are up roughly 52%, driven disproportionately by AI heavyweights.

China provides an even more extreme signal: July new yuan loans contracted by a record 340 billion yuan, despite falling real borrowing costs. Household lending contracted by 460.3 billion yuan. Europe, meanwhile, grew 0.4% quarter on quarter in Q2 — but employment increased only 0.1%. European corporate earnings are now expected to rise 23.4%, their strongest performance in almost four years.

Three economies. Three manifestations of the same underlying phenomenon. This is The Great Split moving from financial markets into the real economy. Because today is Saturday, the market references below use Friday, August 14 closes and frame positioning for Monday, August 17 and the week ahead.

THE GREAT SPLIT

Profits vs. wages. Productivity vs. employment. Capital formation vs. consumption capacity. The split is no longer a market narrative — it is becoming an economic architecture.

01

Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy

Where capital diverges from demand

  1. July retail sales fell 0.6%, consumer sentiment deteriorated sharply, and July employment had already surprised on the downside. Yet corporate earnings remain exceptionally strong, with AI-heavyweight profits driving much of the S&P 500's Q2 expansion.

    The AI economy does not require household consumption to finance every new data center. Hyperscalers, sovereign funds, private credit, banks and infrastructure investors can continue allocating hundreds of billions even while household demand weakens — creating the possibility of an investment boom coexisting with a consumption slowdown. signal: The split is migrating from AI-vs-non-AI toward capital formation vs. consumption capacity.

  2. China's July bank lending contracted by 340 billion yuan against expectations for a 45 billion yuan increase. Household loans fell 460.3 billion yuan and corporate loans fell 130 billion yuan. Outstanding loan growth reached a record-low 5.1%, even as real borrowing costs have declined.

    China faces a credit-demand shortage, not a liquidity shortage. Lower rates cannot create investment when households are deleveraging. Yet industrial capacity remains formidable — pushing excess productive capacity outward toward exports, price competition and global industrial deflation. signal: Cheaper money ≠ stronger credit demand ≠ stronger consumption.

  3. India's Larsen & Toubro has secured an AI-factory contract worth as much as $1.57 billion from Together AI. The Chennai installation will host 10,000 Nvidia B300 chips, India's largest deployment of its kind. Meanwhile Reuters' analysis of 6,371 institutional 13F filers shows 44% trimmed Magnificent Seven holdings while 42% increased them; semiconductors retained a clearer positive bias, with 48% net buyers versus 34.5% net sellers.

    The signal is not that the AI trade is ending. It is that ownership is migrating away from generic AI exposure toward industrial scarcity — accelerated computing, memory, semiconductor equipment, networking, electricity, data centers and infrastructure finance. signal: This is The Great Value Migration — from the AI narrative to the bottlenecks required to industrialize it.

  4. Euro-area GDP expanded 0.4% quarter on quarter in Q2 while employment increased only 0.1%. Year on year, GDP grew 1.0% while employment grew 0.5%. STOXX 600 Q2 profits are expected to increase 23.4%, with European equities within roughly 1% of record highs.

    The critical metric is becoming less how fast GDP is growing and more how much employment growth each incremental unit of GDP requires. This is economically positive for productivity, but carries profound implications for wages, taxation, consumption and social policy. signal: The real AI debate is not employment destruction, but the economy's capacity for employment growth.

  5. Markets now assign roughly a 67% probability the Fed leaves rates unchanged in September, yet this week's 30-year Treasury auction cleared at 5.216%, its highest yield since 2001. U.S. 30-year real yields are close to 3%, near an 18-year high. Japan is moving in the opposite direction, with markets pricing nearly an 80% probability the BOJ raises its policy rate from 1% as soon as September.

    Governments, hyperscalers, AI developers and energy systems are all competing simultaneously for capital. That can keep real yields structurally elevated even as consumption slows. signal: The price of money is becoming less cyclical and more structural.

02

Hidden Patterns Beyond the Headlines

Four forces beneath the data

01

Demand is becoming the scarce variable

Scarcity began with intelligence, moved to accelerated computing, then to electricity, grids and capital. The latest data suggest a new scarce input: economic absorption. China's credit contraction and America's retail slowdown warn that supply velocity can exceed demand velocity.

02

Capital formation can decouple from employment formation

The euro area illustrates it: 0.4% GDP growth, 0.1% employment growth, 23.4% corporate earnings growth. The conventional chain — investment → production → employment → wages → consumption — becomes less reliable, replaced by investment → accelerated computing → automation → productivity → profits → reinvestment.

03

Policy is confronting the absorption constraint

Britain's EV mandate requires EVs to be 33% of new sales in 2026 and 80% by 2030; the government is now consulting on cutting the 2030 requirement to as little as 50%. Battery EVs were 27.4% of July registrations. Industrial policy cannot force deployment faster than markets can absorb technology.

04

Energy is becoming a tax on demand

The Strait of Hormuz was effectively at a standstill Friday — only two vessels visibly tracked, no crude shipment observed, versus more than 130 daily before the war. U.S. gasoline averages around $4.08 per gallon, 29% above a year ago. Oil now acts simultaneously as an inflation shock, a consumption tax, a margin shock and a monetary-policy constraint.

Key takeaways

The absorption frontier

  • AI investment, accelerated computing, productivity, profits and capital formation are accelerating together.
  • Employment, wages, credit demand and consumption are not accelerating at the same rate.
  • The U.S. consumer is weakening, Chinese households are deleveraging, and European GDP is outrunning employment.
  • The Great Split must now be understood as a propagation mechanism, not simply a market phenomenon.
  • The central challenge is no longer whether AI can produce more — but whether the economy can absorb what it enables.
