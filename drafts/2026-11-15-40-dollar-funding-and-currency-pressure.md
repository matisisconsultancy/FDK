Verified evidence
The Federal Reserve’s November 13 Financial Stability Report provides the clearest evidence yet of the financing transformation underpinning accelerated computing.
Debt issuance associated with advanced computing exceeded $680 billion by October 2026, compared with approximately $130 billion during all of 2023.
Outstanding public debt associated with advanced computing now exceeds $1.1 trillion.
The report also identifies more than $1 trillion of undiscounted leases yet to commence and more than $2 trillion in contractual commitments, guarantees and related obligations among major technology firms.
These amounts must not be mechanically added together: they differ in accounting treatment, maturity and economic exposure and may overlap.

X. THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
THE FINANCIAL PRICE OF THE FUTURE
November 15 reveals an economic transformation that extends far beyond the immediate volatility in technology shares.
For several years, the AI Supercycle has been defined by an extraordinary expansion of technological possibilities.
Computing capabilities have advanced.
The cost of performing many intelligence-related tasks has declined.
New business models have emerged.
And the potential for productivity growth across industries has become increasingly substantial.
Those forces remain powerful.
But the financial architecture required to sustain them is becoming more demanding.
The Federal Reserve’s latest report exposes the extraordinary scale of debt, leases, guarantees and investment commitments supporting accelerated computing.
The rise in US sovereign yields reveals the renewed importance of monetary conditions and the price of long-term financing.
Europe’s banking and fiscal tensions demonstrate how sovereign policy can influence the willingness and capacity of financial institutions to support public borrowing and productive investment.
The Strait of Hormuz illustrates how energy costs can remain vulnerable to physical insecurity even as supply conditions improve.
And the disruption of Microsoft and American air-traffic systems shows that technological progress does not automatically guarantee operational resilience.
These developments share a common characteristic.
They reveal the growing distance between what technology makes possible and what financial and physical systems can sustainably deliver.
The next phase of competitive advantage will increasingly depend on closing that distance.
This requires more than investment ambition.
It requires access to patient capital.
It requires credible institutions.
It requires reliable energy and infrastructure.
And it requires business models capable of generating economic returns sufficient to sustain continued investment.
The consequences for global financial markets could become profound.
Companies with strong balance sheets may retain greater strategic flexibility.
Financial institutions capable of underwriting productive investment accurately may gain influence over capital allocation.
Sovereign economies with credible fiscal frameworks and resilient infrastructure may attract a disproportionate share of productive investment.
And businesses able to turn computing capacity into commercially valuable applications may capture returns without carrying the full financial burden of the infrastructure supporting them.
But the opposite dynamics are equally important.
Highly leveraged infrastructure can become vulnerable to refinancing conditions.
Fiscal uncertainty can increase sovereign borrowing costs.
Political conflict can reduce investor confidence.
And operational fragility can destroy economic value even when the underlying technology remains powerful.
These forces are now converging.

But the opposite cycle is also possible.

The resulting divergence is not simply between technological leaders and followers.

SIGNAL TWO — EUROPE’S SOVEREIGN–BANKING CONFRONTATION IS NOW A CAPITAL-MARKET QUESTION
Verified evidence
One of the most consequential European developments comes from Italy.
Financial Times reporting on November 13 describes an escalating disagreement between the Italian government and the banking sector over proposed taxation and deferred tax credits.
The government proposes increasing the corporate tax rate on banks from 27.5% to 30.5%, with approximately €7 billion in additional revenue expected through 2029.
Intesa Sanpaolo chief executive Carlo Messina has warned that the measures could encourage banks to reduce their Italian sovereign-bond holdings.
He has also raised concerns over proposals to defer the realization of approximately €20 billion in tax credits over ten years without interest.
Messina argues that the arrangement could create complications under banking supervisory and accounting rules, potentially including debt-classification concerns.
These are his warnings and legal-financial interpretations, not confirmed findings of an Italian sovereign default or a supervisory determination of cross-default.
The government argues that banks should contribute more to public finances given their earnings and the pressure experienced by households. Financial Times +1
FDK interpretation
The deeper issue is not the tax rate alone.
It is the relationship between sovereign credibility and the financial institutions financing the sovereign.
Italian banks are important holders of domestic public debt.
That relationship can support the stability of the government-bond market.
But it also creates reciprocal exposures.
The state depends on banks as investors, intermediaries and providers of credit.
Banks depend on sovereign credit quality, fiscal predictability and the treatment of public-sector obligations.
A confrontation over taxation or the timing of public obligations can weaken trust between the two sides.
The relevant transmission is:
Fiscal measures → Bank capital-allocation response → Potential changes in sovereign-bond demand → Borrowing-cost sensitivity → Budget and refinancing pressure.
This pathway is conditional. It depends on actual portfolio decisions, market liquidity, regulation and the response of other investors.

Stronger capital productivity generates financial returns.
Financial returns improve investment capacity.
Investment capacity supports technological and industrial leadership.

Large financial commitments can create liabilities as readily as assets.
The decisive distinction is whether the future economic value generated by investment exceeds the full cost of creating and financing it.
THE FINAL MARKET CALL
The AI Supercycle remains one of the most important sources of opportunity in the global economy.
But the financial markets are increasingly confronting the conditions required to sustain it.
For investors, the priority is to distinguish productive growth from capital accumulation whose expected returns remain uncertain.
For boards, it is to preserve balance-sheet flexibility, operational resilience and the ability to convert investment into cash-generating assets.
For policymakers, it is to reconcile sovereign credibility, industrial competitiveness and long-term capital formation.
And for Europe, the challenge is particularly urgent.
The continent possesses substantial savings, industrial capabilities and technological opportunities.
What it must demonstrate is the institutional and financial capacity to mobilize those resources without allowing sovereign fragmentation and political uncertainty to erode their productive value.
The next market regime will not be decided by the greatest promises of technological acceleration.
It will be decided by the economic returns and financial resilience of the investments that make acceleration possible.
The most consequential capital-allocation decision of the next decade may not be which technology to finance. It may be which investments deserve the scarce privilege of long-term financial confidence.

THE BIG TAKE | THE SUNDAY EDITION
The next great financial confrontation may not take place between competing technology companies, or even between the United States and China.
It may take place in the global capital markets, where governments, hyperscalers, infrastructure operators and industrial companies increasingly compete for long-duration financing at a time when inflation is raising its price.
November 15 reveals five consequential developments.
The Federal Reserve has documented more than $680 billion in advanced-computing-related debt issuance through October, representing approximately 31% of nonfinancial investment-grade corporate debt issuance in 2026.
American ten-year Treasury yields have risen to approximately 5.14%, reinforcing the financial pressure on capital-intensive investments.
Europe has attracted $35.13 billion into equity funds in a single week, yet faces a deepening confrontation over the relationship between banks, sovereign debt and national fiscal policy.
Three tanker incidents involving naval mines were reported by Iranian authorities in the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday, even as the International Energy Agency forecasts an emerging oil surplus.
And a global Microsoft service outage, alongside the breakdown of American air-traffic communications, has exposed the growing economic cost of dependence on concentrated digital and physical infrastructure.
These developments reveal an important transformation.
The financial system is no longer being asked simply to fund technological acceleration. It is being asked to fund that acceleration while also financing sovereign debt, energy security, infrastructure modernization and the resilience of increasingly interconnected economies.
Capital is not a fixed global pool. Savings, credit creation and international flows can expand the supply of financing.
But investors’ balance-sheet capacity, risk limits, preferred maturities and required returns impose constraints at the margin.
When demand for financing increases faster than investors’ willingness to supply it at prevailing yields, the cost of capital can rise.
That is the emerging strategic risk.
The central question is not whether the world can afford artificial intelligence. It is whether the financial system can allocate capital among competing long-term priorities without pricing productive investment out of reach.
This is an increasingly consequential expression of The Great Split. Reuters +3

III. THE HIDDEN PATTERN
THE GREAT SPLIT IS BECOMING A DIVIDE IN ACCESS TO PATIENT CAPITAL
The five developments point toward a governing structural thesis.
The scarcity that increasingly matters is not simply capital itself. It is patient, risk-bearing capital willing to finance productive investment over long and uncertain periods.
The distinction is fundamental.
AI infrastructure requires substantial upfront expenditure.
Electricity infrastructure can require years of development.
Transportation modernization involves long procurement, construction and operating cycles.
Sovereign governments must continuously refinance existing debt while meeting new fiscal obligations.
These activities draw upon different financing instruments, investors and time horizons.
But they all depend, ultimately, on confidence in future repayment or economic returns.

THE FDK STRUCTURAL THESIS
The Great Split is evolving into a divergence between companies and economies capable of attracting patient capital at sustainable costs and those whose future productivity is increasingly constrained by the financing conditions of the present.
The process can become self-reinforcing.
Stronger balance sheets support more predictable financing.
Better financing allows productive investments to proceed.
