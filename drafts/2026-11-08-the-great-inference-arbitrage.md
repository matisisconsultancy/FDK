American computing power is enabling Chinese AI models to compete inside Western enterprises. The next phase of the AI Supercycle may redistribute economic value faster than technological leadership changes.

MATERIAL INFLECTION ALERT — HIGH SIGNIFICANCE

THE BIG TAKE

A consequential development strengthens our U.S.–China AI thesis: Chinese models are demonstrating their economic competitiveness inside Western enterprises, even when deployed on American computing infrastructure.

Evidence disclosed by British fintech Revolut at an October technology conference, and reported on November 3, provides an unusually concrete example.

Revolut has made self-hosted Chinese GLM models its default AI coding solution for approximately 2,000 engineers, using 56 Nvidia B200 GPUs.

The company reports that its AI coding inference bill fell approximately 90% relative to its previous reliance on commercial services from Anthropic and OpenAI.

Its reported self-hosted inference cost is approximately $0.30 per million tokens. The comparison concerns reported service bills, not independently verified, fully loaded ownership costs. 

This is more consequential than another demonstration of inexpensive Chinese AI.

It represents an example of Chinese intelligence being deployed within a major European financial institution.

The strategic distinction is becoming clearer: America can retain leadership in frontier models and advanced computing while Chinese competitors capture an increasing share of economically valuable inference workloads.

1. THE ENTERPRISE INFLECTION — FRONTIER INTELLIGENCE BECOMES THE EXCEPTION

Revolut’s experience reveals three important developments.

First, Chinese open-weight models are becoming commercially useful substitutes for more expensive proprietary models in selected enterprise applications.

Second, falling inference costs can expand AI consumption beyond software generation into debugging, documentation, workflow management and operational support.

Third, companies can increasingly separate their choice of AI model from their choice of computing infrastructure.

Revolut continues to use American frontier models where their capabilities justify the additional expense. Its reported strategy makes open-weight models the default rather than eliminating proprietary systems.

The company also reports significant improvements in engineering productivity, although its published measurements do not isolate the contribution of GLM from other organizational and technological changes.

The emerging competitive model is not necessarily Chinese AI replacing American AI. It is Chinese AI competing for the large volume of tasks where frontier performance commands an insufficient economic premium. 

That distinction has substantial implications for the economics of the AI Supercycle.

2. THE AMERICAN RESPONSE — COMPUTING CONTROL BECOMES MORE SOPHISTICATED

A separate development, reported by Bloomberg on November 6, reinforces the geopolitical dimension.

Nvidia is tightening its monitoring of advanced AI processors sold in Southeast Asia.

Prospective Nvidia Cloud Partners in the region must now enroll in its fleet-intelligence system, which can track the location of participating companies’ accelerators.

Importantly, enrollment does not automatically require customers to disclose location information to Nvidia or American authorities.

The reported initiative follows growing scrutiny of possible semiconductor diversion to China and complements Nvidia’s previously reported restrictions on the proportion of cloud services provided to Chinese customers.

This is a corporate compliance measure, not a newly enacted government export-control regulation. 

The strategic consequence is significant.

America is extending its ability to influence access to advanced computing infrastructure while Chinese AI models are increasingly competing for workloads beyond China’s borders.

These developments are not contradictory.They illustrate how different layers of the AI economy can become more geographically concentrated or more internationally competitive at the same time.

3. THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT — THE REPRICING OF INTELLIGENCE

For The Velocity Edge, the most important insight concerns the changing relationship between technological leadership and financial returns.

If enterprises can accomplish a growing proportion of commercially valuable tasks using substantially less expensive models, three market dynamics may follow.

First, inference pricing becomes more competitive. Proprietary frontier-model providers may face pressure on revenues from standardized workloads, even as demand for advanced reasoning continues expanding.

Second, infrastructure economics become more complex. American accelerators can remain indispensable while the models operating on them become increasingly interchangeable.

Third, economic value migrates toward deployment. Enterprises that successfully combine inexpensive models, optimized infrastructure and proprietary operational data may capture substantial productivity benefits.

The critical measurement is no longer simply price per million tokens.

It is the fully loaded cost per successfully completed economic task, adjusted for accuracy, reliability, security and human supervision.

That is where competitive advantage may increasingly emerge.

THE INVESTMENT VERDICT

The November 8 assessment strengthens our existing thesis without demonstrating a reversal of American frontier leadership.

The new evidence is particularly important because it connects China’s model-efficiency strategy with actual Western enterprise deployment.

For investors, the implication is increasing differentiation between companies that create frontier capabilities, those supplying computing infrastructure and those capturing value through deployment.

For boards of directors, the immediate opportunity is to evaluate whether carefully selected open-weight models can reduce operating costs without sacrificing required performance, security or reliability.

For policymakers, the challenge is more complex: semiconductor controls can preserve strategic advantages, but they cannot necessarily prevent increasingly capable foreign models from competing in international software markets.

Three indicators now deserve priority: independently verified enterprise cost savings from Chinese models; the proportion of workloads migrating away from premium proprietary APIs; and whether tightening computing controls materially slows China’s model development or accelerates domestic alternatives.

No independently verified November 8 DeepSeek frontier release or newly implemented U.S.–China export-control change was identified in this review. The material finding is the newly examined evidence of Chinese model adoption within a Western financial institution, alongside Nvidia’s November 6 computing-monitoring development.

THE FINAL TAKE

The AI Supercycle is entering a phase in which technological leadership and economic-value capture may diverge.

America retains substantial advantages in frontier intelligence and advanced computing infrastructure.

China is increasingly demonstrating that sufficiently capable, inexpensive models can compete for valuable enterprise workloads beyond its domestic market.

Both developments can reinforce each other.

Western companies may use American accelerators to deploy Chinese models, capturing productivity gains while challenging the pricing power of American frontier-model providers.

The next great AI competition may not be decided by who produces the most intelligence, but by who captures the greatest economic value from every unit of intelligence produced.

America leads the frontier. China is challenging its economics. The next market rotation will increasingly reward those who turn intelligence into productivity at the lowest sustainable cost.
