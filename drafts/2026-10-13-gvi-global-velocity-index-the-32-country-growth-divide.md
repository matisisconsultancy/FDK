The AI Supercycle is sustaining global economic growth while concentrating its benefits in an increasingly narrow group of economies. The next Great Split will be between countries that capture AI-driven growth, those that convert intelligence into industrial productivity, and those that absorb higher energy and financing costs without participating meaningfully in either.
The IMF’s new World Economic Outlook provides the strongest institutional confirmation yet: global growth remains resilient, but the number of countries generating most of that growth is shrinking dramatically.

I. The New Evidence — Five Signals
1. Global Growth: AI Becomes a Macroeconomic Stabiliser
The IMF’s October 13 outlook marks an important development.
AI infrastructure investment is helping offset the negative effects of the energy shock. The IMF now forecasts global growth of 3.1% in 2026 and 3.4% in 2027.
But the improvement is uneven.
The United States is expected to grow 2.3% in 2026. The eurozone forecast has been upgraded to 1.1%, while the United Kingdom’s forecast rises to 1.3%.
China’s growth forecast has been lowered to 4.5%, reflecting weak domestic demand and higher energy costs.
South Korea and other Asian semiconductor suppliers are among the beneficiaries of stronger AI-related investment. 
The underlying pattern: AI is no longer simply a technology investment cycle. It has become a significant contributor to aggregate economic demand.
That also creates a new vulnerability.
If AI investment weakens before productivity gains materialise, the consequences could extend throughout the global economy.
2. The Great Concentration: Fewer Countries Generate More Growth
The IMF estimates that just 32 countries now account for 95% of global growth, compared with 54 in its April outlook.
It has downgraded growth expectations for 47% of economies since April.
The strongest positions increasingly belong to economies that combine technological capacity, capital-market depth and participation in AI supply chains. 
This is today’s decisive GVI signal.
Technological acceleration does not automatically produce international economic convergence.
It may initially reinforce existing advantages.
The investment implications are substantial: geographical diversification offers less protection when an increasingly concentrated set of technologies, financing channels and supply chains drives a large share of global growth.
3. Japan: Physical AI Moves Toward Industrial Deployment
Japanese industrial automation company Omron announced on October 13 that it will acquire US industrial-computing company Onlogic and its parent for approximately $675 million.
The objective is to combine industrial computing with precision-control technologies to develop physical-AI applications.
Omron also maintains an industrial automation collaboration with Italy’s Comau. 
The transaction is not yet evidence of realised productivity gains.
Its strategic significance lies in the direction of technological integration:
INDUSTRIAL COMPUTING → AI CONTROL SYSTEMS → AUTOMATION → PHYSICAL PRODUCTIVITY
This is potentially the next major stage of enterprise transformation.Japan’s score remains unchanged, but its industrial-AI momentum deserves closer monitoring.
4. China: AI Financing Expands Despite Capital Constraints
Tencent is preparing a proposed offshore bond offering of at least $4.5 billion.
More importantly, company filings show first-half 2026 capital expenditure of 96.3 billion yuan, compared with 45.9 billion yuan a year earlier.
The proposed bonds include maturities extending as far as 30 years.
The offering remains subject to market conditions; proceeds have not yet been confirmed. 
This illustrates an increasingly important feature of the AI Supercycle.
The ability to secure long-duration financing is becoming a competitive asset.
However, capital expenditure is not productivity.
China must demonstrate that growing investment in infrastructure and computing translates into stronger operating efficiency, industrial output and sustainable returns.
5. Energy and Financial Markets: The Counterforce Intensifies
Brent crude settled at $106.65 on October 12 following renewed attacks in Saudi Arabia.
The IMF’s new outlook warns that continued geopolitical disruption, expensive energy and higher borrowing costs could undermine growth.
Its adverse scenario involving prolonged oil prices near $130 per barrel would further weaken global activity.
Separately, the IMF estimates that a sharp AI-investment correction, amplified by falling asset prices and tighter financial conditions, could reduce global output by 1.1% by 2028 relative to the baseline. 
These are conditional scenarios, not predictions.
Nevertheless, their combined significance is clear:
AI CAPEX ↑ → ECONOMIC DEMAND ↑
ENERGY COSTS ↑ → FINANCING COSTS ↑ → INVESTMENT CAPACITY ↓
The next global growth cycle will be shaped by the interaction of these opposing forces.

IV. Second-Order Effects — The New Global Hierarchy
1. AI Becomes Both Shock Absorber and Shock Amplifier
Today’s most consequential insight is the dual macroeconomic role of AI.
In the expansion phase, AI investment supports growth through infrastructure spending, semiconductor demand, construction and technology employment.
But the same investment cycle creates financial exposure.
If anticipated productivity fails to materialise, a correction could simultaneously weaken corporate investment, equity valuations, household wealth and credit conditions.
The technology supporting economic resilience today could become a source of financial instability tomorrow.
The difference will be determined by realised productivity and capital returns.
2. Physical AI Could Transform the Distribution of Economic Benefits
The next major opportunity lies in moving intelligence beyond data centres.
Omron’s acquisition offers a concrete example of the convergence between industrial computing and automation.
The potential applications extend to manufacturing, logistics, energy management, robotics and mobility.
If productivity gains spread across these industries, economic benefits could become less concentrated in semiconductor suppliers and hyperscale platforms.
The diffusion of AI into physical production may be essential to making the Supercycle economically sustainable.
3. The International Productivity Divide May Deepen
The IMF’s findings imply that participation in global AI supply chains is becoming a significant differentiator of economic growth.
Countries lacking adequate infrastructure, capital or industrial implementation capabilities risk falling behind.
This creates a cumulative mechanism:
AI INVESTMENT → PRODUCTIVITY → CASH FLOW → REINVESTMENT → HIGHER VELOCITY
But the reverse dynamic is equally important.
Weak access to capital and infrastructure can delay technology adoption, suppress productivity improvements and widen the gap with leading economies.
The next phase of globalisation could therefore produce greater technological connectivity alongside greater economic divergence.
4. Europe Faces an Industrial Conversion Test
Europe’s improving growth outlook is encouraging.
But its opportunity remains distinct from that of the United States.
Europe possesses important industrial advantages in machinery, electrical systems, automation, mobility and engineering.
Its competitive challenge is to connect those capabilities with AI infrastructure and digital intelligence.
Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden demonstrate different elements of that potential.
Italy must reverse weaker industrial momentum while leveraging its established manufacturing strengths.Spain must convert its significant digital-infrastructure investment pipeline into operating capacity and productive applications.
Europe’s comparative opportunity is not simply to build more intelligence. It is to industrialise intelligence faster.
5. The AI Investment Hurdle Becomes Macroeconomic
The traditional corporate investment test remains:
ROIC > WACC
But at national level, another question becomes relevant.
Can the productivity benefits of AI exceed the combined economic burden of infrastructure financing, energy requirements, industrial adjustment and workforce disruption?
The IMF’s scenarios demonstrate why this matters.
AI investment can support economic growth during its construction phase while leaving future returns uncertain.
The next competitive hierarchy will depend increasingly on the ability to move from capital formation into sustainable productivity.

Institutional Takeaways
For investors: Distinguish economies and companies benefiting from AI infrastructure expenditure from those generating measurable productivity and free cash flow. The latter may provide the more durable investment opportunity as financing conditions tighten.
For executives: Accelerate physical-AI implementation in existing operating assets. Measure time-to-deployment, incremental operating margins, asset productivity and returns on invested capital.
For policymakers: Treat energy security, capital formation, industrial automation, workforce adaptation and innovation financing as components of one competitiveness architecture.
For central banks: Recognise the distinction between AI-related investment demand, which can be inflationary, and realised productivity improvements, which may eventually ease unit-cost pressures.
For the GVI methodology: The IMF’s concentration findings strengthen the case for developing a complementary measure of global growth concentration. Taiwan, India and other major AI supply-chain economies should also be monitored as external benchmarks pending any formal expansion of the country universe.
