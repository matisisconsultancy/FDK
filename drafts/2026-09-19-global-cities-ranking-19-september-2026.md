THE PRICE OF PERMISSION
AI infrastructure is no longer constrained only by chips and power. Cities are beginning to price the social licence to compute.
Data cut: 19 September 2026
The most consequential urban signal this week is a new one: permission is becoming a factor of production. New York is recommending community-investment requirements of at least $1 million for every megawatt of new data-centre utility demand after the state imposed a moratorium on large new facilities. Virginia—the world’s most concentrated data-centre geography—is tightening permitting, transparency and environmental controls. The implication is bigger than regulation: communities are beginning to demand a direct economic return for surrendering electricity, water, land and infrastructure capacity to AI. 
At the same time, capital continues to concentrate in urban ecosystems capable of absorbing it. Global cross-border commercial-property investment jumped 56% to $71.8 billion in H1 2026; Singapore ranked first globally with $8.7 billion, while London and Milan were among the European cities attracting particularly strong international capital. Meanwhile, Taipei strengthens its position in the physical AI stack as MediaTek launched its first smartphone processor on TSMC’s 2-nanometre process and expands into data-centre accelerators after a $3.9 billion convertible financing backed by Nvidia and Alphabet. 
And AI is crossing another boundary—from digital intelligence into the physical economy. Anthropic has established a wet biology lab in the San Francisco Bay Area to combine AI with robotic experimentation, while Tallinn-headquartered Bolt and Lucid plan to deploy 25,000 Level-4 autonomous vehicles across major European cities, within a broader ambition for a 100,000-vehicle autonomous fleet by 2035. 
The structural GVI anchors remain unchanged: Copenhagen leads the 2026 EIU liveability index; Zurich, Oslo and Geneva lead IMD’s Smart City Index; Silicon Valley, New York and London remain Startup Genome’s first three startup ecosystems; and CBRE places San Francisco, Seattle, Toronto, New York and Austin at the top of its North American technology-talent ranking.

Singapore extends the leadSingapore’s score rises to 86.2, widening the gap at the summit. The new evidence is not another AI announcement; it is capital-market confirmation. JLL data show Singapore attracted $8.7 billion of cross-border commercial-property investment in H1, the highest amount of any market globally. In the GVI framework, that reinforces the interaction between institutional trust, capital formation, infrastructure execution and future productive capacity. 
London strengthens rather than loses ground
London gains 0.3 points. International property investors are returning strongly to premium London assets, while the city’s AI discovery layer continues to generate new companies: London-based Mantic raised $25 million after its forecasting AI outperformed human contestants in a major 2026 forecasting competition. Its investors include Microsoft’s M12 and Balderton Capital. 
The importance is not the size of one seed round. It is the persistence of the London mechanism:
DeepMind alumni → frontier capability → venture formation → institutional demand → global capital.
San Francisco becomes more physical
San Francisco rises to 79.5, not because its housing problem has disappeared—it has not—but because the AI ecosystem is moving into an additional layer of the economy. Anthropic’s new Bay Area wet lab combines AI models, life sciences and physical automation, potentially allowing software intelligence to direct robotic scientific experimentation. That is exactly the transition from AI-native software to Industrial AI embedded in the GVI theory. 
Taipei moves up two positions
Taipei rises to #27. MediaTek’s new 2-nm Dimensity 9600 Pro contains a neural processor claimed to improve prompt processing by 51%, while the company is moving into data-centre accelerators and custom AI silicon. Its recent $3.9 billion convertible financing included $3.5 billion from Nvidia, with Alphabet also participating. 
The GVI interpretation is broader than smartphones:
semiconductors → edge AI → custom accelerators → cloud integration → higher hardware-system value.
Hong Kong gains another two positions
Hong Kong moves to #35 and records the largest Top-50 score improvement alongside Tallinn. A further $800 million Hong Kong secondary listing is planned by RoboTechnik, a manufacturer of equipment used for silicon-photonics devices serving data centres and AI infrastructure. The transaction follows the recent revival in IPO activity and reinforces Hong Kong’s emerging role as a financing bridge between Chinese industrial-AI companies and international capital. 
Milan: capital begins to validate the optionality
Milan gains 0.4 points without yet moving position. International real-estate investors have become particularly active in Milan alongside London as European cross-border capital returned sharply in H1. The signal matters because Milan’s GVI thesis has always depended on converting industrial depth and Northern Italian infrastructure into greater international capital formation.

Tallinn deserves special attention. Bolt’s agreement with Lucid is designed to deploy 25,000 fully autonomous vehicles across major European cities, with Bolt building the fleet infrastructure, operating systems and city partnerships required for commercial deployment. Bolt says it now serves more than 200 million customers across more than 850 cities. Because its headquarters and core corporate platform remain in Tallinn, the GVI awards part—but not all—of this Forward Capacity to the Estonian capital. 
A second city outside today’s Top 50 also deserves watching: Eindhoven. Axelera AI, founded there in 2021, launched a new enterprise inference chip this week, has more than 600 customers and is pursuing a pipeline of up to $1.5 billion in potential sales. Its technology is already supplying EU-backed AI-factory projects. This strengthens the case for a future GVI Industrial Intelligence Cities sub-ranking. 
GREAT CITIES AT RISK
New York — the community-permission test
New York falls 0.2 points despite remaining one of the world’s deepest finance and technology ecosystems. The issue is no longer intelligence demand. It is the price communities are beginning to demand for supporting it. The state’s proposed $1 million-per-MW community contribution comes after its moratorium on large new data centres and amid broader concern over power prices and infrastructure burdens. 
This could ultimately be positive if it creates political durability. But in the near term, it raises the marginal cost and complexity of adding accelerated-computing capacity.
Washington, D.C. / Northern Virginia — abundance meets backlash
Washington loses 0.2 points in Forward Capacity. Virginia, home to the world’s greatest concentration of data centres, unveiled a new accountability framework including tighter permitting, disclosure rules and clean-energy incentives. This is the world’s clearest case of a successful AI-infrastructure cluster confronting its own externalities. 
Austin — infrastructure permission follows infrastructure scarcity
Austin remains #21 but loses 0.1. Texas is still a major AI-infrastructure winner, yet the state has begun tightening disclosure requirements around data-centre water consumption. The constraint is evolving from can we build enough power? toward will communities continue allowing the buildout on existing terms? 
San Francisco — value creation without abundance
The Bay Area remains CBRE’s #1 technology-talent market and Startup Genome’s #1 startup ecosystem, while Anthropic’s move into physical laboratories shows its extraordinary capacity to create new industries. But its labor-and-real-estate cost for a representative technology employer remains the highest in CBRE’s study, at roughly $90.6 million annually for its standardized 500-person company scenario. 
The GVI question remains unresolved:
Can the world’s richest intelligence ecosystem turn intelligence abundance into housing, mobility and everyday abundance?
Amsterdam and Dublin — inherited leadership versus incremental capacityBoth remain exposed to the same fundamental risk identified in previous editions: enterprise demand and talent remain strong, but incremental power, land and permitting capacity have become more difficult to add. Their rankings remain high enough to reflect inherited strength, while Velocity remains constrained.

5 KEY SIGNALS
1. Social licence becomes infrastructure. The New York and Virginia actions show that the AI-infrastructure race is entering a political-economic phase. Power, land and water are not enough; developers increasingly need community legitimacy. 
2. Global capital is returning to metropolitan concentration. Cross-border commercial-property investment rose 56% in H1, with Singapore first globally and London and Milan particularly active European destinations. Capital still values great cities—but rising borrowing costs mean those cities must generate correspondingly higher productivity. 
3. AI is moving from the screen into the laboratory. Anthropic’s Bay Area wet lab is an early but consequential example of agentic intelligence entering physical experimentation. The next urban AI cluster may need laboratories, robotics and life-science infrastructure alongside GPU capacity. 
4. Autonomous mobility is crossing from demonstration to industrial deployment. Bolt and Lucid’s planned 25,000-vehicle European deployment is a material shift from pilot fleets toward scaled Level-4 operations. The city will increasingly become both the market and the operating system for autonomous mobility. 
5. The chip race is spreading from training to ubiquitous inference. MediaTek’s new 2-nm platform, its move into cloud accelerators and Axelera’s enterprise inference push show the next compute wave broadening from giant training clusters toward servers, devices, factories and mobility systems.

FDK VELOCITY INSIGHT
THE NEXT SCARCE RESOURCE IS PERMISSION
The AI Supercycle has moved through a sequence of bottlenecks.
First:
Accelerated Computing.
Then:
Memory.
Then:
Optical Bandwidth.
Then:
Power.
Then:
Infrastructure.
The next one is becoming visible:
Permission.
Not permission from regulators alone.
Permission from the city.
Permission from communities.
Permission to consume electricity.
Permission to consume water.
Permission to occupy land.
Permission to reshape transportation.
Permission to automate parts of the labour market.
Permission, ultimately, to convert urban resources into machine intelligence.
That changes the GVI causality chain.
The previous model was:
AI demand → infrastructure → capital formation → productivity.
The emerging model is:AI demand
→ Power + Land + Water + Fibre
→ Community Consent + Regulation
→ Infrastructure Permission
→ Capital Formation
→ Accelerated Computing
→ Enterprise Adoption
→ Productivity
→ Fiscal Capacity
→ Better Urban Services
→ Political Legitimacy for further investment.
If that final step fails, the cycle breaks.
New York and Virginia show the negative version: the faster data-centre capacity grows, the more aggressively communities demand that part of the resulting value be returned to them.
Singapore illustrates the positive version: global capital continues flowing toward a system perceived as capable of coordinating growth, infrastructure and institutional execution.
San Francisco shows a third version: unmatched intelligence creation now begins spilling into physical science—but the broader urban system still struggles to distribute the gains.
Tallinn shows a fourth: a relatively small city can create a platform whose technological capabilities reshape mobility across hundreds of other cities.
This gives us the next General Law of the Velocity City:
The city that wins the AI Supercycle will not be the city that permits the most infrastructure. It will be the city that makes infrastructure expansion politically, economically and socially sustainable.
The implication is decisive.
Resilience has become a factor of production.
Power has become a factor of production.
And now:
Permission is becoming a factor of production.
That is the new geography of Urban Velocity.
