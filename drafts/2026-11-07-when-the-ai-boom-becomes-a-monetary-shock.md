The IMF, Federal Reserve and Bank of Japan Reveal a New Policy Dilemma: AI Investment May Be Weakening Before Inflation Is Defeated

Where Markets, Capital and Monetary Policy Collide

THE BIG TAKE

The AI Supercycle has entered a consequential new phase. Central banks must now contemplate the possibility that the investment boom supporting economic growth could reverse before its inflationary consequences have subsided.

Three developments on November 6 materially change the discussion.

First, IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva explicitly warned that a slowdown in AI-driven growth would need to enter central banks’ monetary-policy calculations.

Second, San Francisco Federal Reserve President Mary Daly indicated that additional monetary tightening would probably still be necessary, despite the sharp correction in technology equities.

Third, Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda identified a fundamentally different possibility: AI-driven labour substitution could strengthen Japan’s productive capacity because the country faces structural labour shortages.

These developments expose an increasingly important asymmetry.

The same technological transformation may support productivity in labour-constrained economies, generate inflation through capital-intensive investment, and destabilise financial conditions if expected returns disappoint.

For investors, the implications extend beyond technology valuations. The relationship between AI capital expenditure, monetary-policy expectations and financial stability is becoming a critical determinant of global asset allocation. 

1. THE IMF: AI INVESTMENT HAS BECOME A MONETARY-POLICY RISK

The most consequential intervention comes from Washington.

Speaking at IMF headquarters on November 6, Kristalina Georgieva warned that a slowdown in AI-driven growth would complicate central banks’ efforts to bring inflation under control.

Her argument identifies a new source of monetary uncertainty.

AI investment has supported economic expansion in several countries, partially offsetting the negative consequences of the Middle East energy shock.

But the same investment cycle has increased demand for capital, infrastructure and energy while contributing to financial-market vulnerabilities.

The recent technology-market correction introduces the opposite risk: declining investment expectations could weaken economic growth and tighten financial conditions. 

The number investors cannot ignore

The IMF’s October 2026 downside scenario estimated that a severe reversal in AI investment, accompanied by financial spillovers, could leave global GDP 1.1% below its baseline by 2028.

The corresponding shortfall for the United States was estimated at 1.8%.

These are modelled deviations from a baseline forecast, not predictions of an outright GDP contraction.

The distinction is essential.

Nevertheless, the magnitude illustrates how dependent the global growth outlook has become on the sustainability of technological capital formation. 

THE HIDDEN PATTERN

AI INVESTMENT REVERSAL → LOWER CAPITAL EXPENDITURE → WEAKER INDUSTRIAL DEMAND → LOWER GROWTH EXPECTATIONS → TIGHTER FINANCIAL CONDITIONS.

But inflation may remain elevated because energy costs and supply constraints persist.

The resulting combination is particularly difficult for central banks.

A decline in investment could reduce demand-driven inflation over time while simultaneously weakening productive capacity and destabilising asset markets.

The AI Supercycle could therefore generate financial disinflation before delivering Industrial-AI disinflation.

Falling asset prices and declining investment expectations should not be confused with productivity-led reductions in production costs.

That distinction is becoming central to the next monetary-policy debate.

2. THE FEDERAL RESERVE: THE AI CORRECTION MAY NOT BE ENOUGH TO STOP TIGHTENING

Mary Daly’s November 6 remarks introduce an equally consequential development.Despite describing the technology-stock correction as painful but orderly, the San Francisco Fed president indicated that further monetary tightening would likely be required.

She stressed the need to evaluate incoming information carefully and proceed gradually amid financial-market volatility.

Her remarks do not represent an FOMC decision or a commitment to a particular rate increase.

They nevertheless demonstrate that financial-market weakness has not automatically displaced inflation as a policy priority. 

The employment figures reinforce the dilemma

The November 6 US employment report provided additional support for the case that monetary restraint may remain necessary.

According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics:

• October nonfarm payrolls increased by 165,000.
• The unemployment rate stood at 4.1%.
• August and September employment estimates were revised upward by a combined 84,000.
• Manufacturing employment increased by 19,000.
• Average hourly earnings increased 3.1% year over year.

Yet the broader labour picture was less uniform. Employment among workers involuntarily working part-time increased by 339,000, while the number of long-term unemployed remained close to two million.

The figures suggest economic resilience alongside continued employment divergence. They do not, by themselves, establish how much employment growth or weakness is attributable to AI. 

Following the employment report, markets assigned approximately an 80% probability to a December Federal Reserve rate increase, up from roughly 67% the previous day. That was a market-implied probability, not a policy commitment. 

THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT: TWO ADJUSTMENTS COULD REINFORCE EACH OTHER

Consider the interaction between monetary tightening and the technology correction.

Higher interest-rate expectations increase discount rates and borrowing costs.

Lower AI valuations can make equity financing less attractive.

Wider credit spreads could further reduce investment by infrastructure companies dependent on external funding.

These developments could produce a negative feedback loop.

HIGHER DISCOUNT RATES → LOWER AI VALUATIONS → MORE SELECTIVE FINANCING → WEAKER CAPITAL FORMATION → LOWER EXPECTED GROWTH.

But the Federal Reserve cannot automatically reverse tightening if inflation remains persistently above target.

This creates a potentially important break from earlier technology-market corrections.

The next AI downturn, if it deepens, may not immediately produce the monetary easing that investors have historically expected during major valuation corrections.

That possibility deserves particular attention in duration-sensitive equities, infrastructure credit and leveraged technology financing.

3. THE BANK OF JAPAN: THE OTHER SIDE OF THE GREAT SPLIT

Kazuo Ueda’s November 6 intervention provides a contrasting perspective.

Speaking at an IMF event in Washington, the Bank of Japan governor acknowledged that AI advances are generating a positive demand shock for the Japanese economy.

He also suggested that technologies capable of replacing certain labour tasks could benefit Japan because of its persistent labour shortages.

At the same time, Ueda warned about cybersecurity weaknesses, particularly among smaller banks and businesses. 

This distinction is important.

In economies with ample labour supply, automation may initially raise concerns about employment displacement and income distribution.

In economies experiencing structural workforce shortages, automation may instead alleviate production bottlenecks and support output.

The disinflationary consequences depend on how effectively companies convert automation into additional productive capacity.

THE MONETARY CONSEQUENCE

Japan illustrates why the AI Supercycle may not produce a uniform global monetary regime.

The same technological capabilities can have different effects depending on demographics, labour-market structure and industrial organisation.

Where labour shortages constrain supply, automation can strengthen productive potential.Where investment demand already exceeds available resources, AI infrastructure construction can initially reinforce inflation.

The Great Split is therefore becoming a divergence in the economic capacity to convert AI adoption into productivity—not merely a divergence in technological access.

This remains a structural hypothesis rather than evidence that Japanese AI adoption has already produced economy-wide disinflation.

4. THE GREAT SPLIT: THE NEXT MARKET ROTATION

The developments of November 6 suggest a more complex financial environment than the conventional distinction between inflation and recession.

The AI Supercycle creates three interconnected forces.

The first is investment-driven inflation, arising from the construction of computing infrastructure and the associated demand for electricity, equipment and specialised labour.

The second is productivity-driven disinflation, arising from the diffusion of AI across industrial processes, services and organisational systems.

The third is financial repricing, which occurs when expected future returns no longer justify existing valuations or financing commitments.

The critical uncertainty concerns their sequencing.

If investment demand moderates while productivity accelerates, monetary conditions could become more favourable without sacrificing sustainable growth.

If investment and financial valuations contract before productivity gains emerge, economic activity could weaken while inflation remains elevated.

If AI investment remains strong but productivity disappoints, higher financing costs could persist alongside increasingly concentrated capital formation.

The next market rotation will therefore depend on whether AI capital expenditure translates into sustainable returns before financing conditions deteriorate materially.

5. THE EUROPEAN IMPLICATION: A SECOND-ORDER CAPITAL SHOCK

Europe faces an additional vulnerability.

A reassessment of American AI investment could weaken global demand for European industrial equipment, semiconductors and technology-related exports.

At the same time, higher US interest-rate expectations could exert upward pressure on global financing costs.

European companies could therefore experience weaker external demand without obtaining proportionate relief from their cost of capital.

This would reinforce existing differences in investment capacity.

For the ECB, the appropriate response would depend on the balance between imported inflation, domestic demand, financial stability and medium-term inflation expectations.

For European policymakers, the structural priority is clearer: protect the conditions for productive investment without insulating unsuccessful projects from financial discipline.

The EU’s Savings and Investments Union, electricity infrastructure, financing for intangible assets and broader Industrial-AI adoption become particularly important in this environment.

Europe must avoid allowing an international AI valuation correction to become a lasting domestic productivity setback.

No new AI-specific reaction-function change or monetary-policy decision by the ECB, Bank of England, MAS, SNB or BIS was identified in the latest reporting window. Their existing policy frameworks remain the relevant baseline.

FIVE SIGNALS FOR INVESTORS AND BOARDS OF DIRECTORS

1. US interest-rate expectations: Monitor whether the probability of additional Federal Reserve tightening remains elevated despite the technology correction. Strong employment and persistent inflation could keep discount rates under pressure.
2. AI capital-expenditure revisions: Track hyperscaler investment guidance, data-centre project cancellations, equipment orders and infrastructure utilisation. A slowdown in announced spending is not necessarily equivalent to falling realised investment.
3. Credit-market transmission: Watch technology-sector spreads, secured infrastructure lending, refinancing conditions and non-bank leverage. An equity correction becomes more consequential when it impairs financing.
4.Productivity versus employment: Distinguish genuine improvements in output per worker from productivity increases associated primarily with slower hiring. The implications for aggregate demand and corporate earnings are different.
5. European capital formation: Monitor industrial investment, electricity costs and financing conditions for AI-adopting businesses. Europe’s competitiveness will depend on sustained deployment rather than technology spending alone.

THE FINAL TAKE — WHEN MONETARY POLICY CONFRONTS THE AI CYCLE

The latest IMF, Federal Reserve and Bank of Japan interventions reveal a new dimension of the monetary challenge.

AI investment can support growth, increase demand for scarce resources and create expectations of substantial future productivity.

But those expectations can also reverse.

The IMF is warning that such a reversal could become sufficiently consequential to influence monetary-policy decisions.

The Federal Reserve is signalling that inflation control may require further tightening despite falling technology valuations.

Japan is demonstrating why the supply-side consequences of AI will differ significantly across economies.

For investors, the critical question is no longer simply whether artificial intelligence will transform productivity.

It is whether the financial system can sustain the investment necessary to deliver that transformation without generating instability.

For central banks, the challenge is increasingly to distinguish temporary investment fluctuations from structural changes in productive capacity.

For European policymakers, the strategic imperative is to ensure that capital-market volatility does not permanently undermine technological diffusion.

VELOCITY INSIGHT

The next Great Split may emerge when the financial markets stop financing expected productivity before the productive economy has begun delivering it.

The defining investment question is no longer how much intelligence can be built. It is how much economic value can survive the repricing of its capital.
