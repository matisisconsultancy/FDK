RETHINKING EDUCATION IN THE VELOCITY AGE

When technology advances faster than institutions can develop expertise, the capacity to learn, exercise judgment and assume responsibility becomes a source of economic advantage.

THE BIG TAKE

Jason Furman has compressed into a single experiment a challenge that will occupy universities, companies and governments for years. The former chair of Barack Obama’s Council of Economic Advisers used AI-assisted coding to build Econvitals, a free platform for economic data and analysis. He told Bloomberg’s Stephanomics that it could reproduce much of the analytical capacity of the 35-person operation he once led at the White House.

The experiment reveals two very different speeds. An experienced economist can acquire new analytical capacity almost as soon as the tools become available. Developing an economist who knows which questions to ask, which answers to distrust and when to act on incomplete evidence still takes years.

The traditional career absorbed some of that difference. An employer needed junior staff to prepare forecasts, reconcile data and draft briefs. In doing that work, juniors learned how senior people reached decisions and where those decisions could go wrong. Their salaries paid for productive work and helped finance professional training. AI can take over part of the work, leaving employers and universities to find another way to finance the training.

That is the challenge of education in the Velocity Age. The economy can change what it asks of people faster than its institutions can prepare them to do it.

WHAT SHOULD EDUCATION ACCELERATE?

AI can shorten the time between a question and an explanation, between an attempt and feedback, and between an idea and a working model. Universities can use that speed to give students more practice before their first consequential assignment. An economics student can test several explanations for a rise in rents in an afternoon, then spend the time saved examining which evidence actually distinguishes among them.

Some capabilities develop through a different process. An economist who revisits a forecast after a recession, an engineer who investigates a failure and a credit analyst who follows a loan through a downturn learn from consequences that unfold over time. Institutions can start that learning earlier by giving people responsibility, close supervision and a chance to revisit their decisions once the results are known.

This changes the design of a degree. Students should move repeatedly between foundational study, AI-assisted inquiry and supervised work. A credible qualification would show how someone’s reasoning held up as the assignments grew harder and the consequences became more real. The same structure can serve a professional returning mid-career to take on a kind of decision that barely existed at graduation.

WHAT WILL BECOME SCARCE WHEN INSTRUCTION IS ABUNDANT?

As good instruction becomes easier to obtain, the scarce resources will be the attention of an experienced supervisor, access to work with real consequences and an institution willing to stand behind its assessment of a learner. A university with strong ties to hospitals, laboratories, firms and public agencies can put those resources within reach. Its competitive position will depend increasingly on how many students gain that access and how far they progress.

This is where the next educational divide could emerge. Two graduates may have access to the same AI tutor and submit work of similar quality. One has had to defend a recommendation to a client and revise it after seeing the consequences. The other is still trying to secure a first assignment. If access to that experience depends on family connections or the ability to accept unpaid work, cheaper instruction can coexist with a narrower route to professional advancement.

WHO WILL FINANCE THE NEXT GENERATION OF EXPERTISE?A company can reduce its junior intake and recognise the payroll savings within a year. The cost of a thinner talent pipeline may surface years later in higher recruitment costs, weaker succession and growth opportunities the firm lacks the people to pursue. Some of that cost will fall on other employers. Each firm has an incentive to hire experienced talent that someone else trained. If enough firms follow that logic, the industry can spend years rebuilding the expertise it needs to grow.

Companies can reinvest some of the time freed up by AI in supervision and give juniors responsibility earlier. Universities can make paid, supervised work part of a degree and offer routes back into practice throughout a career. Where employers recruit from the same talent pool, professional bodies can help them share the costs of training and recognise the resulting qualifications across firms.

TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS AND POLICYMAKERS

• Investors should test the durability of AI-driven margin gains. Alongside revenue per employee, track junior intake, internal promotion rates, the cost of hiring experienced people and succession depth. The investment question is whether the company can sustain growth with the talent it is developing today.
• Education investors should look for institutions that help graduates advance. Repeat employer participation, paid placements and graduates’ progression into roles with greater responsibility are tests of pricing power as instruction gets cheaper.
• Policymakers should widen access to the first consequential assignment. Co-finance paid, supervised placements where entry routes are narrowing, give learners a record of competence they can take across employers and judge programmes by sustained progression into skilled work.

THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION

Can we build an education system that moves at the speed of technological change while giving people the time and responsibility it takes to develop judgment?

The answer will shape who captures the gains from AI, how readily economies adapt and how many people have a hand in deciding what comes next.
