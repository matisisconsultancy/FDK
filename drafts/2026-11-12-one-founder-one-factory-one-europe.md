Europe can turn its purchasing power into industrial productivity and its industrial productivity into an investment case.
The Big Take
China is asking individuals to build AI companies. Europe should ask its factories, railways, ports and utilities to become their first customers.
The Chinese campaign lowers the cost of starting a company so that more people can discover where AI creates value. Europe can attack the next bottleneck in that process. A maintenance engineer in Turin can now build a tool that helps technicians diagnose equipment failures. The economic breakthrough comes when a factory pays to use it, a second factory in Stuttgart can verify the result without repeating the entire review, and a lender can finance delivery against a signed order.
Europe’s policy objective should be to make industrial demand accessible across borders and financeable on the strength of real contracts. That would give one-person companies a route to scale, give established industries a way to raise productivity, and give capital markets evidence on which to reprice both.
1. The policy choice Europe faces
China’s AI Plus strategy makes the spread of AI through industry and society an explicit policy goal. The one-person company movement adds a decentralized mechanism. Individuals with access to models and agents can test opportunities that a ministry, an incumbent or a frontier lab would struggle to identify in advance. Europe can match that ambition by opening its industrial base to founders who know where factories lose time, where transport networks lose capacity and where utilities lose energy. 
The constraint is visible in the adoption data. In 2025, 20% of EU enterprises with at least 10 employees used AI, up from 13.5% a year earlier. Denmark reached 42%, while Italy was at about 16.5%. Europe can use that variation to its advantage if a solution proven in one market travels into another. Today, a small supplier can spend much of its early capital repeating integration work, negotiating data terms and answering similar security questions for each buyer. 
Europe needs frontier compute and model capability. It also needs a market in which improvements in those capabilities reach ordinary businesses quickly. The practical test of the single market is whether the second industrial deployment costs less and happens faster than the first.
2. Four decisions for European policymakers
Use procurement to create the first customer. The Commission should convene buyers from several member states around a small number of industrial problems, starting with machine maintenance, freight and port operations, and energy management. Member states and industry should commit budgets for competitive, paid trials. Each buyer should specify the operating result it wants, the evidence it will accept and the procurement route to production if the trial succeeds. Public procurement exceeds 14% of EU GDP. Even a focused use of that purchasing power could give small AI suppliers reference customers with results other buyers can verify. 
Make the evidence travel across borders. Buyers in each sector should agree on common performance tests, security documentation and model-switching requirements. A second buyer could reuse the first deployment’s evidence and focus its review on site-specific integration, operating conditions and safety. The Commission’s Apply AI strategy, AI Factories and testing facilities give Europe a base for coordinating this work. A founder should be able to carry an evaluation from Turin to Stuttgart as readily as a set of financial statements. 
Finance the purchase order. A small supplier may have to fund integration, insurance and support well before its first production invoice is paid. Buyers can shorten that gap with milestone payments. Banks and other lenders can finance eligible signed orders or receivables where acceptance criteria, payment terms and buyer credit are clear.The EIB Group and national promotional institutions could test targeted risk-sharing with private lenders. Equity would still fund product development and market expansion. This gives the EU’s Savings and Investments Union a concrete transmission mechanism from European capital to industrial deployment. 
Mobilize people who already know industry. Industrial clusters, technical universities and employer associations should recruit maintenance engineers, production supervisors and logistics planners into short programs built around problems that buyers have agreed to fund. Give them secure access to test data, technical help and a decision date from a customer. Measure the program by paid deployments and repeat sales. The individual’s ability to build a business around specialist knowledge is the opportunity China has identified. Europe’s contribution should be a credible market for that knowledge.
3. How this changes the investment case for Europe
Listed industrials could see the first earnings effect. A manufacturer can capture value through fewer hours of downtime, less scrap, lower energy use or higher output from existing assets. A freight operator can improve asset utilization by resolving disruptions faster. The market will have grounds to revise earnings when companies demonstrate that these gains recur across sites and survive the cost of integration, oversight and support. The companies with the strongest opportunity will be those that can retain part of the savings through differentiated products, service contracts or stronger operating margins.
Industrial software could split into two markets. Suppliers embedded in critical workflows can compound their advantage through customer relationships, operating data and repeatable deployments. Providers of generic features face pressure as models improve and larger platforms bundle comparable functionality. The valuation question is whether a supplier can grow recurring revenue while the work required to install and support each new customer falls. A second cross-border customer can tell investors more about that trajectory than a dozen subsidized pilots.
Credit can enter earlier once contracts become easier to underwrite. A paid trial, a production order and a recurring receivable each carry different risks. Standard acceptance criteria and dependable payment terms make it easier for lenders to distinguish delivery risk from customer credit risk. If this market develops, founders can reserve more equity for growth while lenders earn returns on identifiable cash flows. The test is whether private lenders keep financing orders as public risk-sharing steps back.
Europe’s equity case could broaden through earnings. A credible deployment cycle would give investors a way to gain AI exposure through European industrial companies and specialist software suppliers whose earnings benefit from adoption. That could broaden demand for European equities. The first evidence should appear in company-level margins, cash conversion and returns on invested capital. A wider rerating would depend on those results becoming visible across sectors and countries.
4. The numbers to watch
Indicator What it tells policymakers and markets
Time from paid trial to production contract Whether procurement is turning technical proof into revenue.
Share of suppliers winning a second customer in another member state Whether the single market is reducing the cost of expansion.
Deployment cost and time at the second site Whether founders can build scalable product businesses.
Verified savings after integration and oversight costs Whether industrial users can translate adoption into operating profit and cash flow.
Private financing against signed contracts Whether the market can fund deployment on commercial terms.
The Fundamental Question
Can Europe turn a first purchase order into a continent-wide revenue opportunity?
China’s one-person company campaign expands the pool of people trying to build with AI. Europe can match it by expanding the market those people can reach.Give a founder a buyer in Turin, make the operating evidence count in Stuttgart, and give investors and lenders a contract they can underwrite. Every successful repeat sale strengthens the supplier, improves the economics of an industrial customer and gives capital a reason to fund the next deployment.
Europe gets back into the AI race when a contract signed in Turin starts to change the earnings outlook in Stuttgart.
