What happens when the AI Supercycle continues to accelerate while the cost of capital refuses to cooperate? That is the question underneath the markets tonight.

Friday's rebound should not be confused with a return to equilibrium. The Dow gained 1.0%, while the S&P 500 and Nasdaq rose 0.4% and the Russell 2000 0.9%. Yet the week still ended with the S&P 500 down 1.4%, Nasdaq down 2.1%, Dow down 0.8%, and Russell 2000 down 1.6%.

Europe told a similar story. The STOXX 600 gained 0.59% Friday, but recorded its second consecutive weekly decline, even as euro-area business activity accelerated to its fastest pace of 2026. The hidden message is more consequential than either move: capital is not abandoning risk — it is becoming radically more selective about which risks deserve capital.

::pull THE DIAGNOSIS
Capital is becoming more valuable precisely when demand for capital is exploding.

::section The Great Repricing Is Moving From Equities Into the Capital System | Part I
The most important market this week was not equities. It was bonds.

Long-duration sovereign yields are forcing investors to confront a tension that has been building underneath the AI Supercycle: the world is attempting to finance simultaneously AI factories, electricity generation, grids, data centers, defense, industrial reshoring and enormous fiscal deficits.

The U.S. Treasury's decision to increase long-duration debt buybacks illustrates the pressure. Yet markets quickly challenged the intervention; long-term yields remained elevated as concerns around fiscal sustainability persisted.

This creates the first circular chain of causality: AI investment accelerates → electricity and infrastructure requirements rise → capital expenditure rises → financing requirements increase → long-duration capital becomes scarcer → required returns rise → weaker projects lose access to capital → capital concentrates around higher-productivity assets → AI leaders gain relative advantage → ==the Great Split widens==. This is increasingly the defining financial mechanism of the Next Economy.

::pull THE SHIFT
The competition is moving from *who has the best model?* toward *who can industrialize intelligence fastest and at the lowest marginal cost?*

::section The AI Trade Is Becoming an Industrial Capital Trade | Part II — Capital Allocation
The first phase of the AI Supercycle rewarded scarcity: accelerated computing, advanced semiconductors, memory, networking, AI models. The next phase is broader — and much more capital intensive. It requires semiconductors → AI factories → electricity → grids → cooling → fiber → industrial software → robotics → physical AI.

Another recursive loop is emerging: accelerated computing → cheaper intelligence → more inference → more applications → greater electricity demand → more infrastructure investment → higher productivity potential → greater cash generation → more capital expenditure → more accelerated computing. But the loop does not operate equally everywhere. That is the crucial point.

Companies capable of converting capital expenditure into productivity, cash flow and strategic positioning can justify increasingly large investments. Companies that cannot face the opposite loop: higher capital costs → lower investment → slower productivity growth → weaker competitiveness → lower expected returns → capital outflows → still lower investment. This is the financial architecture of the ==K-shaped economy==.

The S&P 500 remains approximately 12.1% higher YTD, Nasdaq 12.6%, Dow 10.8%, while the Russell 2000 is up about 21.6% despite this week's correction. But beneath those index returns, capital is rotating. Bitcoin surged above $77,000 Friday as investors responded to fiscal concerns, bond-market instability and prospective crypto legislation. Meanwhile, Europe's STOXX 600 closed Friday at 654.18, gaining 0.59%, as stronger manufacturing orders lifted euro-area activity.

::pull PRODUCTIVE VERSUS UNPRODUCTIVE
This is not simply risk-on versus risk-off. It is becoming productive capital versus unproductive capital — and that distinction will increasingly determine valuation.

::section Next Week Could Test the Architecture of the AI Supercycle | Part III — What Comes Next
The coming week brings together two forces markets have so far analyzed separately: the economics of AI and the economics of money. Markets enter through two gates.

NVIDIA's next earnings report will test whether the extraordinary investment cycle around AI infrastructure continues to translate into revenues, margins and forward demand. The question is no longer simply whether NVIDIA grows — it is whether the underlying ecosystem shows demand compounding fast enough to justify extraordinary capital formation. Semiconductors are becoming a leading indicator for data centers → electricity → grids → industrial AI → robotics → productivity.

Jackson Hole will test something different: whether monetary policy can coexist with an investment supercycle that requires trillions of dollars of new capital while sovereign financing requirements are simultaneously expanding. The bond market is asking a much older question — who determines the price of capital? That collision matters far beyond bonds: it determines discount rates, discount rates determine valuations, valuations influence capital formation, and capital formation determines how quickly the AI Supercycle can propagate through the physical economy.

The second great causal chain is becoming visible: fiscal expansion → sovereign issuance → higher term premium → higher long rates → higher cost of capital → tougher capital allocation → concentration into highest-return investments → stronger AI leaders → wider Great Split.
