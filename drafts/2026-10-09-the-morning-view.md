THE GREAT CAPITAL RESET
AI investment is accelerating. The cost of financing it is rising. The next market rotation will separate economic productivity from financial ambition.
IN FOCUS
Three developments define the global financial landscape this morning.
First, the Nasdaq lost 1.25% on Thursday, while semiconductor stocks declined 3.4%. The S&P 500 fell 0.47%, despite expectations of strong corporate earnings.
Second, Brent crude settled at $104.28 before retreating toward $103.28 in early Asian trading. U.S. Treasury yields remain exceptionally elevated, with the 10-year ending Thursday around 5.23%.
Third, SoftBank is reportedly seeking as much as $100 billion from Gulf investors for a new AI investment initiative, even as questions intensify over the relationship between AI revenues, infrastructure commitments and financing requirements. The fundraising report remains unconfirmed. 
The evidence points toward a consequential change in the allocation of global capital.
Technology continues to advance.
But financial markets are becoming more demanding about the returns that technological investment must generate.
The result is a new phase of The Great Split: between companies capable of financing growth through expanding cash flows and those dependent on increasingly expensive external capital.
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
Is the AI supercycle creating a new era of productivity—or a new hierarchy of financial dependence?
The distinction will determine which companies attract capital, which sovereign investors gain strategic influence, and which countries can finance their next industrial transformation.
The next economy will be defined not by the amount of capital invested, but by the economic returns generated on that capital.

II. FIVE SIGNALS SHAPING THE NEXT ECONOMY
1. AI — THE BATTLE MOVES FROM COMPUTING POWER TO FINANCING POWER
Verified evidence
SoftBank has completed a $30 billion investment in OpenAI and raised $11.1 billion in high-yield debt during September.
The Financial Times now reports that Masayoshi Son is exploring a further $100 billion fundraising from Gulf investors. Reuters has not independently verified the proposed initiative.
Separately, Broadcom is reportedly arranging approximately $50 billion in financing connected to AI infrastructure for OpenAI. 
FDK interpretation
AI is entering a capital-intensive consolidation phase.
The strategic advantage increasingly extends beyond algorithms and processors to include access to financing, energy, computing capacity and long-duration investment capital.
This creates a second-order consequence.
Sovereign capital is becoming an increasingly important participant in the ownership and financing of AI infrastructure.The Gulf’s strategic role may therefore expand from funding technology companies to influencing the industrial structure of the AI economy.
Capital implication: The financial advantage shifts toward companies combining technological capability with credible financing structures, contracted demand and measurable cash generation.


⸻


2. SEMICONDUCTORS — RECORD PROFITS NO LONGER GUARANTEE HIGHER VALUATIONS
Verified evidence
Samsung forecasts quarterly operating profit of 107.4 trillion won, approximately $80.17 billion, up 783% year on year.
Nevertheless, semiconductor equities declined Thursday.
Reuters also reported that OpenAI’s September annualized revenue was nearly $50 billion, rather than the approximately $70 billion previously signaled. Importantly, the difference primarily reflects how revenue is defined, including the treatment of cloud-partner sales, rather than a verified $20 billion collapse in actual revenue. 
FDK interpretation
The market is moving from rewarding scarcity to examining economic conversion.
Three distinctions are becoming decisive:
Semiconductor earnings are not equivalent to AI application profitability.
Computing demand is not equivalent to sustainable customer revenue.
Revenue growth is not equivalent to attractive returns on invested capital.
The next competitive divide may therefore emerge between semiconductor suppliers capturing present scarcity premiums and infrastructure operators carrying long-duration capital commitments.
Capital implication: Expect greater differentiation between profitable semiconductor franchises, specialized infrastructure providers and highly leveraged AI deployment platforms.

3. ENERGY AND INTEREST RATES — THE COST OF CAPITAL BECOMES THE CONSTRAINT
Verified evidence
Brent rose 4.1% Thursday as disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz coincided with hurricane-related production shutdowns in the Gulf of Mexico.
Approximately 1.3 million barrels per day of U.S. Gulf offshore oil production had been shut in.
Oil retreated early Friday following indications of diplomatic progress between Washington and Tehran.
Meanwhile, the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield declined from approximately 5.35% earlier Thursday to 5.23%, while the 30-year yield remained around 5.60%. 
FDK interpretation
The energy shock and the AI investment boom are converging through one variable: financing costs.
Higher oil prices can sustain inflation.
Persistent inflation can constrain monetary easing or prompt further tightening.
Higher discount rates reduce the value of distant cash flows.
The consequence is a widening financial divide between capital-intensive projects with contracted revenues and those dependent on optimistic future utilization assumptions.
Capital implication: Energy security, transmission networks, storage and energy-efficient industrial technologies gain strategic value. Highly leveraged mobility, transport and infrastructure operators face greater refinancing and margin pressure.

4. EUROPEAN BANKING — THE ITALIAN BATTLE BECOMES A CAPITAL-FORMATION EVENT
Verified evidence
The Italian consolidation contest changed materially on October 8.
Caltagirone’s holding company announced opposition to MPS’s proposed acquisitions of Banco BPM and Banca Generali and to its merger proposal involving Mediobanca.
Crédit Agricole, holding 29.3% of Banco BPM, separately rejected the attractiveness of the MPS offer.
Delfin, with approximately 17.6% of MPS, has already backed Intesa’s improved offer.
The October 29 shareholder vote remains the next decisive governance test. 
FDK interpretation
The probability distribution is shifting toward Intesa, although the outcome is not yet settled.
The second-order effects extend beyond the ownership of MPS.
They concern the configuration of Europe’s savings, banking and insurance platforms.
Intesa represents the potential consolidation of domestic financial depth.
UniCredit represents the development of continental banking reach.
Banco BPM remains a pivotal asset in the reorganization of Italian banking.Generali matters because long-duration insurance and savings capital could become increasingly important in financing Europe’s next industrial investment cycle.
Capital implication: Focus on transaction completion probabilities, regulatory execution, capital efficiency and the potential revaluation of remaining independent banking platforms.
Europe’s strategic opportunity is to connect its savings more effectively to productive continental investment—not merely to create larger banks.

5. EUROPE–CHINA — INDUSTRIAL COMPETITION MOVES INTO CAPITAL ALLOCATION
Verified evidence
European Trade Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič is engaging Beijing amid mounting tensions over market access, industrial overcapacity and critical supply chains.
The EU’s trade deficit with China reached approximately €360.6 billion in 2025.
China reiterated its preference for resolving disputes through dialogue and cooperation on October 8. 
FDK interpretation
Europe faces a strategic choice.
It can respond to Chinese industrial scale principally through restrictions.
Or it can combine proportionate trade measures with accelerated investment in industrial competitiveness.
The latter would require faster permitting, deeper capital markets, competitive electricity costs and coordinated investment in semiconductors, batteries, rail, intelligent mobility and industrial AI.
This is where Europe’s financial architecture becomes central to its industrial future.
Capital implication: European grid technology, automation, industrial software, advanced manufacturing and selected mobility-infrastructure companies could benefit from a more investment-led industrial strategy.

III. THE HIDDEN PATTERN — THE GREAT SPLIT ENTERS ITS THIRD PHASE
The competitive structure of the AI supercycle is evolving.
Phase I — Technological Acceleration
Capital rewarded access to frontier technology, computing capacity and semiconductor scarcity.
Phase II — Infrastructure Expansion
Investment shifted toward data centres, energy networks, advanced memory and large-scale deployment.
Phase III — Financial Selection
The emerging phase rewards the ability to convert technological investment into sustainable economic returns.
This is the most consequential transition.
The companies announcing the largest investments will not necessarily capture the highest financial returns.
The countries building the most infrastructure will not necessarily achieve the fastest productivity growth.
And the investors providing the most financing will not necessarily receive the strongest risk-adjusted returns.
The decisive advantage is increasingly the ability to convert infrastructure into productivity faster than financing costs accumulate.
Three second-order effects follow.
First, capital becomes more selective within AI rather than necessarily abandoning the sector.
Second, energy efficiency and infrastructure utilization become competitive variables with direct financial consequences.
Third, sovereign wealth funds, banks, insurers and private-capital institutions gain influence over the geography and ownership of technological development.
That is the deeper evolution of The Great Split.

5. European industrial competitiveness. Follow EU–China negotiations and any signals affecting critical materials, batteries, automotive supply chains and industrial technology.
The central question before the close is whether markets begin distinguishing profitable technological expansion from financing-dependent growth more sharply.

VII. WHAT’S NEXT — THE FINANCIAL ENDGAME
Three evolutionary dynamics could reshape capital allocation through the remainder of 2026.
First, the AI investment cycle may become more concentrated.
Companies with access to low-cost financing, proprietary technology and recurring revenues could consolidate competitive advantages.
Second, sovereign capital may become a more influential source of technological ownership.
The reported SoftBank initiative illustrates how Gulf capital could acquire a larger role in AI-related acquisitions, industrial deployment and infrastructure ownership. Its actual scale and structure remain uncertain.
Third, Europe may begin connecting financial consolidation with industrial transformation.
Banking scale, insurance savings, capital-market integration and investable infrastructure projects could become mutually reinforcing.
But that outcome is not automatic.
It requires transparent investment economics, regulatory coordination and projects capable of delivering returns above their financing costs.
The next stage of competition is therefore not simply technological. It is financial, industrial and geopolitical simultaneously.

VIII. FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS
For investors — Prioritize economic conversion. Strong revenue growth is insufficient when valuations require near-perfect execution. Measure free cash flow, capital intensity, refinancing exposure and returns on invested capital.
For corporate boards — Reprice the investment hurdle. Every large AI, infrastructure and automation commitment should be reassessed against financing costs, contracted demand, utilization and energy exposure.
For banks and institutional investors — Prepare for capital concentration. The ability to finance long-duration productive assets may become a more important competitive differentiator than balance-sheet size alone.
For European policymakers — Turn savings into industrial capacity.Europe must move beyond fragmented national financing mechanisms toward investable projects, integrated capital markets and strategic infrastructure platforms.
For global strategists — Follow the second-order effects. The critical competitive shift is from ownership of technology toward control of the financing, energy and industrial systems required to deploy it profitably.

THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
The AI supercycle is not necessarily slowing.
Its financial architecture is changing.
The first phase rewarded innovation.
The second rewarded infrastructure expansion.
The third will reward economic productivity.
That transition will redistribute capital, alter corporate valuations and reshape the competitive balance between regions.
For the United States, the challenge is converting extraordinary AI investment into sustainable returns.
For China, it is translating industrial and technological scale into durable global competitiveness.
For Europe, it is mobilizing savings, financial institutions and industrial capabilities into a new cycle of productive investment.
The Great Split is moving from technological capability to financial execution.
And that leads to the defining investment principle of the next economy:
THE FUTURE WILL NOT BELONG TO THOSE WHO MOBILIZE THE MOST CAPITAL.
IT WILL BELONG TO THOSE WHO MAKE CAPITAL THE MOST PRODUCTIVE.
The next great market rotation will reward the conversion of technological acceleration into economic returns.
