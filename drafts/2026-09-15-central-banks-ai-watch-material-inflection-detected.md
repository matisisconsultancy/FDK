EUROPE IS BEGINNING TO PAY FOR THE AI SUPERCYCLE BEFORE IT FULLY PARTICIPATES IN IT

The most consequential new development is Christine Lagarde’s 14 September 2026 speech in Vienna, reinforced by a new BIS assessment released the same day. Together they push the central-bank debate one step further.

The issue is no longer simply whether AI will eventually raise productivity and lower unit costs.

The new question is:

WHO FINANCES THE AI SUPERCYCLE — AND WHO CAPTURES THE PRODUCTIVITY?

That distinction goes directly to the heart of the Great Split.

Lagarde disclosed that euro-area firms are now expected to devote roughly 10% of total investment to AI in 2026, while AI-related borrowing accounted for around one-quarter of the growth in euro-area corporate credit in Q1. AI use by euro-area workers has already risen above 50%, yet U.S. digital investment has been growing roughly twice as fast, and American workers spend two to three times as much of their working week using AI as workers in the largest euro-area economies. 

This means AI has moved beyond the productivity debate.

It is already beginning to influence credit creation, capital allocation and monetary transmission.

THE NEW GREAT SPLIT: COST OF CAPITAL VS PRODUCTIVITY CAPTURE

Lagarde’s most important macro-financial point is even sharper.

U.S. hyperscalers issued more than $100 billion of bonds last year, mostly long-dated, and now represent close to 10% of new euro-denominated corporate bond issuance by non-financial companies. Market participants already attribute part of the rise in U.S. long-term real yields to AI-related borrowing. Because euro-area long-term rates are correlated with U.S. yields, Europe is importing part of the financing cost of the American AI buildout. 

That produces a striking chain of causality:

U.S. AI Capital Formation
→ Hyperscaler Bond Issuance
→ Greater Global Demand for Capital
→ Higher Long-Term Real Yields
→ Higher European Borrowing Costs

while, simultaneously:

U.S. AI Investment
→ Faster Adoption
→ Higher Productivity
→ Stronger Cash Flows
→ Greater U.S. Economic Advantage

Europe risks participating in the first chain without capturing enough of the second.

That is a new formulation of the Great Split.

EUROPE MAY IMPORT THE COST OF AI BEFORE IT IMPORTS THE PRODUCTIVITY.

This is potentially one of the most consequential monetary-policy implications of the AI Supercycle.

⸻

AI IS NOW BECOMING A CREDIT-CYCLE VARIABLE

The fact that AI-related borrowing generated roughly a quarter of euro-area corporate credit growth in Q1 is especially important.

It means aggregate credit data may increasingly conceal a widening internal divergence:

AI-linked sectors
→ rising investment
→ rising credit demand
→ productivity expectations
→ capital formation

versus:

Traditional sectors
→ higher borrowing costs
→ weaker investment economics
→ slower capital formation
→ employment pressure

That is the financial version of the Great Split.

A central bank can therefore face the paradox of maintaining restrictive financial conditions while a specific technology-driven investment boom continues accelerating.

The implication is profound:

The same interest rate can be restrictive for the Old Economy and insufficiently restrictive for the AI Economy.

This makes the interpretation of monetary transmission materially harder.

⸻

THE ECB HAS NOW LINKED AI DIRECTLY TO EUROPE’S FISCAL ARITHMETIC

Lagarde also quantified the upside.

ECB estimates suggest that rapid AI adoption could raise the level of European productivity by as much as 4% over a decade. With Europe’s workforce projected to shrink by more than one million people annually over the next 25 years, she framed productivity growth not as an optional benefit but as necessary to sustain Europe’s economic and social model. 

This materially strengthens the case we have been developing:

Industrial AI deflation is not simply about lower prices.

It is about raising output per worker fast enough to offset:• demographic decline;
• higher defence spending;
• energy investment;
• digital infrastructure;
• higher debt-service costs;
• and Europe’s widening strategic-investment requirement.

This means the ECB increasingly confronts two different forms of inflationary pressure.

Nominal inflation: energy, wages, geopolitical shocks.

Structural scarcity inflation: capital, electricity, data centres, GPUs and infrastructure required by the AI buildout.

Against both sits the potential deflationary force of:

AI → Productivity → Lower unit costs.

The sequencing will matter enormously.

⸻

THE BIS HAS MOVED FROM OBSERVING THE AI BOOM TO WARNING ABOUT ITS FINANCIAL ARCHITECTURE

The second development is the BIS.

Its new 14 September assessment says the AI-driven global market rally is beginning to show “growing signs of vulnerability,” with particular concern around leverage, opaque financing and the durability of expected AI profitability. Aggregate tech-company borrowing in private credit has risen from around $22 billion in 2010 to more than $1 trillion by 2025, according to the BIS analysis cited by Reuters. 

This builds directly on Pablo Hernández de Cos’s warning that the five largest global technology companies are expected to invest more than $1 trillion in AI across 2025–26, while industry estimates imply global AI investment could eventually reach as much as $4 trillion by 2030. The BIS is specifically concerned that capital expenditure is beginning to outrun cash generation, forcing greater reliance on debt, private credit and financing structures that can be opaque and interconnected. 

This changes the financial-stability equation.

The first phase of the AI Supercycle was dominated by:

Equity appreciation → Cash generation → Capex

The next phase increasingly becomes:

Capex ↑
→ Free cash flow insufficient
→ Debt/private credit ↑
→ Leverage ↑
→ Interconnection ↑
→ Financial-stability sensitivity ↑

This is precisely where central banks become materially more involved.

⸻

THE BIS WARNING IS MORE SERIOUS THAN “AI BUBBLE” TALK

The key concern is not simply valuation.

It is transmission.

The BIS identifies three channels through which an AI correction could become macroeconomic rather than merely technological:

Household wealth. Equity ownership is now sufficiently broad that a sharp correction could affect consumption.

Global concentration. U.S. technology equities have become unusually important in global portfolios.

Credit interconnection. Increasing debt and private-credit financing creates links among hyperscalers, chipmakers, AI companies, lenders and institutional investors that may be difficult to observe ex ante. 

The central-bank risk is therefore becoming:

AI CAPEX BOOM → CREDIT BOOM → ASSET CONCENTRATION → FINANCIAL-STABILITY LOOP

This is a materially different problem from whether ChatGPT raises office productivity.

⸻

EUROPE’S NEW AI TRILEMMA

Lagarde’s speech effectively identifies a European AI trilemma:

ADOPTION

Europe must adopt AI quickly enough to capture productivity.

SOVEREIGNTY

Europe cannot become completely dependent on external models and infrastructure.

CAPITAL

Europe must finance enough domestic AI infrastructure to avoid exporting both its savings and its future growth.

Euro-area households already hold roughly €440 billion in U.S. technology companies. Meanwhile, Europe hosts only around 5% of global AI computing capacity, versus roughly three-quarters in the United States. Europe’s shortage of data-centre capacity is projected to expand more than sixfold within a decade if present trends continue. 

The capital loop today is therefore:

European Savings
→ U.S. Technology Assets
→ U.S. AI Capex
→ U.S. Productivity
→ Higher U.S. Asset Values

while Europe receives:

Higher global yields + imported AI services + dependency.

Lagarde’s answer is explicitly the Savings and Investments Union.Her argument is unusually direct: AI may be the transformative project capable of forcing Europe finally to build capital markets deep enough to finance investment beyond the capacity of bank balance sheets alone. 

That is important.

The Savings and Investments Union is no longer merely a financial-market reform.

It is becoming part of Europe’s AI industrial policy.

⸻

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR NEUTRAL RATES

The emerging evidence reinforces the possibility of a temporarily higher equilibrium interest rate during the AI buildout.

The mechanism is straightforward:

AI optimism
→ Investment demand ↑
→ Credit demand ↑
→ Bond issuance ↑
→ Competition for savings ↑
→ Long real yields ↑
→ R* potentially ↑

But once productivity diffusion becomes dominant:

AI adoption
→ Productivity ↑
→ Unit costs ↓
→ Potential output ↑
→ Inflationary pressure ↓

Central banks are therefore trying to identify a moving target.

The equilibrium rate during the construction of the AI economy may be different from the equilibrium rate after the diffusion of AI productivity.

That is another reason why policy errors become easier.

⸻

INVESTOR IMPLICATION: THE GREAT SPLIT IS MOVING INTO CREDIT

The most important investor consequence is that the Great Split is no longer purely an equity-market phenomenon.

The next differentiation should increasingly occur between:

AI CASH-FLOW GENERATORS

and

AI CAPITAL CONSUMERS.

The first group can self-finance.

The second becomes progressively more exposed to:

• long-duration yields;
• refinancing risk;
• private-credit pricing;
• power costs;
• data-centre utilization;
• and the speed at which AI revenues materialize.

The AI Supercycle therefore moves from:

Technology selection

toward:

CAPITAL-EFFICIENCY SELECTION.

That should increasingly matter for equities, investment-grade credit, private credit, infrastructure and eventually bank exposures.

⸻

THE EU POLICY IMPLICATION

For Europe, the policy hierarchy is becoming clearer.

The objective cannot simply be:

“Spend more on AI.”

It must be:

European Savings
→ European Capital Markets
→ European AI Infrastructure
→ European Models and Industrial AI
→ Productivity
→ Cash Flow
→ Reinvestment

Europe needs to close that circuit.

Otherwise the adverse loop is:

European savings finance American AI
→ American AI raises global capital demand
→ European borrowing costs rise
→ European investment slows
→ productivity divergence widens.

That may be the most consequential second-order effect of the AI Supercycle for Europe.

⸻

THE VELOCITY EDGE — CENTRAL BANK INSIGHT

The previous phase of the central-bank debate was:

Will AI be inflationary or deflationary?

The question is becoming much bigger:

WHO CAPTURES THE PRODUCTIVITY — AND WHO CARRIES THE COST OF CAPITAL?

The ECB is now explicitly linking AI to credit growth, long-term yields, productivity, strategic autonomy and capital-market architecture. The BIS is simultaneously moving AI into the domain of leverage, private credit and systemic financial stability. 

That is a genuine change in the debate.

And it suggests a new chain of causality:

AI SUPER-CYCLE → CAPITAL FORMATION → CREDIT → YIELDS → FINANCIAL STABILITY → PRODUCTIVITY → MONETARY POLICY

The AI Supercycle is no longer merely entering the central-bank reaction function.

It is beginning to reshape the financial system through which monetary policy operates.
