Central Banks Are Beginning to Measure the Distance Between AI Investment, Economic Growth and Realised Productivity

Why the Bank of England’s New Evidence Could Change How Investors Value the AI Supercycle

THE BIG TAKE

The most consequential monetary development is a new empirical distinction: AI is already contributing meaningfully to economic growth, but its measurable productivity dividend remains considerably smaller than businesses anticipate.

The Bank of England’s November 5 Monetary Policy Report provides unusually important evidence.

Its new analytical framework suggests that emerging AI-related activity has accounted for just under one-fifth of British GDP growth since the beginning of 2026.

Yet business surveys indicate that AI has contributed only 0.1–0.2 percentage points to annual productivity growth since early 2024.

Companies expect a substantially larger contribution—approximately 0.9 percentage points annually over the next three years.

These estimates measure different periods and concepts, but their contrast is revealing.

The investment cycle is accelerating. The productivity transformation remains incomplete.

Meanwhile, on November 6, New York Federal Reserve President John Williams acknowledged that extraordinary AI investment is increasing prices for certain technology products and services, reinforcing the inflationary pressures already created by energy markets.

The Bank of England has responded to inflation by raising its policy rate to 4%.

Together, these developments bring the Great Split into sharper focus.

Capital is being committed on the expectation of future productivity, while interest rates are being determined by inflationary pressures that are already materialising. 

1. THE BANK OF ENGLAND: THE PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND BECOMES MEASURABLE

The November 5 Monetary Policy Report introduces two particularly consequential analyses.

The first examines AI’s contribution to economic growth.

Bank staff have developed an AI-activity proxy incorporating computer programming, information services, software development and related activities.

After adjusting for longer-term technology-sector expansion, the Bank estimates that new AI-related activity accounts for just under 20% of GDP growth since the start of 2026.

This is a proxy-based estimate, not a direct measurement of AI-generated value added.

Nevertheless, it demonstrates that AI-related activity is becoming macroeconomically significant.

The second analysis examines productivity.

The Bank finds that much of Britain’s recent productivity improvement reflects cyclical developments, including slower hiring and the reversal of earlier labour hoarding.

Evidence of AI-driven productivity improvements exists, but remains modest.

Most importantly, the Bank distinguishes realised gains from expectations.

Businesses estimate that AI has increased productivity by approximately 0.5% cumulatively over three years.

They anticipate much faster improvements as adoption expands.

This distinction matters because financial markets discount expected future productivity, while monetary policy must respond to current economic conditions. 

THE HIDDEN PATTERN — PRODUCTIVITY EXPECTATIONS ARE RUNNING AHEAD OF PRODUCTIVITY REALISATION

The monetary transmission mechanism is becoming clearer.

AI INVESTMENT → HIGHER CURRENT DEMAND → STRONGER GDP → HIGHER RESOURCE REQUIREMENTS.

But the productivity sequence is slower.

AI ADOPTION → ORGANISATIONAL TRANSFORMATION → HIGHER OUTPUT PER WORKER → LOWER UNIT COSTS → POTENTIAL DISINFLATION.

The difference between these two sequences is fundamental.

It determines whether the AI Supercycle supports sustainable economic expansion or creates an investment-driven inflation cycle followed by financial repricing.

It also identifies a critical valuation risk.Companies whose valuations assume rapid productivity gains may become increasingly vulnerable if those gains take longer to materialise while financing costs remain elevated.

2. THE FEDERAL RESERVE: AI-RELATED INFLATION IS NO LONGER HYPOTHETICAL

Speaking at an ECB conference in Frankfurt on November 6, New York Fed President John Williams reinforced the importance of inflation control.

Williams acknowledged that heavy AI investment is contributing to higher costs for certain technology-related products and services.

He nevertheless emphasised that inflation expectations remain anchored and that wage growth appears broadly consistent with the Federal Reserve’s inflation objective.

He did not endorse a specific next interest-rate decision.

The significance is therefore analytical rather than an announced change in the Fed’s reaction function.

The Federal Reserve is increasingly confronting a technology investment cycle that can raise prices before generating sufficient productive capacity to reduce them.

Market expectations reported alongside his remarks placed the probability of another rate increase in December above 50%. This represents market pricing, not a Federal Reserve commitment. 

THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT — STRONGER GROWTH DOES NOT NECESSARILY MEAN LOWER INTEREST RATES

Higher AI investment can increase the demand for capital and scarce industrial resources.

Over time, successful productivity improvements could increase expected investment returns and potentially raise equilibrium real interest rates.

But disappointing returns could produce the opposite adjustment through weaker investment and falling asset valuations.

The direction of the neutral rate therefore remains uncertain.

No new formal AI-specific estimate of the neutral rate was established in today’s Federal Reserve developments.

For investors, the critical distinction is between a higher cost of capital justified by stronger future cash flows and a higher cost of capital driven by persistent inflation or risk premiums.

Only the former necessarily supports stronger long-term valuations.

3. THE BANK OF ENGLAND’S RATE INCREASE: THE INFLATIONARY COST ARRIVES FIRST

On November 5, the Bank of England increased Bank Rate by 25 basis points to 4.00%.

The Monetary Policy Committee voted 8–1, with Catherine Mann favouring a larger 50-basis-point increase.

The Bank now expects consumer-price inflation to approach 5% in the second quarter of 2027, principally because of sustained energy-price pressures.

Although evidence of broader wage-price effects remains limited, policymakers judge that the accumulated inflationary pressures justify tighter monetary policy.

Importantly, the Bank’s report explicitly recognises that AI investment has supported economic activity.

Britain is therefore tightening monetary policy during an investment expansion that could eventually improve its productive potential. 

This is the central monetary paradox of the AI Supercycle.

The demand generated by technological investment can strengthen economic growth.

The supply-side improvements needed to accommodate that growth may arrive only gradually.

Meanwhile, energy inflation raises both operating costs and financing requirements.

Central banks cannot simply disregard current inflation because future productivity improvements are expected.

Nor can investors assume that technological progress will automatically produce lower interest rates.

4. THE GREAT SPLIT IS ALSO EMERGING IN EMPLOYMENT

The Bank of England’s new productivity analysis introduces another consequential distinction.

Businesses report relatively little aggregate employment displacement attributable to AI so far.

However, employers increasingly indicate that AI adoption is reducing recruitment and the replacement of departing employees.

Their expectations for future productivity improvements partly reflect anticipated slower employment growth rather than solely faster output expansion.

This matters for monetary policy.An increase in output per worker can result from stronger production, weaker hiring, or a combination of both.

The economic consequences are not equivalent.

Productivity-led growth can support higher real incomes and stronger demand.

Employment-led adjustment can improve measured productivity while weakening household income formation and consumption.

The Great Split may therefore become visible first in recruitment patterns and income distribution rather than aggregate unemployment.

For central banks, this requires a more granular assessment of labour-market conditions.

For investors, it introduces a distinction between companies generating productivity through sustainable revenue expansion and those relying primarily on workforce compression. 

5. FINANCIAL STABILITY: THE BIS IDENTIFIES A NEW CONCENTRATION RISK

A November 3 BIS speech by Alexandre Tombini adds an important financial-stability dimension to the latest monetary evidence.

Tombini warns that excessive AI investment, complex financing structures and circular investment relationships can obscure concentrations of financial risk.

He also identifies operational vulnerabilities associated with increasingly autonomous AI systems.

The significance is the interaction between technological investment and its financing architecture.

AI CAPITAL CONCENTRATION → COMPLEX CREDIT EXPOSURES → POTENTIAL MISPRICING → DISAPPOINTING RETURNS → CREDIT REPRICING.

This is a potential transmission mechanism, not evidence that a systemic crisis is underway.

The BIS intervention reinforces the importance of examining who ultimately bears the financing risk associated with AI infrastructure.

Investors should distinguish projects supported by established operating cash flows from those dependent on optimistic utilisation assumptions, refinancing or interconnected financing arrangements. 

A separate institutional development is equally relevant.

In a November 4 speech, Bank of England Chief Data Officer Ben Martin explained how the institution is deploying AI across its analytical operations.

One application, ARIA, helps analyse intelligence collected by the Bank’s regional agents, while other systems support internal knowledge retrieval.

This provides a concrete example of central-bank AI adoption strengthening the information available for monetary-policy preparation, without replacing expert judgment. 

6. EUROPE’S STRATEGIC QUESTION: WHO CAPTURES THE PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND?

The latest evidence reinforces an important European challenge.

The United States remains the principal centre of frontier AI capital formation.

Britain is beginning to measure the contribution of AI-related activity to economic growth.

Europe has substantial industrial capabilities but continues to face constraints in financing, energy infrastructure and the diffusion of AI across smaller enterprises.

The monetary consequences may be significant.

If global investment demand contributes to higher real interest rates, European businesses could face more expensive capital before achieving equivalent productivity improvements.

This would deepen the Great Split.

The appropriate European response is not preferential monetary accommodation for AI investment.

It is stronger productive-capital formation through integrated financial markets, competitive energy infrastructure and financing mechanisms that support technological adoption.

The ECB must also improve its measurement of the difference between AI-related investment demand and realised productivity gains.

Europe’s challenge is to prevent the global AI investment cycle from increasing its cost of capital faster than it increases its productive capacity.

FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS AND EUROPEAN POLICYMAKERS

1. The productivity gap is now an investable variable. The Bank of England’s latest estimates demonstrate why investors should distinguish AI capital expenditure from measurable improvements in output, operating margins and returns on invested capital.
2. Inflationary investment demand can precede Industrial-AI deflation.Central banks are observing the current effects of AI investment on demand and selected input prices while remaining cautious about the scale of future productivity gains.
3. Employment divergence could become a leading indicator. Slower recruitment alongside rising measured productivity may signal organisational transformation, but it does not necessarily imply stronger aggregate demand or broadly distributed economic gains.
4. Financial concentration deserves closer scrutiny. The BIS warning reinforces the need to monitor AI-related credit structures, refinancing dependence, interconnected exposures and the sustainability of investment returns.
5. Europe must accelerate the conversion of savings into productive investment. Deeper capital markets, financing for intangible assets, workforce adaptation and more competitive electricity infrastructure will determine how much of the AI productivity dividend European companies ultimately capture.

THE FINAL TAKE — THE REPRICING OF PRODUCTIVITY

The November 5–6 developments establish a more measurable foundation for assessing the AI Supercycle.

The Bank of England is identifying meaningful AI-related economic activity but only modest realised productivity improvements.

The Federal Reserve is acknowledging inflationary pressures associated with technological investment.

And the BIS is warning that concentrated, increasingly complex financing structures could amplify financial vulnerabilities.

Together, these developments reveal the next challenge for investors.

The AI Supercycle is not simply a technological revolution.

It is a capital-allocation cycle in which investment expenditures, financing costs and expected productivity gains evolve at different speeds.

The next financial rotation will increasingly distinguish companies that can demonstrate realised productivity from those whose valuations depend on productivity that has yet to materialise.

For central banks, the corresponding challenge is to distinguish current demand pressures from changes in sustainable productive capacity.

For European policymakers, the priority is to close the gap between technological adoption and economic returns.

VELOCITY INSIGHT

The next Great Split will be determined by the distance between the capital invested in intelligence and the productivity ultimately delivered by it.

For investors, the decisive variable is no longer the velocity of AI investment. It is the velocity of its financial returns.
