Why Central Banks Are Confronting an AI Investment Boom Before the Productivity Dividend Arrives

The Great Split is becoming a monetary-policy problem.

THE BIG TAKE

A consequential shift emerged during November 3–6: central banks and international monetary authorities are increasingly treating AI as a source of current demand, inflation and financial risk—not merely future productivity growth.

The Bank of England has supplied unusually revealing empirical evidence. The Federal Reserve is maintaining an inflation-focused stance despite the technology-sector correction. The BIS has intensified its warnings about AI financing structures, while Singapore is institutionalising AI adoption and supervision.

The underlying contradiction is becoming measurable.

AI investment is supporting growth today. Its productivity benefits remain much smaller, less evenly distributed and more difficult to verify.

This creates a policy dilemma. Tightening monetary conditions can suppress the investment required for future productivity, while excessive accommodation can amplify infrastructure overinvestment and financial instability.

1. BANK OF ENGLAND — THE MOST IMPORTANT NEW EVIDENCE

November 5, 2026 | Policy rate increased to 4.00%.

The Bank of England raised Bank Rate by 25 basis points, with eight Monetary Policy Committee members supporting the increase and one advocating 50 basis points.

UK inflation reached 3.5% in September and is projected to approach 5% in mid-2027. Rising energy costs remain the immediate problem, but stronger activity is complicating the policy response. 

More significant for the AI Supercycle is the new productivity analysis accompanying the decision.

The Bank reports that surveyed firms estimate AI raised their productivity by approximately 0.5% cumulatively over the previous three years, equivalent to an annual contribution of approximately 0.1–0.2 percentage points.

Looking forward, however, the same firms expect AI to increase annual productivity growth by approximately 0.9 percentage points over the next three years.

Crucially, they expect roughly half that improvement to come from slower employment growth rather than faster output growth.

These are firms’ expectations, not the Bank’s official central forecast. 

This distinction is fundamental.

The Great Split may emerge not simply between AI leaders and laggards, but between economies experiencing productivity gains through expanding output and those achieving higher measured productivity partly by reducing labour demand.

The monetary implication: productivity growth will not automatically translate into stronger employment, household income or aggregate demand.

For investors, the central question becomes whether AI raises revenues and operating margins sufficiently to justify capital expenditure—or primarily reduces labour costs.

2. FEDERAL RESERVE — THE AI CORRECTION HAS NOT PRODUCED A POLICY PIVOT

November 5–6, 2026 | Inflation concerns remain dominant.

The Federal Reserve maintained its target range at 3.75–4.00% on October 28. Two policymakers preferred an immediate increase.

On November 5, Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack and Richmond Fed President Thomas Barkin favoured gradual additional tightening.

On November 6, San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly indicated that further increases would probably be necessary, notwithstanding the correction in technology equities. 

The November 6 employment report reinforced the Fed’s dilemma: US nonfarm payrolls increased by 165,000 in October, while unemployment declined to 4.1%. These figures were stronger than expected, although weaknesses remained among younger workers and those seeking full-time employment. 

The emerging implication is consequential.

A correction in AI valuations is not sufficient, by itself, to justify monetary easing while inflation remains elevated and employment resilient.The Fed may increasingly need to distinguish between an equity-market correction that removes speculative excess and a contraction in AI investment serious enough to weaken aggregate demand.

3. IMF — AI HAS ENTERED THE MONETARY REACTION FUNCTION

November 6, 2026 | Georgieva highlights AI-growth dependence.

IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva warned that a slowdown in AI-driven economic growth would become an important consideration for central banks assessing how to address persistent inflation.

She noted that AI investment is helping offset the negative growth effects of the Middle East energy shock, while simultaneously increasing inflationary and financial-stability pressures.

The IMF had previously estimated that a severe AI investment reversal could reduce global GDP by 1.1% by 2028, including 1.8% in the United States. These are adverse-scenario estimates, not baseline forecasts. 

This exposes the central policy dilemma:

AI capital formation is simultaneously an inflationary demand impulse and a source of potential future economic resilience.

Reducing that investment could ease current inflation while weakening subsequent growth.

That is why the distinction between inflationary capital formation and productivity-led disinflation is becoming critical.

4. BIS — AI FINANCING BECOMES A FINANCIAL-STABILITY CONCERN

November 3, 2026 | New BIS intervention.

In his speech Navigating Financial Stability in the Age of AI, BIS representative Alexandre Tombini identified three important vulnerabilities: potential overinvestment, increasingly complex debt structures and circular investment relationships.

He also highlighted cybersecurity, operational concentration and governance risks associated with autonomous financial systems. 

The implications extend beyond technology valuations.

AI investment increasingly involves hyperscalers, infrastructure developers, utilities, banks and non-bank financial intermediaries.

Where financing relationships become interconnected, a reduction in expected AI returns could propagate through credit markets.

The financial-stability question is shifting from whether AI is valuable to whether its financing architecture can withstand a reassessment of that value.

5. ECB AND SINGAPORE — TWO DIFFERENT APPROACHES TO AI TRANSFORMATION

ECB: monetary caution, infrastructure development.

The ECB maintained its deposit facility rate at 2.50% on October 29, emphasising rising energy prices, tightening financing conditions and the absence of substantial wage-driven second-round inflation effects.

Its reaction function remains centred on the inflation outlook, underlying inflation and monetary transmission—not an assumed future AI productivity dividend. 

On November 5, ECB Executive Board member Piero Cipollone also outlined potential applications of AI-enabled payments in the digital euro innovation programme.

This is not a monetary-policy change. It is an indication that AI is beginning to influence the architecture of central-bank money and payment infrastructure. 

Singapore: institutionalising deployment and supervision.

On November 3, MAS announced that its Future of Finance Institute had begun operating.

Its initial priorities are practical AI adoption, development of agentic-finance capabilities and stronger risk management.

This follows MAS’s October monetary-policy decision to maintain the prevailing appreciation path of its exchange-rate policy band, while explicitly recognising that the global AI boom could create additional domestic cost pressures. 

Singapore’s approach is particularly relevant to Europe: productivity diffusion and financial supervision are being pursued together.

Meanwhile, the Swiss National Bank’s November 5 speech reinforced the importance of exchange rates in absorbing external shocks. Chairman Martin Schlegel emphasised the flexibility afforded by Switzerland’s 0–2% price-stability range and the combined use of interest-rate and foreign-exchange instruments. This was not an AI-specific policy announcement.

THE NEXT MONETARY INFLECTIONThese developments suggest that central banks increasingly confront three distinct AI-related forces.

First, the demand effect. AI investment raises demand for capital, equipment, electricity, infrastructure and specialised labour.

Second, the productivity effect. Wider adoption can increase output per worker and eventually reduce unit production costs.

Third, the financial effect. AI investment concentration, leverage and market valuations can amplify instability when expected returns change.

The difficult task is determining which force dominates at any given moment.

This also complicates estimates of the neutral interest rate, r-star.

Sustained AI investment could increase equilibrium real rates through stronger capital demand and expected investment returns. Conversely, an investment reversal could weaken demand and reduce the appropriate policy rate.

No verified announcement this week establishes a new official AI-adjusted neutral-rate estimate. The important development is growing recognition that conventional measurements may become less reliable during a major technological transition.

IMPLICATIONS FOR INVESTORS AND EUROPEAN POLICYMAKERS

For investors, the principal distinction is now between AI infrastructure demand and realised productivity.

The Bank of England’s evidence illustrates why this matters: firms anticipate substantial future productivity improvements, but realised gains remain modest.

Investment analysis should therefore increasingly distinguish between committed capital expenditure, realised revenues, incremental free cash flow and actual changes in output per worker.

For European policymakers, the problem is more structural.

Europe must finance electricity infrastructure, industrial automation, technological adoption and workforce adaptation while monetary conditions remain restrictive.

The policy priority should be to accelerate productive deployment rather than subsidise infrastructure construction independently of demonstrated demand.

Three developments deserve particular attention over the coming weeks: the US inflation release and subsequent Fed communications, the UK’s revised productivity statistics scheduled for November 17, and evidence that European AI investment is generating measurable productivity beyond the digital sector.

THE FINAL TAKE

The AI Supercycle has entered a more demanding phase for monetary authorities.

Its capital requirements are visible.

Its inflationary effects are increasingly relevant.

Its financial vulnerabilities are receiving greater supervisory attention.

But its economy-wide productivity dividend remains uncertain.

This creates a fundamental distinction between financing intelligence and monetising intelligence.

For investors, that distinction determines whether elevated valuations are sustainable.

For central banks, it determines whether AI eventually permits lower inflation without weaker growth.

For European policymakers, it determines whether industrial investment narrows the productivity divide or merely raises the cost of capital.

VELOCITY INSIGHT

THE NEXT GREAT MONETARY DIVIDE WILL NOT BE BETWEEN HAWKS AND DOVES. IT WILL BE BETWEEN ECONOMIES THAT TURN AI INVESTMENT INTO PRODUCTIVITY — AND THOSE THAT ONLY FINANCE THE BUILDOUT.
