The AI Supercycle has entered a decisive new phase. This morning, ASML confirmed extraordinary semiconductor-equipment demand extending toward 2030, while the IMF warned that rising sovereign borrowing costs threaten financial stability and the International Energy Agency identified an energy shortage that could persist into 2027.
The central contradiction is becoming unavoidable: the world’s demand for productive intelligence is accelerating faster than its capacity to finance, power and deploy that intelligence at sustainable economic returns. 
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
Will the AI Supercycle generate enough economic value to overcome the rising cost of capital—or will financial scarcity concentrate the next generation of economic power in the hands of a few corporations and sovereign investors?
This is the defining question behind The Great Split.
The technology is advancing. Demand remains extraordinary. But capital markets are beginning to differentiate between technological potential and financial productivity.

FIVE SIGNALS SHAPING THE NEXT ECONOMY
1. ACCELERATED COMPUTING — THE SCARCITY PREMIUM IS MOVING UPSTREAM
Verified facts. ASML reported €11.7 billion in quarterly sales and €5 billion in net income. Its customers are increasing equipment-demand forecasts, with some expressing interest in placing orders for 2029 and 2030.
The company is considering expanding production capacity beyond its previously announced increases and expects annual revenue of €44–45 billion.
Its shares gained approximately 3% during early European trading. 
FDK interpretation.
The economic significance extends beyond one company’s results.
The AI investment cycle is increasingly constrained by the physical capacity to manufacture advanced computing equipment.
This reinforces the competitive position of companies controlling essential production technologies.
But scarcity is not uniform across the AI value chain.
Semiconductor-equipment manufacturers can benefit from pricing power while some infrastructure developers face higher construction costs and more demanding financing conditions.
Second-order effect: The market could progressively favor indispensable equipment suppliers and cash-generating infrastructure providers over businesses whose valuations depend on deploying capacity that cannot yet be delivered.
The next source of AI pricing power may reside as much in the machines that manufacture intelligence as in the intelligence itself.
2. ENERGY — THE PHYSICAL CONSTRAINT BECOMES A FINANCIAL CONSTRAINT
Verified facts. The International Energy Agency warned this morning that diesel shortages could persist well into 2027.
Middle Eastern and Russian diesel exports declined by a combined 1.3 million barrels per day in September. Diesel refining margins reached nearly $95 per barrel during that month.
Meanwhile, Houthi forces threatened additional attacks on Saudi oil infrastructure following damage to Aramco’s headquarters. Saudi authorities reported limited damage and no casualties at the facility; a resulting interruption to oil production has not been established. 
FDK interpretation.The immediate impact extends beyond oil markets.
Higher transportation, refining and energy costs affect industrial margins, household purchasing power and inflation expectations.
For AI infrastructure, reliable power is essential to achieving expected utilization and cash flows.
The transmission mechanism is:
Energy disruption → Higher operating costs → Inflation persistence → Higher financing costs → Lower infrastructure returns.
Second-order effect: Energy resilience could become a decisive determinant of which AI infrastructure projects receive financing.
The investment opportunity consequently extends toward electricity networks, storage, efficient cooling, power electronics and energy-management technologies.
Energy resilience is becoming a component of capital productivity.
3. SOVEREIGN CAPITAL — THE NEW COMPETITION FOR GLOBAL SAVINGS
Verified facts. The IMF intensified its warning about global debt sustainability during today’s meetings in Bangkok.
Public debt is approaching 100% of global GDP. Average G7 10-year government-bond yields increased by more than 110 basis points between February and September.
The IMF projects the average effective cost of government financing to increase from 2.3% in 2021 to 3.7% by 2031. 
FDK interpretation.
The consequences for the AI Supercycle are substantial.
Governments must finance debt refinancing, defence, infrastructure and energy security while technology companies compete for long-duration capital.
That competition does not automatically imply private investment will be crowded out. But it raises the importance of borrowing costs, maturity structures and demonstrable project returns.
Second-order effect: Institutions capable of committing patient capital may gain greater negotiating power over infrastructure ownership, financing conditions and industrial partnerships.
The likely beneficiaries include strong corporate balance sheets, well-capitalized infrastructure investors and sovereign investment institutions.
The next hierarchy of economic power will increasingly reflect who controls long-duration productive capital.
4. CHINA — INDUSTRIAL ACCELERATION MEETS WEAK DOMESTIC DEMAND
Verified facts. China’s producer prices increased 5% year-on-year in September, while consumer inflation reached only 0.9%.
Core consumer inflation remained at 1%.
The contrast reflects rising industrial input costs alongside persistent weakness in domestic consumption. 
FDK interpretation.
China is confronting an increasingly important imbalance.
Its industrial economy can expand rapidly while domestic demand remains insufficient to absorb the resulting production.
The AI Supercycle potentially reinforces China’s technological and manufacturing strengths.
But higher energy and commodity prices can simultaneously compress industrial margins.
Second-order effect: Continued export-led expansion could intensify competition in global technology and industrial markets, accelerating technology diffusion while reducing pricing power in more contestable segments.
The United States and China therefore face different economic challenges.
America must reconcile exceptional technology investment with increasingly demanding financing conditions.
China must translate manufacturing strength into stronger domestic economic demand.
The Great Split is becoming a divergence between different models of capital formation, production and economic capture.
5. EUROPE — THE BANKING SYSTEM CONFRONTS THE NEXT INDUSTRIAL TRANSITION
Verified facts. European equities recovered this morning, with the STOXX 600 advancing approximately 0.5%, supported by ASML’s results.
Yet Europe’s 20 largest banks have declined by approximately 7% on average since early August, according to Reuters Breakingviews.
Separately, BlackRock reported assets under management of $15.49 trillion, supported by substantial institutional and ETF inflows. 
FDK interpretation.
Europe faces a fundamental capital-allocation challenge.Its industrial companies are participating in the AI Supercycle, but sovereign-financing pressure and fragmented capital markets constrain the continent’s capacity to mobilize investment.
The role of European banking is consequently changing.
The strategic question is no longer simply which institutions consolidate.
It is which financial structures can channel European savings toward industrial AI, energy resilience and productive infrastructure.
Higher rates may support bank revenues initially but eventually weigh on credit demand, funding costs and borrower quality.
Second-order effect: European banking consolidation could become increasingly connected to industrial competitiveness, provided larger institutions translate scale into greater productive lending and capital-market capacity.
Europe needs stronger financial institutions not merely to consolidate its banking industry, but to finance its next industrial economy.

THE HIDDEN PATTERN
THE GREAT SPLIT IS BECOMING A COMPETITION BETWEEN TWO CAPITAL SYSTEMS
Today’s developments reveal two increasingly distinct models.
The capital-productive economy combines technological leadership, secure energy, patient financing and rapid commercialization.
Its investment cycle reinforces itself:
Capital → Computing capacity → Productive deployment → Cash flow → Reinvestment.
The capital-dependent economy expands investment commitments faster than revenues and returns.
Its vulnerability is the reverse sequence:
Higher financing costs → Delayed deployment → Weaker cash conversion → Greater refinancing requirements.
The critical difference is not technological ambition.
It is the relationship between time to revenue, return on invested capital and the economic cost of financing.

THE CAPITAL-ALLOCATION HIERARCHY
For investors and boards, today’s evidence suggests three immediate priorities: favor demonstrated cash generation over speculative capacity; incorporate energy security and financing maturity into infrastructure valuations; and distinguish technological leadership from sustainable economic profitability.
The most important emerging rotation may not be from technology into traditional defensive sectors.
It may be within the AI economy itself—from businesses requiring continuous capital injections toward those capable of converting technology into recurring, capital-efficient earnings.
THE CLOSEThe AI Supercycle is entering a period of extraordinary opportunity and increasingly demanding financial discipline.
ASML’s results confirm that demand for accelerated computing remains exceptionally strong.
The IMF’s warnings demonstrate that the financial environment supporting this expansion is becoming more restrictive.
The IEA’s assessment reveals that physical energy constraints may persist longer than financial markets initially anticipated.
Together, these developments expose the next fundamental transformation of the global economy.
Technology will continue expanding the frontier of what is possible.
But financing conditions, energy availability and capital productivity will increasingly determine which possibilities become economically viable.
The first phase of AI rewarded the accumulation of computing power. The second rewarded the financing of infrastructure. The next will reward the conversion of intelligence into sustainable financial returns.
That is where the new market rotation will emerge.
And that is where The Great Split will become most consequential.
