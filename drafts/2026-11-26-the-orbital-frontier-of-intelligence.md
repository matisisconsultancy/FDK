Why the AI Supercycle Is Moving Beyond Earth—and How Orbital Computing Could Redefine Global Capital, Industrial Power and the Next Economy
NOVEMBER 26, 2026 | FDK
Introducing THE VELOCITY EDGE — SPACE & AI FRONTIER
THE BIG TAKE
The AI Supercycle is beginning to challenge the geographical boundaries of its own infrastructure. The next great frontier of intelligence may not be defined simply by computing power, but by the location, economics and strategic control of the systems that produce it.
On November 25, the US Federal Communications Commission authorized SpaceX to deploy up to one million orbital computing satellites under its Starmind initiative.
The decision represents the first US authorization for a constellation explicitly designed to operate as space-based data centers. It is a significant regulatory development, although it does not establish technical feasibility, completed financing or commercial viability. 
eu-assets.contentstack.com+1
Its economic implications could nevertheless be extraordinary.
For decades, the expansion of computing has depended on terrestrial infrastructure: electricity grids, land, cooling systems, semiconductor supply chains and increasingly large pools of financial capital.
The next stage could introduce another infrastructure layer.
Solar-powered computing systems in orbit.
Optically connected satellite networks.
Autonomous processing of information generated in space.
And eventually a hybrid intelligence architecture extending from terrestrial hyperscalers to industrial edge computing and orbital infrastructure.
This development does not mean that conventional data centers are becoming obsolete. On the contrary, terrestrial computing will likely remain dominant for the foreseeable future.
But it introduces a new possibility: the geography of intelligence could become an independent source of competitive advantage.
For technology companies, orbital computing opens a new engineering frontier.
For investors, it could create an emerging value chain connecting aerospace, semiconductors, solar energy, communications and computing services.
For governments, it extends the debate about technological sovereignty into the control of orbital infrastructure.
For Europe, it introduces an opportunity to combine its aerospace and industrial capabilities with the next phase of the AI Supercycle.
Yet one fundamental constraint remains.
Space does not eliminate the economics of scarcity. It transforms them.
The next competitive advantage will belong not necessarily to those launching the greatest number of computing satellites, but to those capable of delivering the greatest economically useful intelligence at the lowest sustainable, risk-adjusted cost.
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
What if the next phase of the AI Supercycle is determined not by who builds the largest terrestrial computing infrastructure, but by who discovers how to produce, distribute and monetize intelligence most efficiently across Earth and orbit?
That question could redefine industrial competition, sovereign strategy and global capital allocation over the coming decade.

The financial significance lies in vertical integration.

The real technological breakthrough would be proving that orbital computing can operate reliably. The real financial breakthrough would be demonstrating that it can earn sustainable returns on invested capital.

The technological opportunity is substantial.
But not every terrestrial workload benefits from operating in orbit.
Training frontier models requires dense interconnection among large numbers of processors, rapid data exchange and continuous access to sophisticated supporting infrastructure.
Those characteristics favor terrestrial hyperscale clusters.
By contrast, processing Earth-observation data directly aboard satellites may reduce the need to transmit raw information to ground facilities.
Space-native scientific and defense applications could therefore become commercially viable earlier.
The orbital opportunity is not the wholesale relocation of computing from Earth. It is the allocation of selected computing workloads to a new infrastructure layer where orbit creates measurable economic advantage.
This distinction will determine the industry's commercial evolution.
III. THE COST OF INTELLIGENCE BEYOND EARTH
The financial challenges remain formidable.
Boston Consulting Group's August 2026 analysis estimates the twenty-year total cost of ownership of orbital computing at approximately $660 million–$750 million per megawatt, compared with $230 million–$300 million for equivalent terrestrial capacity under its modeled assumptions.
The orbital premium is approximately 2.5–3 times, even assuming that important technical challenges have been addressed.

The cost structure differs fundamentally.
Terrestrial data centers incur significant recurring electricity, cooling and maintenance expenses.Orbital data centers are more heavily exposed to initial hardware and deployment expenditure, with satellite reliability and replacement determining long-term economics.
BCG estimates that computing hardware accounts for approximately half of modeled orbital total cost, while launch costs contribute approximately one-fifth.
That is why reducing launch prices alone cannot guarantee parity.
The economics also depend on lighter radiators, longer satellite lifetimes, reliable inter-satellite networks and efficient processors.
Under a realistic improvement scenario, BCG estimates that the orbital premium could narrow toward 1.5 times terrestrial costs over the next decade. Even more aggressive scenarios approach parity only under favorable assumptions.
The next orbital computing breakthrough may therefore come as much from reducing system-wide capital intensity as from improving processor performance.
PART II — THE NEW ECONOMICS OF ORBITAL INTELLIGENCE
IV. THE LEVELIZED COST OF ORBITAL INTELLIGENCE
The investment framework for orbital computing should move beyond satellite counts, theoretical computing capacity and regulatory permissions.
The governing metric should be the risk-adjusted cost of commercially delivered intelligence.
I propose the Levelized Cost of Orbital Intelligence (LCOI):
\[ \boxed{ LCOI = \frac{ PV(C_L+C_H+C_E+C_T+C_N+C_O+C_R) }{ PV(Q_D) } } \]
Where:
\(C_L\) = launch and deployment expenditure
\(C_H\) = computing hardware and radiation protection
\(C_E\) = energy-generation and storage infrastructure
\(C_T\) = thermal-management systems
\(C_N\) = communications infrastructure
\(C_O\) = operating, insurance and control costs
\(C_R\) = replacement, disposal and decommissioning expenditure
\(Q_D\) = commercially delivered, usable computing output
For consistency, the numerator and denominator must incorporate the same economic life and discounting convention. The metric must also account for hardware degradation, operating availability, communication limits and actual utilization.
A more complete investment criterion is:
\[ \boxed{ NPV_{\mathrm{orbital}} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{FCF_t}{(1+WACC)^t} -I_0 } \]
Where \(FCF_t\) denotes future free cash flows, \(WACC\) the risk-adjusted cost of capital, and \(I_0\) the initial investment.
These models are analytical proposals, not validated industry standards.
The investment decision requires both technological and financial evidence.
A constellation can achieve impressive nominal computing capacity and still destroy shareholder value if replacement costs, utilization or financing exceed expectations.
Likewise, orbital computing can remain more expensive than terrestrial computing and still generate attractive returns for specialized workloads commanding sufficient revenue premiums.
The investment winner will be determined by the value of useful intelligence delivered relative to its full lifecycle capital cost—not by the quantity of computing hardware launched.

The importance of thermal management deserves emphasis.
The vacuum of space does not cool processors through convection.
High-density computing systems must reject waste heat through radiators.
As processing power increases, radiator mass and area can become binding design constraints.
Similarly, low launch prices do not eliminate the cost of replacing failed or obsolete hardware.
Computing hardware may become commercially outdated before the satellite reaches the end of its engineered lifetime.
This introduces an unusual asset-management problem.
A long-lived satellite carrying rapidly depreciating computing technology may become economically obsolete while remaining technically operational.
The optimal architecture may therefore require smaller modular systems, upgrade strategies or specialized processors with longer economically useful lives.
The next capital advantage will accrue to architectures that reconcile rapid technological progress with the physical and financial constraints of long-duration orbital assets.
VI. THE COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE: THE SPACE–AI CONVERGENCE
SpaceX's authorization is not an isolated technological development.
Google has begun testing artificial-intelligence hardware in orbit.
Its Project Suncatcher prototype launched on October 1, 2026. On October 30, Google reported generating an AI response from a data-center-class TPU operating aboard the satellite.
The achievement demonstrated an initial inference capability. It did not demonstrate commercial hyperscale operations.
Google plans to test two interconnected satellites in 2027, with further experiments covering thermal performance, radiation and optical communications.

Other companies are pursuing orbital data-center proposals.
Starcloud has applied for an 88,000-satellite constellation, while Blue Origin has proposed Project Sunrise, involving 51,600 satellites. Neither application should be treated as an operating commercial system.

The companies building the orbital intelligence economy may not be the same companies that ultimately capture most of its economic profits.
That is a familiar pattern from earlier industrial revolutions—and a central question for investors.

PART III — EUROPE AND THE NEXT GEOGRAPHY OF ECONOMIC POWER
VIII. WHY EUROPE CANNOT AFFORD TO IGNORE ORBITAL INTELLIGENCE
Europe faces a consequential strategic choice.
It can approach orbital computing as another frontier dominated by American technology companies.
Or it can recognize that the industry combines capabilities in which Europe already possesses significant experience.
Satellite engineering.
Telecommunications.
Optical systems.
Precision manufacturing.
Aerospace materials.
Navigation.
Earth observation.
Defense.
European institutions and companies—including the European Space Agency, Airbus, Thales Alenia Space, Leonardo and Arianespace—already participate in important elements of this industrial system.
But possessing individual capabilities is not equivalent to controlling an integrated commercial platform.
Europe's challenge is organizational and financial as much as technological.
The continent must connect its aerospace and AI capabilities with the scale-up capital, institutional partnerships and industrial execution required to develop globally competitive businesses.
The objective should not be to replicate the scale of SpaceX's proposed constellation.
It should be to identify where Europe can generate superior economic returns and establish strategic capabilities that are difficult to substitute.
IX. A EUROPEAN ORBITAL INTELLIGENCE INITIATIVE
I propose launching a European Orbital Intelligence Initiative, organized around four strategic pillars.
1. Space-native AI applications. Prioritize commercial and governmental applications where intelligence is generated or needed in orbit: Earth observation, climate monitoring, maritime surveillance, autonomous satellites, disaster response and scientific processing.
2. Critical industrial technologies. Build competitive capabilities in radiation-resilient computing, optical communications, thermal management, lightweight energy systems and autonomous orbital servicing.
3. An integrated industrial ecosystem. Connect aerospace companies, semiconductor manufacturers, AI developers, universities, defense suppliers and infrastructure operators through common research, demonstration and procurement programs.
4. European growth capital. Develop financing pathways that allow promising space-technology companies to expand without depending exclusively on foreign acquisition or overseas public markets.
The emerging SIX–BME–Aquis growth-market architecture could potentially support the financing and eventual listing of European space and industrial-AI companies.
Such a role would require issuer demand, institutional participation, liquidity and regulatory coordination; exchange ownership alone would not create these conditions.
The strategic ambition should be European in scope while retaining strong connections with international investors.
Europe should seek to become a leader in economically valuable orbital intelligence, not merely a participant in the largest orbital infrastructure race.
PART IV — THE NEXT ECONOMY BEYOND EARTH
X. THE HIDDEN PATTERN: INTELLIGENCE IS BECOMING A MULTI-LAYERED INFRASTRUCTURE
The deeper significance of orbital computing is not the prospect of relocating data centers beyond Earth.
It is the emergence of a more distributed architecture of intelligence.
The first phase of modern computing concentrated processing in large terrestrial facilities.
Cloud infrastructure created global platforms.
Industrial edge computing began moving intelligence closer to productive assets and physical operations.
Orbital infrastructure could add a third dimension.

The three layers would complement rather than replace one another.
Each offers distinct technological advantages and financial trade-offs.
The strategic objective becomes allocating workloads across the infrastructure best suited to produce economically useful results.
The next generation of computing infrastructure may be defined less by its absolute scale than by its capacity to locate intelligence where it generates the greatest economic value.
This could reshape industrial organization, infrastructure financing and technological sovereignty.

THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
THE NEXT FRONTIER IS NOT SPACE ALONE. IT IS THE ECONOMICS OF INTELLIGENCE BEYOND EARTH.
The SpaceX announcement represents an important strategic inflection.
For the first time, a US regulatory authority has authorized an orbital computing project on a scale that invites consideration of space as a potential extension of global AI infrastructure.
But the industrial opportunity remains separated from commercial reality by substantial technical and financial constraints.
Solar power does not eliminate thermal-management costs.
Reusable launch does not eliminate replacement expenditure.
Regulatory permission does not establish profitable demand.
And one million authorized satellites are not one million economically productive assets.
The next investment opportunity will emerge where technological capabilities, paying customers and sustainable infrastructure economics converge.
For investors, this introduces a new value chain spanning launch technology, satellite manufacturing, semiconductors, communications, power systems and specialized computing services.
For corporate boards, it expands the strategic questions surrounding the geography, resilience and ownership of computing infrastructure.
For governments, it introduces orbital access, space governance and control of computational capability into the debate about technological sovereignty.
For Europe, it creates an opportunity to combine aerospace engineering, industrial AI and growth-capital formation in a new field of international competition.
THE CLOSING VIEW
THE AGE OF EXTRA-TERRESTRIAL INTELLIGENCE
The history of computing has been a succession of transformations in the location and economics of intelligence.
Mainframes concentrated computing within institutions.
Personal computers distributed it to individuals.
Cloud platforms reconcentrated computing within global infrastructure.
Industrial edge systems brought intelligence closer to productive physical activity.
Orbital computing could add another layer beyond Earth's surface.
Its commercial success is not assured.
But the strategic possibility is now supported by regulatory authorization, early technological demonstrations and emerging industrial competition.
The critical question is no longer simply whether a processor can operate in orbit.
It is whether orbital computing can develop into a reliable commercial service whose economic value exceeds its extraordinary capital requirements.
That is the question financial markets must ultimately answer.
Europe must also recognize the broader strategic implications.
The continent cannot afford to approach the space economy as an isolated aerospace sector disconnected from AI, industrial competitiveness and financial markets.
The next opportunity lies in combining those capabilities into a coherent system of technological and economic leadership.
The next generation of economic power may not be determined exclusively by who develops the most powerful intelligence, but by who controls the most productive infrastructure through which intelligence is generated, distributed and deployed.
Orbital computing may become one of the most important tests of that proposition.
THE FINAL VERDICT
The AI Supercycle is beginning to transcend the geographical boundaries of its own infrastructure. The next market leaders will be those capable of turning orbital ambition into reliable, scalable and economically productive intelligence.
THE CLOSING LINE
The next great frontier of the intelligence economy will not be defined by how far computing can travel beyond Earth, but by how much enduring economic value it can create wherever it operates.
In the Age of Velocity, intelligence expands the frontier. Space changes its boundaries. Capital productivity determines who captures its value.
