A new inflection in the economics of artificial intelligence

MATERIAL INFLECTION ALERT — HIGH SIGNIFICANCE

The U.S.–China AI competition has reached a consequential inflection: China is beginning to export not only competitive AI models, but also the software infrastructure that makes those models economical to operate. Meanwhile, new American financial disclosures expose the scale of capital commitments required to sustain frontier leadership.

Three developments on October 20–21 materially strengthen The Velocity Edge’s central thesis.

1. DEEPSEEK OPENS ITS INFERENCE ENGINE

On October 20, DeepSeek released DeepInfer, the inference framework supporting its production services, under the Apache 2.0 open-source license.

The release provides access to optimized computing kernels, distributed execution, speculative decoding and scheduling techniques designed to improve inference throughput.

Crucially, it supports both Nvidia Blackwell SM100 GPUs and Huawei Ascend 950 accelerators.

DeepSeek reports that a production cluster using its broader inference architecture processes 155 trillion tokens daily. That figure is company-reported and has not been independently verified. 

There are important limitations. The public release currently supports only DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, provides offline inference rather than a complete production-serving service, and excludes certain caching and load-balancing components. Published hardware benchmarks use Nvidia GPUs; comparable independent results for Huawei are not yet available. 

Nevertheless, the strategic implications are considerable.

DeepSeek is attempting to commoditize a second layer of the AI value chain: the software required to operate intelligence at scale.

The first disruption came from releasing competitive model weights.

The second could come from making sophisticated inference optimization more accessible to competing cloud operators and enterprises.

This strengthens China’s diffusion strategy without establishing parity in frontier computing.

2. AMERICA: THE FINANCIAL COST OF FRONTIER LEADERSHIP BECOMES VISIBLE

On October 20, Reuters reported details of Anthropic’s draft IPO prospectus.

The disclosures are consequential.

$518 BILLION :: Minimum cloud, computing and infrastructure commitments over approximately ten years.

$9 BILLION :: Computing and infrastructure spending during the first half of 2026, compared with $2.3 billion a year earlier.

$80.7 BILLION :: Reported first-half net loss, including approximately $72 billion in noncash accounting charges related to convertible financing instruments. 

The accounting distinction is essential: the $80.7 billion headline loss must not be confused with operating cash consumption.

The more consequential financial signal is the enormous contractual exposure to future computing capacity.

Anthropic’s disclosures sharpen the investment question facing American frontier developers.

Can rapidly expanding revenues support the growing financial commitments required to maintain model leadership?

The American AI frontier is increasingly becoming a long-duration financial obligation as well as a technological advantage.

For investors, this raises the importance of compute utilization, contractual flexibility, pricing power and revenue durability.

3. CHINA: SEMICONDUCTOR SUBSTITUTION IS ALTERING MARKET SHARE

On October 21, Reuters reported that Samsung and SK Hynix are upgrading their Chinese NAND memory plants as competition from domestic manufacturer YMTC intensifies.

According to Omdia figures cited by Reuters, YMTC captured 38.5% of China’s NAND market in the second quarter, compared with 28% during 2025.

Samsung’s share declined to 33.5%.

Samsung and SK Hynix are seeking to produce more advanced memory in China, but upgrades remain subject to U.S. equipment-export restrictions and licensing requirements.This is a different semiconductor market from the advanced AI accelerators that determine frontier training capacity.

Nevertheless, the development provides tangible evidence that China’s substitution strategy is generating competitive pressure in parts of the semiconductor supply chain.

It also reveals an important second-order effect.

Export controls may constrain access to technology while simultaneously reinforcing incentives for domestic substitution.

Whether those incentives ultimately generate superior manufacturing economics remains an open question.

THE HIDDEN PATTERN: TWO ECONOMIES, TWO FINANCIAL REGIMES

The three developments point toward a clearer competitive asymmetry.

UNITED STATES — FRONTIER CAPITAL REGIME

Technological leadership → Infrastructure commitments → Premium capability → Monetization requirements.

CHINA — DIFFUSION ECONOMICS REGIME

Domestic hardware alternatives → Open inference infrastructure → Lower deployment barriers → Wider industrial adoption.

These models are not mutually exclusive.

American companies also develop efficient inference systems, while Chinese technology companies continue investing heavily in infrastructure.

The distinction is one of strategic emphasis, not exclusive capability.

The emerging investment hypothesis is that frontier technology could remain concentrated while the software economics of deploying intelligence become progressively more competitive.

This would create substantial differences in earnings growth across the AI value chain.

⸻

THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT: THE NEXT MARKET ROTATION

DeepInfer’s release introduces an additional mechanism through which inference costs could decline.

If independent operators can reproduce DeepSeek’s optimizations, competition among inference providers may intensify.

Lower prices could stimulate more workloads, increasing aggregate demand for computing capacity while compressing margins for less efficient operators.

That creates a potentially divergent outcome.

Advanced accelerator manufacturers may continue benefiting from infrastructure demand.

Cloud operators may face greater pressure to demonstrate utilization and operating efficiency.

Model providers could experience further pricing competition.

Industrial enterprises may capture an increasing share of AI’s economic benefits through improved productivity.

For The Velocity Edge, the distinction is between growth in intelligence consumption and growth in the profits earned by producing it.

The two should no longer be assumed to move together.

WHAT INVESTORS SHOULD WATCH NEXT

The decisive near-term test is independent benchmarking of DeepInfer on Nvidia and Huawei hardware, measured through cost per completed workload, throughput, latency and energy consumption.

For American frontier companies, attention should turn toward the financial implications of long-term infrastructure commitments, particularly the relationship between contractual obligations, recurring revenues and free-cash-flow generation.

For Chinese semiconductor suppliers, market-share gains must be evaluated alongside manufacturing economics and the sustainability of margins.

The next material inflection would occur if Chinese inference optimization demonstrated competitive performance across domestic accelerators at a materially lower total deployment cost.

That would move the competition beyond cheaper AI models toward a more substantial challenge to American infrastructure economics.

VELOCITY INSIGHT

AMERICA STILL LEADS THE AI FRONTIER. CHINA IS CHALLENGING THE COST OF ACCESSING AND DEPLOYING IT. THE NEXT MARKET LEADERS WILL BE DETERMINED NOT BY WHO PRODUCES THE MOST INTELLIGENCE, BUT BY WHO CAPTURES THE ECONOMIC VALUE OF ITS DIFFUSION.
