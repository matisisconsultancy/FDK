China — the full-stack strategy becomes explicit
Alibaba’s Apsara announcements are the most important technology signal overnight. The company unveiled its Zhenwu V900 AI processor, which it says delivers three times the performance of its predecessor and can participate in clusters of up to 500,000 accelerators. Commercial mass production is scheduled for the first quarter of 2027. Alibaba also disclosed that earlier-generation Zhenwu chips have already been deployed across hundreds of external customers spanning autos, finance, energy, manufacturing and embodied intelligence. 
This matters because China’s competitive architecture is becoming increasingly symmetrical with the US hyperscaler model:
models → proprietary chips → supernodes → cloud → agents → enterprise distribution.
The company is also targeting more than 20 GW of global cloud data-centre capacity by 2032 and says customer demand for AI is already exceeding supply. 
But the GVI does not add another +0.1 today. The new chip is not yet in commercial mass production and the 20-GW target is future capacity. The evidence raises China’s momentum, not today’s structural score.

Energy — the first genuine reversal of the September shock
This is today’s most important measured macro change.
Saudi Arabia restarted the East-West pipeline on September 22. The route had been shut since September 11 after drone attacks damaged three pumping stations. It is currently operating at reduced rates; restoration to full capacity could take six to eight weeks. The pipeline can bypass Hormuz and has recently carried roughly 4 million barrels per day, equivalent to about 4% of global supply. 
Brent subsequently closed below $100 and traded around $99.18 early Wednesday. 
This finally changes the classification from:
price relief only
to:
price relief + partial infrastructure restoration.
The GVI therefore admits a positive Smart Capital contribution today.
But the penalty is not fully reversed. The pipeline remains below normal capacity, Hormuz risks remain material and monetary policy is still restrictive.
South Korea — resilience becomes industrial policy
South Korea announced a ten-year resource-security strategy aimed at cutting its dependence on Middle Eastern crude from roughly 70% in 2025 to 50% by 2035, expanding crude stockpiles by about 20 million barrels by 2030, and broadening its critical-minerals list from 38 to 51 materials, including germanium, an important semiconductor input. 
This is precisely the type of policy architecture that matters in an increasingly physical AI economy:
semiconductors + energy security + critical minerals + stockpiles = industrial resilience.
The plan is official, but its targets remain prospective. No points are added today.

Institutional interpretation — 
The Full-Stack Race
The evidence is converging toward a more powerful definition of competitive advantage.
The first AI race was about the model.
The second was about the GPU.The third became about energy and capital.
The emerging race is about controlling the entire system:
Silicon → Memory → Networking → Models → Data → Agents → Power → Capital → Distribution.
Alibaba’s strategy makes that architecture explicit. Saudi Arabia’s pipeline restoration shows why physical resilience matters. South Korea’s resource-security strategy shows that semiconductor leadership increasingly depends on energy and minerals. US enterprise AI companies show that value creation is migrating from raw model capability toward verification, cybersecurity and specialised data. 
The main accelerator: integration
Velocity rises when complementary layers reinforce each other.
Owning a powerful model is valuable.
Owning the model plus compute, memory, data, power, distribution and financing is much more valuable because feedback loops become faster and dependency risk falls.
The main decelerator: financing still costs too much
The oil shock is easing, but central banks have not followed it downward. Two-year US yields remain close to 4.8%, and another Fed increase is still meaningfully priced. 
The hurdle therefore remains:
AI productivity must compound faster than the combined cost of capital, energy and infrastructure.
For investors, the opportunity is moving from generic “AI exposure” toward companies that control scarce bottlenecks or convert them into recurring cash flow: memory, networking, power equipment, specialised data, cybersecurity and highly utilised compute.
For executives, the strategic question should be less “Which AI model do we use?” and more “Which layers of the AI stack must we control to protect economics, resilience and speed?”
For policymakers, the Korean response is increasingly instructive as a structural concept: technological competitiveness must be integrated with energy security, critical minerals, grid capacity, capital markets and supply-chain redundancy.
