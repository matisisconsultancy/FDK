New Central-Bank Evidence Reveals Why the AI Supercycle Could Raise Neutral Rates Before Delivering Its Productivity Dividend
Where Monetary Policy, Industrial AI and the Great Split Converge
THE BIG TAKE
A consequential development emerged on November 9: the Reserve Bank of Australia published a major speech and four research studies examining the economic consequences of artificial intelligence.
Together, they provide unusually concrete evidence of the divergence between AI capital formation and realised productivity.
The central findings are striking. Australian data-centre investment has increased tenfold in three years. South Korean DRAM export prices have risen more than 200% over twelve months. Australian household financial wealth exposed to AI-related equities has increased substantially. Yet the RBA still finds little evidence of a material AI-driven improvement in aggregate productivity.
Most importantly, the RBA is explicitly examining whether the investment boom is contributing to a higher neutral interest rate.
This is the monetary significance of the Great Split: AI investment is becoming economically measurable faster than its productivity dividend.

II. THE MONETARY BREAKTHROUGH — AI AND THE NEUTRAL RATE
In her November 9 speech, Assistant Governor Sarah Hunter identified an increasingly important channel through which AI could affect monetary policy.
Large-scale AI investment increases the demand for capital. If global savings do not expand correspondingly, equilibrium real interest rates may rise.
The RBA notes tentative evidence consistent with this mechanism: its model estimates of the neutral rate have increased in recent years, although the contribution attributable specifically to AI remains uncertain.
Hunter also emphasised that stronger growth and higher interest rates can coexist, citing the expansion of the internet during the late 1990s and early 2000s.
Crucially, this is analytical recognition, not a newly announced AI-specific estimate of neutral rates or a change in the RBA’s policy target. 
The implication for global monetary policy is substantial.
A productivity revolution does not necessarily require lower interest rates. During the investment phase, the opposite may occur.
AI could raise the equilibrium cost of capital before it lowers the economy’s cost of production.
This reinforces the High-Rate Productivity scenario within the Secular Volatility framework.
III. INDUSTRIAL AI DEFLATION — THE EVIDENCE IS STILL INCOMPLETE
The RBA’s research distinguishes sharply between technology adoption and productivity creation.
Business adoption has accelerated, but much remains relatively limited. Approximately 35% of surveyed firms reported no productivity gains from AI, while only around 11% reported significant gains.
At the economy-wide level, the RBA has not yet identified a substantial productivity improvement attributable to AI.Hunter also challenged the assumption that AI will produce permanent disinflation.
Initial infrastructure investment increases demand and may raise prices. Productivity improvements can subsequently expand supply. But higher productivity may itself stimulate spending, investment and incomes.
Consequently, the long-run inflation effect could be broadly neutral rather than persistently negative. 
This distinction is fundamental for the ECB and Federal Reserve.
Industrial AI deflation should not be incorporated into monetary-policy expectations before productivity gains become observable.
For investors, the relevant distinction is between companies investing in AI infrastructure and companies converting AI into higher output, lower unit costs and stronger free cash flow.
IV. THE GREAT SPLIT — EMPLOYMENT AND WEALTH
The newly published RBA labour-market analysis provides another important qualification.
There is little evidence of widespread AI-driven employment displacement. Nevertheless, tentative signs of weaker hiring and employment outcomes are emerging among younger workers in more AI-exposed occupations.
These findings are descriptive and do not establish that AI caused the deterioration.
The RBA’s research also recognises that AI may affect the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment, complicating conventional interpretations of labour-market slack. 
Meanwhile, the financial benefits of AI investment remain concentrated.
Australian household exposure to AI-related equities increased from 2.0% of financial wealth at end-2022 to 5.3% in June 2026, mostly through retirement savings.
This creates an additional transmission channel:
AI valuations → household wealth → consumption → monetary conditions.
The central-bank problem is becoming more complex. An AI equity correction may simultaneously weaken wealth, financial confidence and investment demand, even before employment deteriorates materially. 
V. WHAT CHANGES FOR THE ECB, FED AND EUROPE?
The November 9 research strengthens three existing policy concerns without establishing that the ECB, Fed or Bank of England has changed its reaction function today.
First, imported AI inflation may become more important. The RBA identifies substantial price increases in semiconductor supply chains, particularly South Korean memory chips. These pressures have begun reaching downstream products, although direct consumer-price effects remain concentrated in relatively small expenditure categories. European policymakers should monitor the distinction between upstream investment costs and broad consumer inflation. 
Second, energy and infrastructure constraints can create geographical divergence. Countries with reliable electricity, available grid capacity and efficient infrastructure financing are better positioned to convert AI spending into productive capacity. For Europe, this increases the importance of integrated energy markets, faster permitting and deeper capital markets.
Third, financial supervision must examine the consequences of AI investment concentration. The RBA’s findings complement the BIS’s November 3 warning concerning overinvestment, complex financing arrangements and concentrated technology-related risks. A reassessment of AI investment returns could transmit through corporate credit, equity markets and non-bank financial institutions. 
The most important European lesson is that subsidising investment volume is not equivalent to raising productivity.
The policy objective should be to shorten the interval between AI capital formation and measurable industrial productivity.
THE FINAL TAKE — THE MONETARY PRICE OF THE AI SUPERCYCLE
The November 9 evidence reinforces a distinction that financial markets cannot afford to ignore.
AI’s investment cycle is already influencing capital demand, industrial supply chains and financial exposures.
Its productivity dividend remains uncertain in both magnitude and timing.For central banks, this creates a demanding transition: restrictive monetary conditions may be necessary to contain inflation, even while the economy undertakes investments essential to expanding future productive capacity.
For investors, it raises the importance of capital efficiency, balance-sheet resilience and realised returns.
For European policymakers, it makes Industrial AI deployment—not simply infrastructure expenditure—the strategic priority.
The next monetary inflection will come when central banks can distinguish the inflation created by building AI from the disinflation generated by using it.
VELOCITY INSIGHT
THE COST OF BUILDING INTELLIGENCE IS ALREADY VISIBLE. THE RETURN ON INTELLIGENCE MUST STILL BE PROVEN.
