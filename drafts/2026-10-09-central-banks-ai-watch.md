THE AI SUPERCYCLE: THREE CENTRAL BANKS, THREE DIFFERENT RISKS

In Focus | 9 October 2026

The AI Supercycle has reached a new policy threshold. The Federal Reserve is responding to its inflationary consequences, the Bank of England is confronting its systemic financial risks, and Singapore is approaching another monetary tightening as AI-driven growth strengthens its economy.

Three developments on October 8–9 reveal a monetary-policy divergence that is becoming increasingly consequential.

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller explicitly identified AI investment as a contributor to persistent inflation and reiterated the case for additional interest-rate increases.

Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey warned that a reassessment of AI earnings and adoption expectations could transmit instability across equity, credit and sovereign-debt markets.

Meanwhile, all ten economists surveyed by Reuters expect the Monetary Authority of Singapore to tighten monetary policy on October 14, with the extraordinary AI investment boom supporting growth alongside renewed energy-related inflation pressures. 

The Great Split is no longer simply between economies that produce AI and those that consume it. It is emerging between economies exposed to different monetary and financial consequences of the same investment boom.

1. THE FED: AI INFLATION IS NOW AN ARGUMENT FOR HIGHER RATES

Waller’s October 8 speech provides an important confirmation of the Federal Reserve’s changing reaction function.

He explained that evidence accumulated during 2026 showing that the AI infrastructure expansion was materially increasing high-technology consumer prices, while projections for the scale of investment continued to rise.

Combined with persistent energy pressures and trade-policy uncertainty, this helped justify September’s 25-basis-point increase in the federal funds target range to 3.75–4.00%.

Waller expects further tightening if economic conditions evolve as anticipated, although increases need not occur at consecutive meetings.

Sixteen of eighteen FOMC participants projected at least one additional increase during the remainder of 2026. 

The second-order implication is significant.

AI investment is becoming sufficiently large to influence the inflation outlook before corresponding productivity gains are fully measurable.

That changes the conventional technological-disinflation argument.

The Fed cannot justify easier monetary policy on the basis of productivity improvements that have yet to materialise.

2. THE BANK OF ENGLAND: THE NEXT AI SHOCK COULD BEGIN IN CREDIT

Bailey’s October 8 speech adds a different dimension.

He recognised AI’s capacity to strengthen potential growth, but warned that increasing financial exposure to the technology creates new systemic vulnerabilities.

If expected AI earnings or adoption rates disappoint, the resulting adjustment could spread beyond technology equities into corporate credit and sovereign-debt markets.

Bailey also connected these vulnerabilities to leveraged investment strategies, fiscal credibility and the capacity of core financial markets to absorb shocks. 

The transmission mechanism is increasingly clear:

AI EARNINGS DISAPPOINTMENT → EQUITY REPRICING → CREDIT SPREAD WIDENING → DELEVERAGING → SOVEREIGN-MARKET PRESSURE.

This is a crucial evolution.

The AI Supercycle is no longer exclusively a question of how much capital companies can raise.

It is becoming a question of whether financial markets can absorb the consequences if that capital fails to generate the expected returns.

For investors, the distinction between AI capital formation and AI economic value creation is becoming fundamental.

3. SINGAPORE: THE FIRST TEST OF AN AI-DRIVEN EXTERNAL SURPLUS ECONOMY

Singapore presents a third monetary configuration.

According to Reuters’ October 9 survey, all ten economists expect MAS to tighten monetary conditions at its October 14 review.The expectation reflects strong economic growth, supported partly by global AI investment, together with renewed inflation risks from energy and food.

August core inflation was 2.2%, while headline inflation reached 2.3%.

Unlike the Fed and ECB, MAS conducts monetary policy principally through the Singapore dollar’s effective exchange rate rather than a conventional policy interest rate.

The anticipated tightening could therefore take the form of a steeper appreciation path for the currency. This remains an analyst forecast, not an announced MAS decision. 

The significance is structural.

Singapore benefits from participation in the global semiconductor, electronics and AI infrastructure supply chains.

It is therefore experiencing a different transmission of the Supercycle from Europe.

AI strengthens Singapore’s productive economy and external demand while monetary policy seeks to contain imported inflation.

Europe, by contrast, faces a comparatively smaller domestic AI infrastructure boom while remaining exposed to higher global capital costs.

4. THE WTO PROVIDES THE DECISIVE EVIDENCE

The most important new quantitative evidence comes from the World Trade Organization’s October 8 report.

The WTO raised its 2026 forecast for world merchandise trade growth from 1.9% to 3.9%.

The AI Supercycle explains a substantial portion of that revision.

Three figures stand out:

• 47%: the share of global merchandise trade growth attributable to AI-enabling goods during the first half of 2026.
• 67%: year-on-year growth in trade in semiconductors, servers and related AI-enabling products.
• At least 30%: the WTO’s projected increase in global AI infrastructure spending during 2026. 

These figures establish that AI capital formation is no longer a marginal contributor to global trade.

It has become a central source of international demand.

But the geographical distribution of its benefits remains highly concentrated.

The demand shock is global. The production and productivity benefits are unevenly distributed.

That is the empirical foundation of the Great Split.

5. THE EUROPEAN MONETARY DILEMMA

For the ECB, these developments reinforce an increasingly difficult policy problem.

Europe faces three simultaneous pressures.

First, the global AI infrastructure boom contributes to demand for capital and can put upward pressure on long-term yields.

Second, energy prices continue to threaten inflation persistence.

Third, Europe’s relatively smaller AI investment base limits its participation in the immediate capital-formation benefits.

ECB Chief Economist Philip Lane’s October 5 analysis already identified this asymmetry: higher global yields can tighten European financial conditions by more than domestic AI investment supports economic activity. 

The emerging European risk is therefore:

IMPORTED CAPITAL-COST PRESSURE + ENERGY INFLATION + LIMITED DOMESTIC AI CAPITAL FORMATION = WEAKER PRODUCTIVITY CONVERGENCE.

The danger is not simply slower technological progress.

It is a widening divergence between Europe’s cost of capital and its capacity to earn returns on new productive investment.

The policy consequence

Europe needs to accelerate investment in compute, electricity generation, grid capacity, storage, industrial automation and workforce skills.

But it must also strengthen the financing infrastructure connecting European savings with European investment.

Otherwise the ECB could face an increasingly uncomfortable trade-off: monetary tightening that restrains domestic investment without materially reducing the global forces driving capital costs.

FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS

I. Monetary policy. The Fed is explicitly linking AI investment to persistent inflation and the possible need for additional rate increases.

II. Financial stability. The Bank of England is identifying a potential transmission from AI earnings disappointments into corporate credit and sovereign markets.

III. International divergence.Singapore illustrates how economies deeply integrated into AI supply chains can experience stronger growth and different monetary-policy pressures.

IV. Capital formation. The WTO’s latest evidence demonstrates that AI infrastructure investment is already reshaping global trade on an extraordinary scale.

V. Europe. The essential policy challenge is to capture more of the AI productivity dividend rather than merely absorb its financing and energy costs.

THE FINAL QUESTION: WHO CAPTURES THE RETURN?

The next phase of the AI Supercycle will not be defined exclusively by technological leadership.

It will be determined by the capacity of economies and companies to convert extraordinary capital formation into sustainable productivity, earnings and free cash flow.

The monetary implications follow directly.

Countries generating substantial AI investment may experience stronger demand and greater inflationary pressure.

Countries financing the boom without capturing comparable productive returns may experience higher capital costs and weaker growth.

Financial systems heavily exposed to AI valuations may confront the consequences if earnings fail to justify investment.

The Great Split has therefore entered its financial phase.

The first winners of the AI Supercycle were those who controlled computing power. The next winners will be those who convert that power into economic returns faster than the cost of capital rises.
