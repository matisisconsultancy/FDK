3. MARVELL–LUMENTUM — THE INFRASTRUCTURE BOTTLENECK MOVES TO CONNECTIVITY
On November 2, Marvell announced its proposed acquisition of optical-components manufacturer Lumentum.
The transaction has an enterprise value of approximately $115 billion, including debt, and represents one of the largest bets yet on AI infrastructure.
The strategic rationale concerns optical connectivity.
As increasingly large numbers of accelerators operate together, the ability to transfer information between processors becomes critical to computing performance.
Yet the financial reaction was unfavorable.
Marvell shares declined more than 10% on November 2, while Lumentum fell more than 15%, reflecting investor concerns about valuation and execution risks.
The transaction remains subject to regulatory approval and is expected to close in 2028. 
The significance extends beyond semiconductors.
The AI infrastructure competition is evolving from individual processors toward integrated computing systems incorporating memory, networking, optical interconnects, energy and software.
For American companies, this creates opportunities to capture additional infrastructure revenues.
For China, it introduces further technological dependencies that domestic suppliers must overcome.
For investors, it reinforces the distinction between identifying a strategic bottleneck and paying an economically attractive price to control it.

THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT — TWO AI ECONOMIES, ONE FINANCIAL CONSTRAINT
The three developments reveal an emerging competitive divergence.
The American model is increasingly defined by frontier capabilities, advanced computing infrastructure and substantial capital intensity.
The Chinese model is increasingly characterized by efficient models, broad distribution and an emphasis on industrial and commercial diffusion.
Neither system is financially unconstrained.
At the Hong Kong Global Financial Leaders’ Investment Summit on November 3, Xiao Yuanqi, deputy head of China’s National Financial Regulatory Administration, warned that concentrated AI investment could raise borrowing costs for traditional industries and increase financial risks.
At the same event, senior executives from Blackstone, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan emphasized continued demand for AI investment while acknowledging valuation and capital-allocation risks. 
The deeper implication is that the AI Supercycle can accelerate technologically while becoming more difficult to finance profitably.
This distinction is central to The Velocity Edge.
Expanding token consumption does not guarantee profitability for model developers.
Increasing demand for advanced computing does not guarantee attractive returns for infrastructure investors.
And growing industrial adoption does not automatically establish measurable productivity improvements.
The winners will be those capable of converting technological capability into sustainable financial performance.

THE BIG TAKE

1. NVIDIA — THE COMPETITION MOVES BEYOND NATIONAL BORDERS
On November 3, the Financial Times reported that Nvidia has instructed international cloud and data-center partners to sell at least 51% of their computing services to non-Chinese customers.
The reported requirement applies to future contracts, not existing agreements. It has been communicated verbally rather than through a publicly documented regulation.
Chinese technology companies have increasingly rented advanced computing capacity in overseas data centers to access processors unavailable for direct import into China.
Nvidia’s reported restriction therefore targets an important alternative route to frontier computing capacity.
This is a corporate commercial restriction operating alongside U.S. export controls—not a newly announced U.S. government regulation. 
Why it matters: The competitive advantage of the United States increasingly includes the ability to influence access to advanced computing infrastructure outside its own territory.
For China, the strategic incentives to develop domestic accelerators, memory technologies and independent computing infrastructure become stronger.
However, tighter restrictions could also reduce Nvidia’s addressable market and encourage the adoption of non-American alternatives.
The second-order effect could be an acceleration of technological separation rather than a permanent reinforcement of American semiconductor dominance.
2. CHINA — THE TOKEN ECONOMY ENTERS MASS-MARKET COMMERCE
A second development is equally revealing.
Reuters reported on November 3 that artificial-intelligence computing credits have become a significant product category in China’s Singles’ Day shopping festival.
During a November 2 livestream, China’s prominent online retailer Austin Li sold 100,000 monthly memberships to Alibaba’s QwenWork AI service in approximately 30 minutes.
Those memberships represent access to approximately 2 trillion AI tokens.
Alibaba reported that sales of AI-token packages increased 450% during October 1–20 compared with the previous month.
Major Chinese platforms, including Alibaba’s Tmall and JD.com, are now distributing token packages from multiple AI developers, including DeepSeek, Moonshot, MiniMax and Z.AI. These are company-reported sales figures; prepaid tokens do not establish equivalent realized consumption or productivity gains. 
This is a particularly important development for our New General Theory of Competitive Advantage.
Intelligence is beginning to acquire the characteristics of a standardized, commercially distributed input.
The analogy with electricity is imperfect: tokens differ substantially in quality, capability and productive value.
Nevertheless, distributing intelligence through mass-market commercial platforms could accelerate adoption and price competition.
China’s opportunity is not necessarily to manufacture the world’s most powerful model.
