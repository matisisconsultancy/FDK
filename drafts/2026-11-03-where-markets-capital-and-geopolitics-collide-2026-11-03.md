THE GREAT COST SHIFT
AI’s Profits Are Concentrating While Its Financing, Energy and Political Costs Spread Across the Global Economy
THE BIG TAKE — THE NEXT MARKET ROTATION WILL BE DETERMINED BY WHO CAPTURES THE VALUE AND WHO ABSORBS THE COST
The most consequential development this morning is not another technology selloff. It is the emergence of a profound redistribution of costs across the AI Supercycle—one that is beginning to reshape corporate profitability, capital markets, industrial competitiveness and political priorities.
Three developments reveal the magnitude of this transformation.
First, the cost of financing AI is becoming increasingly visible. Oracle announced a $20 billion financing package on November 2, including $10 billion in equity and $10 billion in bonds carrying coupons between 7% and 8.65%. Its shares fell 8.8% following the announcement.
Second, the cost of accelerated computing is spreading beyond the companies building it. Apple reported quarterly revenue of $114.48 billion, exceeding expectations, but forecast a decline in gross margins from 47.9% to 45–46%, partly because of rising memory-chip costs.
Third, the scale of future financial commitments is becoming extraordinary. Anthropic’s amended IPO filing disclosed approximately $895.4 billion in aggregate purchase and lease commitments, substantially related to computing infrastructure, despite the company remaining operationally unprofitable in its latest quarter. 
The consequences are now spreading through global financial markets.
South Korean equities fell as much as 4.1% overnight. Taiwanese shares declined 3.3%. European equity futures were down approximately 0.9%.
The US ten-year Treasury yield traded near 4.91%, while Brent crude stabilized around $105 following Monday’s dramatic decline.
Meanwhile, Americans are voting today in midterm elections that could influence fiscal policy, AI regulation, infrastructure investment and the political tolerance for rising electricity costs associated with data centres. 
These developments are connected by a single underlying economic mechanism: the costs of technological acceleration are increasingly being transferred across companies, financial institutions, consumers and national economies.
The semiconductor supplier captures the scarcity premium.
The hyperscaler finances the infrastructure.
The corporate customer absorbs the computing cost.
The electricity system requires additional investment.
The consumer may ultimately face higher prices.
And financial markets must determine whether the resulting productivity gains justify the capital committed.
This is the next evolution of The Great Split.
The emerging competitive divide is no longer simply between technological leaders and laggards. It is between those capable of capturing the economic rents generated by technological acceleration and those increasingly bearing its financial costs.
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
What happens when the companies creating the greatest technological value are no longer necessarily the companies capturing the greatest financial returns—and the costs of that technological progress begin spreading across the wider economy?
The answer may determine the next global market rotation.
Our central thesis is that the AI Supercycle remains expansionary, but its financial consequences are becoming increasingly uneven.
Capital will increasingly favour businesses that can preserve pricing power, finance investment competitively and convert AI deployment into measurable productivity. Companies and economies absorbing higher costs without comparable productivity gains face the greatest risk of competitive deterioration.

III. FIVE SIGNALS SHAPING THE NEXT ECONOMY
1 | AI — THE SUPERCYCLE IS BECOMING A CAPITAL-MARKET EVENT
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
Oracle announced plans to raise $20 billion through a combination of bonds and new equity.
Its share offering was priced at an 11.2% discount to Friday’s closing price.
Its debt financing includes fixed-rate bonds maturing between 2029 and 2046.
Oracle had approximately $125.34 billion in borrowings at the end of August, against fiscal 2026 revenue of $67.36 billion. Management expects free cash flow to turn positive in the second half of fiscal 2028. 
Anthropic provides an equally revealing perspective.
Its updated prospectus reported third-quarter revenue of approximately $16.96 billion, up 47% sequentially.
Yet it recorded an operating loss of approximately $567 million and disclosed aggregate future commitments of $895.4 billion, substantially related to computing and infrastructure.Its reported net loss is substantially affected by noncash financing-related accounting adjustments. Consequently, the operating result and cash-flow disclosures provide a more useful assessment of underlying business economics than the headline net loss alone. 
VELOCITY INTERPRETATION
The AI Supercycle is entering a phase in which financing conditions may increasingly determine technological competitiveness.
Access to computing remains essential.
But the ability to finance computing is becoming equally important.
The relevant transmission is:
Computing Demand → Infrastructure Commitments → Financing Requirements → Cost of Capital → Financial Returns → Future Investment Capacity.
The strategic danger is a potential mismatch between rapidly growing demand and even faster growth in capital obligations.
Oracle illustrates the pressure associated with financing infrastructure expansion.
Anthropic illustrates the importance of long-duration computing commitments.
Neither case establishes that the investment is uneconomic.
But both demonstrate why financial markets are becoming more demanding.
MARKET GUIDANCE
The next allocation decision should distinguish three groups.
Capital providers may benefit from attractive contractual yields, provided credit risks are adequately priced.
Infrastructure operators face greater exposure to utilization, financing and depreciation risks.
AI application providers may generate attractive returns if they can monetize computing without assuming proportionate infrastructure obligations.
Our call: the next financial winners will increasingly be determined by the relationship between contracted revenue, financing duration and incremental returns on capital.
The greater risk lies where long-term obligations are financed against uncertain future revenues.

2 | SEMICONDUCTORS — THE SCARCITY PREMIUM IS BECOMING A MARGIN PROBLEM
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
Apple’s latest results demonstrate how technological scarcity can create competing financial outcomes.
The company exceeded earnings expectations.
Quarterly revenue reached $114.48 billion.
Its iPhone business generated $57.58 billion in revenue.
But Apple warned that higher memory prices would pressure profitability during the important holiday quarter.
Management expects gross margins of 45–46%, compared with 47.9% in the preceding quarter.
Apple also indicated that AI-related cloud costs are affecting the economics of its services. 
VELOCITY INTERPRETATION
This development exposes a fundamental asymmetry within the AI value chain.
Advanced memory manufacturers benefit from strong demand and constrained supply.
Their customers absorb higher component prices.
Some customers possess sufficient pricing power to pass those increases to consumers.
Others must accept lower margins.
The resulting transmission is:
AI Infrastructure Demand → Memory Scarcity → Component Inflation → Margin Compression → Consumer Prices or Lower Returns.
This is the beginning of a broader distributional question.
The semiconductor industry’s extraordinary profitability can coexist with deteriorating economics for downstream manufacturers.
That does not mean semiconductor scarcity will persist indefinitely.
Additional capacity and technological substitution could eventually reduce the premium.
But in the current phase, AI-driven scarcity is redistributing financial returns across the technology ecosystem.
MARKET GUIDANCE
Differentiate between companies controlling scarce inputs and those purchasing them.
Advanced memory, specialized interconnects and efficient computing technologies may continue enjoying relative advantages.
Downstream hardware manufacturers deserve more scrutiny of pricing power, inventory management and margins.
Our call: the next semiconductor rotation may increasingly favour technologies capable of lowering the total cost of computing rather than merely expanding its capacity.

3 | SOVEREIGN CAPITAL — THE GLOBAL COST OF MONEY IS BECOMING UNEQUAL
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
Australia’s central bank raised its policy rate by 25 basis points to 4.85% today, its fifth increase during 2026.
The decision passed by a 6–3 vote.
The bank cited continued inflation concerns while signalling that further tightening is not predetermined.
Meanwhile, China’s central bank announced an increase in the foreign-exchange reserve requirement ratio from 4% to 6%, effective November 15.
Reuters estimates the measure will absorb approximately $24 billion in foreign-currency liquidity.
The yuan has appreciated more than 5% against the dollar this year. 
Europe faces a different challenge.
The ECB’s latest lending survey shows net tightening in corporate credit standards of 9%, compared with an expected 5%.
Banks attribute the deterioration partly to heightened risk perceptions, geopolitical uncertainty and energy-related pressures.
The survey also identifies gradual growth in lending demand related to AI infrastructure. 
VELOCITY INTERPRETATION
The global monetary environment is increasingly differentiated.
Some central banks are responding to persistent inflation with additional tightening.
Others are managing exchange-rate pressures and liquidity conditions.
Europe must reconcile restrictive financing conditions with substantial industrial investment requirements.
The consequence is a widening difference in the financial conditions under which companies compete.
A technological investment generating attractive returns in one financing environment may be significantly less attractive in another.
This creates an important second-order effect.
Capital may increasingly migrate toward countries offering more favourable combinations of financing costs, energy availability and regulatory predictability.
THE EUROPEAN DIMENSION
France remains particularly exposed to the interaction among sovereign uncertainty, financing costs and weak investment.
Its recent negative sovereign outlook and third-quarter economic contraction warrant continued monitoring.
The critical risk is transmission from sovereign financing conditions into corporate investment.
This is not yet evidence of a generalized European credit crisis.
But persistent differences in financing conditions can influence the future location of productive investment.
MARKET GUIDANCE
Monitor sovereign spreads alongside corporate credit, rather than treating government-bond markets as an isolated asset class.
For Europe, the French OAT–Bund spread, bank funding costs and corporate investment commitments remain central indicators.
Our call: sovereign credibility is increasingly becoming a determinant of industrial competitiveness.

4 | ENERGY AND GEOPOLITICS — THE NEXT COST TRANSFER COULD INVOLVE THE ELECTRICITY SYSTEM
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
Brent crude stabilized around $105 during early Asian trading, after falling more than 7% on November 2 amid hopes of diplomatic progress involving the United States and Iran.
However, reports of US strikes on Iran’s Qeshm Island have introduced renewed uncertainty.
The broader supply and geopolitical outlook remains unresolved. 
A second energy development is becoming increasingly important.
American data-centre expansion has generated political debate over electricity costs and infrastructure financing.
Congress has considered proposals intended to prevent large electricity users from transferring the full costs of new grid infrastructure to households and smaller businesses.
The political debate includes questions of permitting, ratepayer protection and the allocation of infrastructure costs. 
VELOCITY INTERPRETATION
The AI Supercycle is beginning to confront a fundamental economic question.
Who should pay for the electricity infrastructure required to support accelerated computing?
If technology companies finance new generation and transmission capacity, infrastructure development becomes more capital-intensive for them.
If utilities finance the expansion, the costs may be reflected in regulated rates, subject to regulatory decisions.
If governments provide subsidies, part of the burden moves toward public finances.Each arrangement has different implications for corporate returns and capital allocation.
The transmission is potentially significant:
AI Electricity Demand → Grid Investment → Cost Allocation → Corporate Margins, Consumer Prices or Public Spending.
The ultimate distribution of costs is not predetermined.
It depends on regulation, commercial contracts, investment economics and political choices.
MARKET GUIDANCE
Electricity networks, power management, grid modernization and energy-efficiency technologies remain strategically attractive.
But investors must distinguish the beneficiaries of increased investment from those financing it.
Our call: the next major infrastructure opportunity may emerge around reducing the cost of delivering reliable electricity—not simply increasing installed capacity.

5 | THE US ELECTIONS — POLITICAL POWER IS BECOMING A VARIABLE IN THE AI INVESTMENT EQUATION
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
Americans vote today in elections determining control of Congress.
Polling before the election indicates that Democrats have a meaningful opportunity to gain control of the House, while the Senate remains competitive.
No election outcome is established at the time of publication.
Reuters reports that investors are evaluating the implications for fiscal spending, debt-ceiling negotiations, AI oversight and data-centre investment. 
VELOCITY INTERPRETATION
The election is consequential for financial markets because different congressional configurations could alter the political environment for capital formation.
A divided Congress may increase legislative constraints and fiscal negotiation risks.
A Republican sweep could support policy continuity, deregulation and potentially greater fiscal expansion.
Democratic control of one or both chambers could increase scrutiny of technology companies, AI safety and data-centre development.
None of these outcomes mechanically determines market performance.
The financial consequences will depend on the policies pursued and the response of Treasury yields, credit spreads and corporate investment.
THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT
The more consequential question may be the political allocation of infrastructure costs.
The expansion of AI has created substantial demand for electricity, land, water and local infrastructure.
This is becoming relevant to voters and policymakers.
The AI Supercycle is moving from a largely corporate investment decision toward a broader debate about the distribution of its economic costs and benefits.
MARKET GUIDANCE
Investors should avoid positioning exclusively on a predicted election winner.
The more useful approach is to monitor the policy channels through which election outcomes affect capital markets.
Our call: the most consequential election-related variables will be Treasury financing, AI regulation, energy infrastructure and the fiscal capacity to support productive investment.

IV. BEYOND THE HEADLINES — THE HIDDEN PATTERNS
FIRST — THE AI BOOM MAY BE CREATING A NEW FORM OF COST INFLATION
The most visible inflationary consequences of AI are not necessarily appearing in consumer-price indices.
They are emerging in the prices of scarce components, specialized infrastructure, electricity access and financing.
The financial transmission is uneven.
Some suppliers capture higher margins.
Others face greater investment costs.
Consumers may eventually absorb part of those increases.
The hidden risk is that the infrastructure phase of AI becomes more expensive before the productivity phase generates sufficient savings to offset it.
SECOND — THE NEXT MARKET CORRECTION COULD BE A REPRICING OF FINANCING RISK
Oracle’s fundraising demonstrates the importance of access to capital markets.
Anthropic’s commitments reveal the scale of future financing and commercial obligations.
Together, they point toward an increasingly complex financing architecture.
The critical variables are maturity, utilization, contractual revenue and counterparty concentration.The next financial adjustment may begin not with weaker AI demand, but with higher financing costs and declining confidence in the returns available to infrastructure investors.
THIRD — THE GREAT SPLIT IS BECOMING A DIVIDE BETWEEN RENT CAPTURE AND COST ABSORPTION
This is the most consequential hidden pattern.
Economic value is not distributed proportionately across the AI ecosystem.
Suppliers controlling scarce technologies may earn exceptional returns.
Infrastructure operators may face substantial capital intensity.
Application providers can benefit from falling computing costs as technology diffuses.
Industrial companies may improve productivity, but only after completing difficult organizational transformations.
The next hierarchy of competitive advantage will increasingly depend on where each company sits within the process of value migration.
FOURTH — THE COST OF CAPITAL COULD DETERMINE THE GEOGRAPHY OF THE NEXT ECONOMY
Technological innovation is globally interconnected.
The financing of innovation remains heavily influenced by national institutions, capital-market depth and sovereign credibility.
This creates the possibility of a geographic redistribution of investment.
Countries combining strong financing conditions, reliable energy and effective industrial execution may attract a disproportionate share of new productive capacity.
The next phase of The Great Split may occur between economies possessing similar technologies but very different capacities to finance and deploy them.

THE FINAL TAKE — THE NEW DISTRIBUTION OF ECONOMIC POWER
The events of November 3 reveal a transformation more consequential than a correction in technology valuations.
The AI Supercycle is continuing to expand.
Commercial demand is growing.
Computing capabilities are accelerating.
Companies are committing extraordinary amounts of capital to infrastructure.
But the financial consequences are becoming increasingly unequal.
Oracle demonstrates the cost of financing the expansion.
Anthropic reveals the scale of long-term commitments required to compete.
Apple illustrates how rising component costs can compress margins even at extraordinarily profitable technology companies.
The semiconductor industry demonstrates the economic value of technological scarcity.
Central banks reveal how different national financing conditions influence the cost of productive investment.
And today’s US elections introduce a political dimension into the distribution of infrastructure costs.
These developments are not independent.
They reveal a structural migration of financial costs and economic returns across the Next Economy.
The first phase of the AI Supercycle was defined by technological acceleration.
The second was defined by the rapid accumulation of computing infrastructure.
The third is increasingly being defined by the distribution of financial obligations and economic value.
The implications extend beyond Silicon Valley.
Industrial companies must decide how much capital to commit to technologies whose productive potential remains substantial but unevenly realized.
Financial institutions must determine which infrastructure investments generate adequate risk-adjusted returns.
Sovereign economies must decide how to finance the electricity, transport and digital infrastructure required to compete.
Investors must distinguish between the businesses creating technological capabilities and those ultimately capturing the financial benefits.
The Great Split is therefore evolving into a more fundamental division between economic value creation, value capture and cost absorption.
The companies controlling scarce technologies may continue capturing exceptional profits.
But the companies absorbing the costs of those technologies must demonstrate that productivity improvements justify the investment.
The same applies to national economies.
Technological capabilities alone will not determine future competitiveness.
The decisive advantage will increasingly belong to those capable of financing technological transformation, deploying it efficiently and retaining an attractive share of the value created.
The next great market rotation will follow the redistribution of that economic surplus.
For investors, this means looking beyond revenue growth toward incremental returns on capital.
For boards, it means treating financial productivity as a central strategic discipline.
For policymakers, it means recognizing that the cost of building the Next Economy is becoming inseparable from the distribution of its benefits.
THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
The next financial shock may not come from the failure of the AI Supercycle. It may come from the widening gap between those capturing its extraordinary profits and those financing its extraordinary costs.
The next hierarchy of economic power will be determined not simply by who creates the future—but by who captures its value without bearing a disproportionate share of its cost.
