China Is Turning Artificial Intelligence into an Industrial Export Strategy. The Next Battle Will Be for the Global Distribution of Economic Value.
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
What if America continues to dominate the technological frontier of AI, while China becomes the first economy to industrialize the global export of intelligence?
Three consequential developments on November 26–27 materially strengthen our governing thesis.
China is formally incorporating AI services into its national export strategy, accelerating domestic computing infrastructure and expanding industrial AI deployment across its manufacturing ecosystem.
The emerging competitive challenge is no longer confined to DeepSeek or frontier-model rankings. It is becoming a coordinated industrial and commercial strategy.
1. THE BIG TAKE — FROM MADE IN CHINA TO INTELLIGENCE FROM CHINA
On November 26, China's Ministry of Commerce and seven other government departments published the 15th Five-Year Development Plan for Trade in Services.
The plan includes a dedicated section on developing AI-related service exports, emphasizing the international expansion of Chinese cloud computing, AI models and digitally delivered services.
Its priorities encompass international AI commercialization, hardware–software integration, industrial applications, cross-border data flows and greater participation in international standards.
The strategic significance is considerable: China is seeking to replicate elements of its manufacturing export model in the emerging global market for intelligence.
The next competitive advantage may emerge from exporting not simply physical products, but the intelligence embedded in products, services and industrial processes.

The unresolved financial question is whether installed capacity can generate competitive revenues and sustainable returns on invested capital.

Conceptual feedback mechanism, conditional on international adoption, infrastructure availability and positive economic returns.
The mechanism is potentially self-reinforcing.
Larger domestic markets generate deployment experience. Greater utilization can support software optimization and improved system economics. More efficient intelligence can expand international demand, generating additional opportunities for scale and reinvestment.
But none of these relationships is guaranteed.
International regulatory barriers, semiconductor constraints, cybersecurity concerns and weak commercialization could limit the flywheel.

7. THE NEXT VELOCITY METRIC — INTELLIGENCE EXPORT INTENSITY
We propose incorporating an additional measure into the Global Velocity Index:

Where \(X_i^{AI}\) represents identifiable cross-border revenues from AI models, inference services, AI-enabled cloud computing and associated software exports, with care taken to avoid double counting.
The Intelligence Export Intensity Ratio would track an economy's ability to convert its AI capabilities into internationally monetizable services.
It complements measures of innovation velocity, intelligence productivity and financial fragility.
The distinction is central: possessing advanced AI is not the same as exporting it profitably.
8. FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS, BOARDS AND POLICYMAKERS
1. The geography of AI value creation is expanding. Investors should examine enterprise distribution, recurring service revenues and industrial adoption alongside frontier-model rankings.
2. Chinese industrial AI deserves closer attention. Boards should evaluate whether AI-enabled Chinese competitors can produce durable cost and productivity advantages, rather than treating every deployment announcement as proof of a breakthrough.
3. Computing infrastructure is becoming strategic export capacity. Governments must assess electricity, connectivity, semiconductors and trade policy as interconnected elements of competitiveness.
4. Lower inference costs may expand demand faster than they compress revenues. The financial outcome depends on demand elasticity, commercial differentiation and sustainable system-level margins.5. Europe faces a strategic choice. It can remain predominantly a purchaser of foreign intelligence or develop competitive industrial AI capabilities, sovereign infrastructure and internationally scalable applications.
THE FINAL TAKE — THE NEXT GEOGRAPHY OF ECONOMIC POWER
The November 27 developments materially strengthen the diffusion and industrial-policy dimensions of our U.S.–China thesis.
America continues to possess important frontier-model and advanced-computing advantages. China is developing a coordinated approach to the production, distribution and export of increasingly affordable intelligence.
The next investment cycle will reward companies capable of turning those advantages into durable cash flows, industrial productivity and international market positions.
The first phase of the AI Supercycle rewarded those who built intelligence. The next will reward those who industrialize its economic value.
America leads the frontier. China is building an export economy around intelligence. The next great capital rotation will follow the systems that turn intelligence into globally scalable earnings.
