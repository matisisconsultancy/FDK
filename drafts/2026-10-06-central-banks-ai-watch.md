THE GREAT SPLIT HAS ENTERED MONETARY TRANSMISSION

The Big Take — 6 October 2026

The most consequential new development is coming from the ECB. The AI Supercycle is no longer simply changing growth and inflation; it is beginning to change how monetary policy itself is transmitted through the economy.

New ECB analysis published today finds that the euro area’s most AI-intensive firms are structurally different borrowers. They tend to be less leveraged, more equity-financed and, when they do borrow, increasingly reliant on market debt rather than bank loans. Because market debt generally carries longer maturities, these companies are less exposed to the conventional cash-flow impact of ECB rate hikes and more exposed to movements at the long end of the yield curve.

That has a major second-order implication:

THE SAME ECB RATE CAN NOW PRODUCE DIFFERENT MONETARY CONDITIONS FOR THE AI ECONOMY AND THE REST OF THE ECONOMY.

This may become a new mechanism widening the Great Split.

The new monetary divide

Traditional European companies — particularly SMEs — remain heavily dependent on banks. Higher ECB policy rates therefore feed relatively quickly into their borrowing costs, investment decisions and cash flows.

AI-intensive companies are increasingly different. They have more equity, greater access to securities markets and lower overall leverage. Their investment can therefore remain resilient even while monetary tightening suppresses activity elsewhere.

ECB Chief Economist Philip Lane quantified the scale yesterday: the AI ecosystem is now responsible for just under one percentage point of annual euro-area corporate credit growth — roughly one quarter of the total increase. At the same time, he warned that the global AI investment boom is helping push up long-term interest rates by substantially more than Europe’s comparatively smaller AI boom can justify domestically.

Today’s ECB research makes that diagnosis considerably more powerful.

Europe is facing an asymmetric transmission mechanism:

GLOBAL AI CAPEX
→ HIGHER GLOBAL LONG-TERM YIELDS
→ HIGHER EUROPEAN COST OF CAPITAL

but:

EUROPEAN AI CAPITAL FORMATION
< U.S. AI CAPITAL FORMATION

The ECB also finds that investment responds more strongly to positive AI shocks in the United States than in the euro area, reflecting deeper venture-capital markets, broader equity financing and a larger unified market. European savings are meanwhile increasingly flowing into U.S. technology equities rather than European technology companies.

This is the monetary version of the Great Split:

Europe imports the tightening generated by the AI boom faster than it captures the productivity generated by it.

Japan moves AI from analysis toward the reaction function

The second important development comes from the Bank of Japan.

Yesterday Deputy Governor Shinichi Uchida put AI into the discussion of the natural rate. Today Governor Kazuo Ueda moved one step closer to policy action.

Ueda explicitly identified strong AI-related demand alongside energy prices and the weak yen as a risk that could push underlying inflation above the BOJ’s 2% target. He said financial conditions remained accommodative even after September’s rate increase and reaffirmed that the BOJ would continue raising borrowing costs as monetary support is withdrawn.

This is one of the clearest examples yet of AI moving from a central-bank productivity scenario into a near-term inflation and tightening argument.

The sequencing increasingly looks like this:

AI INVESTMENT → DEMAND → ENERGY + CAPITAL SCARCITY → INFLATION → TIGHTER POLICY

before:

AI DIFFUSION → PRODUCTIVITY → LOWER UNIT COSTS → INDUSTRIAL-AI DEFLATION

The monetary-policy problem is therefore fundamentally about timing.

BIS: AI could make the next financial crisis faster

A third consequential signal came from BIS General Manager Pablo Hernández de Cos.His October 5 speech adds an important financial-stability dimension: widespread use of similar AI models across trading, portfolio allocation, risk management and liquidity management could increase herding, market co-movement and the speed of price adjustments during stress.

The BIS argument is particularly consequential because it combines AI with two other structural changes: historically elevated government debt and the increasing role of leveraged non-bank financial institutions.

That creates another second-order chain:

COMMON AI MODELS
→ COMMON SIGNALS
→ SYNCHRONISED POSITIONING
→ FASTER MARKET MOVES
→ LIQUIDITY STRESS
→ FASTER CENTRAL-BANK INTERVENTION

But central banks may simultaneously have less room to intervene, because large sovereign debt burdens make bond-market purchases increasingly vulnerable to accusations of fiscal financing.

AI therefore changes not only the probability distribution of financial instability.

It may change its velocity.

The ECB’s emerging two-rate problem

Olli Rehn reinforced another part of the emerging ECB framework today. He argued that rapidly rising long-term yields are already slowing activity and limiting the transmission of expensive energy into wages and broader prices, even while strong AI investment is helping keep the euro-area economy resilient.

That means monetary conditions increasingly have two components:

THE POLICY RATE — controlled by the ECB.

THE LONG RATE — increasingly influenced by global AI capital formation, sovereign issuance and term premia.

The ECB may therefore encounter an unusual regime in which the AI boom is simultaneously:

supporting demand
and
tightening financial conditions.

That makes future policy considerably less mechanical.

The five implications now

1. The Great Split is becoming a monetary-transmission split. AI leaders may remain investment-resilient while interest-sensitive SMEs and traditional industry absorb disproportionate tightening.
2. The long end matters more than the policy rate. AI capex, corporate issuance and sovereign borrowing increasingly compete for the same pool of global capital.
3. Industrial-AI deflation remains a second-stage phenomenon. Central banks are being forced to react to investment demand, energy scarcity and financing pressures before the productivity dividend is fully visible.
4. For investors, financing structure becomes a core AI factor. The next dispersion should increasingly separate internally funded and equity-funded AI winners from companies whose economics depend on continuous refinancing at rising long-term rates.
5. For Europe, Capital Markets Union is becoming monetary policy by another route. Without deeper European equity and risk-capital markets, tighter ECB policy can suppress traditional investment while failing to generate sufficient European AI capital formation to deliver the future productivity offset.

THE NEW CENTRAL-BANK EQUATION

The critical question is shifting again.

It is no longer merely:

Will AI raise productivity enough to lower inflation?

It is becoming:

Can central banks finance and stabilise the transition long enough for the productivity dividend to arrive?

That is the deeper significance of today’s evidence.

The Great Split is moving from markets into monetary transmission itself — and once that happens, a single interest rate no longer means a single economic reality.
