The most consequential new development is coming from the ECB. The AI Supercycle is no longer simply changing growth and inflation; it is beginning to change how monetary policy itself is transmitted through the economy.

New ECB analysis published today finds that the euro area's most AI-intensive firms are structurally different borrowers. They tend to be less leveraged, more equity-financed and, when they do borrow, increasingly reliant on market debt rather than bank loans. Because market debt generally carries longer maturities, these companies are less exposed to the conventional cash-flow impact of ECB rate hikes and more exposed to movements at the long end of the yield curve.

The second-order implication is stark: the same ECB rate can now produce different monetary conditions for the AI economy and the rest of the economy. This may become a new mechanism widening the Great Split.

Europe imports the tightening generated by the AI boom faster than it captures the productivity generated by it.

::section The New Monetary Divide | asymmetric transmission
Traditional European companies — particularly SMEs — remain heavily dependent on banks. Higher ECB policy rates therefore feed relatively quickly into their borrowing costs, investment decisions and cash flows.

AI-intensive companies are increasingly different. They have more equity, greater access to securities markets and lower overall leverage. Their investment can therefore remain resilient even while monetary tightening suppresses activity elsewhere.

ECB Chief Economist Philip Lane quantified the scale yesterday: the AI ecosystem is now responsible for just under one percentage point of annual euro-area corporate credit growth — roughly one quarter of the total increase. At the same time, he warned that the global AI investment boom is helping push up long-term interest rates by substantially more than Europe's comparatively smaller AI boom can justify domestically.

Europe is facing an asymmetric transmission mechanism. Global AI capex drives higher global long-term yields, which drive a higher European cost of capital — yet European AI capital formation lags U.S. AI capital formation. The ECB also finds that investment responds more strongly to positive AI shocks in the United States than in the euro area, reflecting deeper venture-capital markets, broader equity financing and a larger unified market. European savings are meanwhile increasingly flowing into U.S. technology equities rather than European technology companies.

::signals Three Central-Bank Signals | ECB, BOJ, BIS
# Japan moves AI into the reaction function
Yesterday Deputy Governor Shinichi Uchida put AI into the discussion of the natural rate. Today Governor Kazuo Ueda moved one step closer to policy action, explicitly identifying strong AI-related demand alongside energy prices and the weak yen as a risk that could push underlying inflation above the BOJ's 2% target. He said financial conditions remained accommodative even after September's rate increase and reaffirmed that the BOJ would continue raising borrowing costs as monetary support is withdrawn.

The sequencing increasingly runs AI investment → demand → energy and capital scarcity → inflation → tighter policy, *before* AI diffusion → productivity → lower unit costs → industrial-AI deflation. The monetary-policy problem is therefore fundamentally about timing.
signal: AI has moved from a productivity scenario into a near-term tightening argument.

# BIS: AI could make the next crisis faster
BIS General Manager Pablo Hernández de Cos used his October 5 speech to warn that widespread use of similar AI models across trading, portfolio allocation, risk management and liquidity management could increase herding, market co-movement and the speed of price adjustments during stress. The argument is amplified by two other structural changes: historically elevated government debt and the growing role of leveraged non-bank financial institutions.

The chain runs from common AI models to common signals, synchronised positioning, faster market moves, liquidity stress and faster central-bank intervention. But central banks may simultaneously have less room to intervene, because large sovereign debt burdens make bond-market purchases increasingly vulnerable to accusations of fiscal financing.
signal: AI may change not only the probability of instability but its velocity.

# The ECB's emerging two-rate problem
Olli Rehn reinforced the framework today, arguing that rapidly rising long-term yields are already slowing activity and limiting the transmission of expensive energy into wages and broader prices, even while strong AI investment keeps the euro-area economy resilient. Monetary conditions now have two components: the policy rate, controlled by the ECB, and the long rate, increasingly influenced by global AI capital formation, sovereign issuance and term premia.

The ECB may therefore face an unusual regime in which the AI boom is simultaneously supporting demand and tightening financial conditions. That makes future policy considerably less mechanical.
signal: The long end now matters as much as the policy rate.

::patterns The Five Implications Now | what changes for policy and capital
# Transmission split
The Great Split is becoming a monetary-transmission split. AI leaders may remain investment-resilient while interest-sensitive SMEs and traditional industry absorb disproportionate tightening.

# The long end rules
AI capex, corporate issuance and sovereign borrowing increasingly compete for the same pool of global capital. The long end matters more than the policy rate.

# Deflation is second-stage
Industrial-AI deflation remains a later phenomenon. Central banks must react to investment demand, energy scarcity and financing pressures before the productivity dividend is fully visible.

# Financing structure is an AI factor
For investors, the next dispersion should separate internally and equity-funded AI winners from companies whose economics depend on continuous refinancing at rising long-term rates.

# CMU as monetary policy
For Europe, Capital Markets Union is becoming monetary policy by another route. Without deeper European equity and risk-capital markets, tighter ECB policy can suppress traditional investment while failing to generate sufficient European AI capital formation.

::section The New Central-Bank Equation | the deeper significance
The critical question is shifting again. It is no longer merely: *will AI raise productivity enough to lower inflation?* It is becoming: can central banks finance and stabilise the transition long enough for the productivity dividend to arrive?

That is the deeper significance of today's evidence.
