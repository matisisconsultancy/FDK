AI is no longer being financed by technology companies alone. Governments, utilities, banks and industrial balance sheets are now moving into the same capital chain.
The newest evidence is unusually clear. Washington is preparing a $4.2 billion loan to Vistra to expand nuclear output as AI data centres push U.S. electricity demand higher. Broadcom has agreed to provide Anthropic with up to $42 billion of infrastructure financing. In Japan, JERA, Dell and RHAELM are developing a $15 billion, 400-MW AI data-centre project with Apollo as financial partner. Europe, by contrast, still finances most corporate AI investment from retained earnings. 
The implication is bigger than AI.
Capital formation itself is becoming industrial policy.
And because today is Sunday, the operative market horizon is Monday, 5 October.
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
Who wins the AI race when power, credit and industrial policy become the same market?
The old hierarchy was simple: algorithms, GPUs, models.
The new hierarchy is broader:
compute → power → financing → infrastructure → strategic control
That is where The Great Split is moving next.

FIVE SIGNALS SHAPING THE NEXT ECONOMY
1 | POWER IS BECOMING A SOVEREIGN AI ASSET
VERIFIED. The U.S. government plans to lend Vistra about $4.2 billion to uprate at least three nuclear stations. Vistra’s fleet produces more than 6.5 GW, and Washington has explicitly linked rising electricity demand to AI data centres, transport electrification and other power-intensive uses. The administration has set a goal of quadrupling U.S. nuclear capacity by 2050. 
FDK READ.
This is a structural break.
AI infrastructure is becoming too strategic—and too capital intensive—to remain a purely private technology story.
The state is entering the stack because electricity has become part of computational sovereignty.
The investment consequence is clear:
the scarcity premium is migrating from chips toward firm power, grids, nuclear, storage and transmission.
The next hyperscaler advantage may depend as much on megawatts secured as GPUs purchased.


⸻


2 | VENDOR FINANCING IS BECOMING PART OF THE AI BUSINESS MODEL
VERIFIED. Broadcom has agreed to lend Anthropic up to $42 billion, potentially financing roughly one-third of Anthropic’s $125.2 billion five-year TPU lease commitment. Broadcom is expected to become deeply tied to Anthropic both as supplier and financier. 
FDK READ.
The AI cycle is evolving from:
sell the chip
to
finance the customer who buys the chip.
That accelerates demand but changes the risk.
Revenues, hardware sales, customer creditworthiness and financing exposure begin to reinforce one another.
The critical variable is no longer simply backlog.
It is the quality of the balance sheet underneath the backlog.
That makes credit spreads, lease structures, utilization rates and refinancing schedules increasingly important leading indicators for AI equities.


⸻


3 | EUROPE’S AI PROBLEM IS INCREASINGLY A CAPITAL-FORMATION PROBLEM
VERIFIED. ECB data show 72% of euro-area companies expect to finance AI investment through internal funds.Only 16% expect bank loans, 6% equity or venture capital and just 1% debt securities. More than 80% expect to rely on a single source of finance. 
Meanwhile, France is seeking around €54 billion in budget savings for 2027 while debt-market pressure and domestic protests are rising. 
FDK READ.
This is the European contradiction in one frame.
The U.S. is using:
corporate debt + private capital + vendor financing + public balance sheets
to scale the Next Economy.
Europe is still asking many companies to finance transformation largely out of cash flow.
That difference compounds.
Internal finance favors incumbents, tangible assets and incremental projects. It penalizes intangible, high-risk, high-velocity investment.
Europe therefore risks losing the AI race not because it cannot invent—but because it cannot finance scale fast enough.


⸻


4 | THE ENERGY SHOCK IS ACCELERATING ELECTRIFICATION
VERIFIED. Renault plans to invest more than €10 billion in France over five years in electric vehicles and affordable cars. EVs reached a record 42% of French new-car registrations in September, with Renault explicitly citing higher fuel prices as a demand catalyst. 
At the same time, the G7 has agreed to release 100 million barrels of oil and diesel reserves after China restricted fuel exports, while a reported Houthi attack targeted an Aramco facility in Riyadh over the weekend. 
FDK READ.
The energy shock has two opposing effects.
It raises inflation and financing costs.
But it also shortens the economic payback of electrification.
That creates a powerful second-order mechanism:
oil insecurity → higher fuel cost → better EV economics → faster electrification → more grid demand
The winners are not merely EV manufacturers.
They are batteries, charging, storage, grids and electricity providers.
The mobility transition is becoming an energy-security trade.


⸻


5 | ITALIAN BANKING HAS MOVED FROM OPTIONALITY TO PATH DEPENDENCY
VERIFIED. Intesa Sanpaolo raised its offer for Monte dei Paschi di Siena, adding another €800 million of cash value. Its broader bid is worth about €34 billion. MPS shareholders are due to vote on 29 October on a defensive strategy involving possible bids for Banco BPM and Banca Generali; approval could allow Intesa to withdraw its offer. 
FDK READ.
The Italian banking contest is no longer a series of independent transactions.
Each move now changes the feasible moves of every other player.
This is capital concentration in real time.
The strategic question is shifting from who buys whom to which balance sheets become large enough to compete across Europe.
That matters beyond Italy.
Higher capital requirements, AI investment, sovereign volatility and wealth-management economics are increasing the value of scale.
The Banking Union is increasingly being built by transaction rather than treaty.

THE HIDDEN PATTERN
The same mechanism now appears across sectors.
Washington is financing nuclear power. Semiconductor companies are financing AI customers. Private capital is financing hyperscale data centres. Renault is funding electrification. Germany is sending €1 billion of military aid and €350 million for Ukrainian energy infrastructure while moving toward joint drone production. A U.S.–Ukraine investment fund has begun backing critical minerals, batteries and energy hubs. 
Different headlines.
One underlying shift:
CAPITAL IS BECOMING STRATEGIC INFRASTRUCTURE.
This is the new industrial-policy regime.
Capital is no longer simply allocated to the highest expected return.
It is increasingly directed toward power, compute, defence, minerals, mobility and financial institutions deemed strategically necessary.
That will create winners whose advantage comes not only from technology or operating performance—but from privileged access to capital.


⸻


WHERE CAPITAL IS LIKELY TO MOVEACCELERATING: nuclear and firm power; grid infrastructure; advanced semiconductors; HBM and networking; contracted AI infrastructure; infrastructure credit; defence technology; batteries and storage; electrified mobility; critical minerals; large European banks with consolidation optionality.
DECELERATING: uncontracted compute capacity; high-duration technology without cash-flow visibility; energy-intensive businesses without pricing power; leveraged infrastructure exposed to refinancing; fragmented European corporates dependent solely on retained earnings; weaker financial institutions unable to absorb rising technology and compliance costs.
The investment rule is becoming sharper:
Do not just own scarcity. Own scarcity with financing power.

THE MONDAY TEST
For investors, the first question is whether Friday’s weak U.S. payroll report can pull long yields sustainably lower. U.S. stocks rallied Friday—the Nasdaq rose 1.19% and the S&P 500 0.73%—as the probability of an October Fed hike fell sharply. But the 10-year Treasury touched 5.34% only a day earlier. If bonds fail to rally meaningfully after softer jobs, the structural cost-of-capital thesis remains intact. 
For executives, watch the financing architecture around competitors as closely as their product roadmaps. The emerging advantage is the ability to secure long-duration power, cheap funding and committed demand simultaneously.
For policymakers, Europe’s central question is no longer how much AI investment it wants. It is how quickly it can build capital markets capable of underwriting it.
And for boards, the new strategic test is simple: does the company control a scarce asset—or merely consume one at somebody else’s financing cost?

FINAL TAKE — THE GREAT SPLIT MOVES INTO THE BALANCE SHEET
The AI race began with models.
Then it moved to chips.
Now it is moving into power and capital.
The U.S. government financing nuclear expansion, Broadcom financing Anthropic, Apollo financing Japanese AI infrastructure, European companies relying overwhelmingly on retained earnings, and banks consolidating for scale are not separate stories.
They describe the same economic transition.
The Next Economy is becoming so capital intensive that the architecture of finance is becoming part of competitive advantage itself.
That changes the winners.
The strongest will combine technology, infrastructure, cash flow and privileged financing into one system.
The weakest will discover that being technologically competitive is not enough if the balance sheet cannot fund the race.
The first Great Split was technological.
The next one will be financial.
And in the decade ahead, who controls the capital may matter as much as who controls the intelligence.
