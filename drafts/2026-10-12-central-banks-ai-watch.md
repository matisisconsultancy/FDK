THE GREAT SPLIT ENTERS THE GLOBAL POLICY ARCHITECTURE

AI Is Sustaining Global Growth While Reshaping Employment and Financial Stability

In Focus | 12 October 2026

THE BIG TAKE — FROM MONETARY DIVERGENCE TO SYSTEMIC COORDINATION

Two consequential developments emerged today as the IMF–World Bank Annual Meetings opened in Bangkok.

First, the Financial Stability Board has brought its finalised AI supervisory practices to the G20, moving international coordination beyond risk identification toward a common framework for responsible adoption.

Second, the IMF has quantified an increasingly important economic divergence: AI infrastructure investment is supporting global growth near 3%, but its benefits are concentrated geographically while its effects on employment are becoming more disruptive.

Together, these developments expose a new dimension of the Great Split:

AI IS SUPPORTING AGGREGATE DEMAND BEFORE ITS PRODUCTIVITY BENEFITS ARE BROADLY DISTRIBUTED — WHILE FINANCIAL SUPERVISORS ARE BEING FORCED TO PREPARE FOR THE CONSEQUENCES. 

1. THE FSB: AI SUPERVISION BECOMES A G20 PRIORITY

On October 12, Financial Stability Board Chair and Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey presented the organisation’s latest financial-stability agenda ahead of the October 15 G20 meeting.

The FSB confirmed that it has finalised sound practices for responsible AI adoption, incorporating public consultation feedback.

The framework addresses organisation-wide governance, AI lifecycle management, human oversight, cybersecurity and third-party dependencies.

These are nonbinding supervisory practices, not a new international regulation. Nevertheless, their finalisation is significant.

AI supervision is moving toward a common international reference framework.

Bailey’s letter also connects AI vulnerabilities with volatility in sovereign bond markets and fragilities in private credit.

That connection matters because AI is simultaneously changing financial institutions’ operations and increasing the capital requirements of the wider economy. 

The second-order effect

International supervisory convergence could reduce fragmentation and strengthen resilience.

But compliance costs could also favour large financial institutions with sufficient technology, capital and specialist personnel.

The danger is a supervisory paradox:

Stronger institutional AI controls could coexist with greater concentration among the technology providers on which those institutions depend.

For central banks, the central question becomes whether better individual-bank governance is sufficient when many institutions share the same models, infrastructure and service providers.

2. THE IMF: THE AI INVESTMENT SHOCK IS ALREADY MACROECONOMIC

Speaking in Bangkok on October 12, IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva provided important quantitative evidence.

AI infrastructure investment is helping sustain global growth at approximately 3%, despite the negative effects of the energy supply shock.

According to Georgieva, AI-related investment could contribute approximately 0.5–1 percentage point to growth in advanced Asian economies and 0.2–0.8 percentage points in emerging Asian economies.

These are estimated contributions, not confirmed realised gains.

The distinction is nevertheless consequential.

AI is already producing geographically concentrated increases in investment demand, export activity and economic growth. 

The monetary implication

Central banks must increasingly distinguish three effects:

CAPITAL FORMATION: higher investment demand, with potential inflationary pressure.

PRODUCTIVITY DIFFUSION: higher potential output, with possible disinflationary consequences.

GEOGRAPHICAL DIVERGENCE: different growth and inflation outcomes depending on each economy’s position in the AI supply chain.

The Great Split therefore creates different monetary-policy conditions across countries exposed to the same technological transformation.Importantly, today’s IMF remarks do not establish a new central-bank interest-rate decision or a revised estimate of the neutral rate.

They strengthen the evidence that policymakers need to distinguish the investment phase from the productivity phase.

3. SINGAPORE: THE EMPLOYMENT DIVIDE BECOMES VISIBLE

Singapore President Tharman Shanmugaratnam identified another emerging risk at the Bangkok meetings.

AI is already reducing demand for some entry-level graduate positions, particularly in highly exposed sectors.

His concern extends beyond immediate unemployment.

When graduates lose access to their first professional positions, they may also lose opportunities to acquire experience, develop skills and advance through conventional career structures.

Tharman warned that insufficient policy responses could produce a new divergence between advanced and developing economies. 

This introduces a more complicated labour-market problem for central banks.

AI can support investment and aggregate output while weakening employment opportunities in specific occupations.

The resulting unemployment may not respond to conventional monetary stimulus if it reflects structural displacement rather than insufficient demand.

This is an interpretation of the emerging evidence, not a formal change in the MAS reaction function.

4. THE EUROPEAN CONSEQUENCE

For Europe, the combination of these developments is particularly important.

The United States and major Asian economies are capturing substantial portions of AI infrastructure investment.

European companies remain more dependent on internally generated funds and less able to mobilise risk capital at comparable scale.

Meanwhile, global AI financing demand can contribute to higher long-term interest rates, affecting European investment even where domestic AI capital formation remains relatively limited.

Europe therefore faces a threefold challenge:

**GLOBAL CAPITAL-COST PRESSURE

• UNEVEN AI PRODUCTIVITY DIFFUSION
• EMERGING EMPLOYMENT DISPLACEMENT.**

The appropriate European response extends beyond monetary policy.

EU policymakers need to accelerate industrial AI adoption, improve access to growth capital, expand reliable energy capacity and redesign workforce-transition mechanisms.

They must also ensure that AI supervision encourages competition and resilience rather than reinforcing dependence on a small number of foreign technology providers.

5. WHAT’S NEXT — THE POLICY CATALYSTS

The next important developments are already identifiable.

On October 13, ECB Chief Economist Philip Lane is scheduled to speak ahead of the International AI Summit in Dublin. His remarks may provide new evidence on how the ECB is integrating AI investment and productivity expectations into monetary analysis.

On October 15, G20 finance ministers and central-bank governors will discuss the FSB’s supervisory agenda.

The IMF’s updated global economic outlook will also provide an opportunity to evaluate whether AI-related investment is changing growth, inflation and financial-stability assessments.

FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS AND POLICYMAKERS

1. Financial supervision: The FSB’s finalised AI practices represent a meaningful advance toward international supervisory coordination.
2. Growth divergence: The IMF’s latest estimates suggest that AI capital formation is creating materially different growth opportunities across Asian economies.
3. Employment: AI-related displacement may emerge before productivity benefits are broadly distributed, complicating conventional assessments of labour-market slack.
4. Investment strategy: The next phase should increasingly distinguish companies capturing measurable productivity returns from those merely increasing AI expenditure.
5. European competitiveness: The decisive policy objective is to accelerate the conversion of savings, energy and technological adoption into productive investment while preserving financial resilience.

THE FINAL TAKE — THE GREAT SPLIT IS MOVING INTO ECONOMIC POLICY

The new evidence reinforces a fundamental distinction.Economic growth generated by AI investment is not equivalent to productivity growth generated by AI adoption.

The former can support demand, raise financing requirements and concentrate economic benefits.

The latter can expand productive capacity, reduce unit costs and support sustainable increases in real income.

Between them lies a potentially prolonged period of financial, geographical and employment divergence.

For central banks, the challenge is to distinguish these mechanisms without destabilising the transition.

For investors, the challenge is to identify where capital expenditure becomes economic value.

For Europe, the challenge is to secure a larger share of that value before the divergence becomes structural.

The next monetary regime will not be determined by the scale of AI investment alone, but by how quickly that investment translates into productive capacity, financial returns and broadly distributed economic gains.
