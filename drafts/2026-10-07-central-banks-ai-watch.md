A consequential shift has occurred in the central-bank debate.

The AI Supercycle is no longer being treated primarily as a future productivity event. It is increasingly being treated as a present macroeconomic shock — operating simultaneously through demand, inflation, capital formation, energy, financial markets and financial stability.

Three developments over the past 24 hours make the change explicit. The Federal Reserve has inserted AI-related chip scarcity directly into its inflation-persistence test. The Monetary Authority of Singapore has moved AI from principles-based experimentation into formal prudential supervision. And the IMF has now framed the global economy as being hit simultaneously by two opposite but mutually reinforcing shocks: energy as a negative supply shock, AI as a positive demand shock. Both push inflation upward in the near term.

::signals The Policy Shift | three moves in 24 hours

# The Fed: AI as an inflation-persistence test
San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly has gone beyond describing AI as a source of productivity or investment growth. On October 6 she explicitly included AI alongside tariffs and the Middle East energy shock in the set of disturbances determining whether further rate hikes will be necessary. If these shocks behave conventionally — arriving, raising prices temporarily, then dissipating — further tightening may not be required. But if they compound or persist, the Fed may need to tighten again.

Daly specifically identified rising AI demand for semiconductors as a potential source of more persistent inflationary pressure. The reaction function moves from *AI → productivity → disinflation* to *AI → chip scarcity → input-cost inflation → persistence test → monetary response*. If AI demand competes for scarce inputs used by other industries, AI ceases to be a sectoral investment story and becomes part of underlying inflation dynamics.
signal: The central variable is no longer the level shock — it is persistence.

# The IMF codifies the new regime
IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva described the global economy as pulled simultaneously by a negative energy supply shock and a positive, inflationary AI demand shock. After more than five years of above-target inflation, pressure is now being generated by several forces at once: AI investment, energy and food prices, tariffs, defence spending and rising debt-service burdens. She said many economies may now require a "prudently hawkish bias", calling recent Fed, ECB and Bank of Japan tightening appropriate.

The IMF also warned that AI investment as a share of GDP could ultimately exceed previous booms such as railways, electricity grids or telecommunications, while market concentration and high valuations create the possibility that disappointing earnings could generate a broader financial shock.
signal: The AI Supercycle now enters the same framework as energy shocks, fiscal expansion and trade fragmentation.

# MAS: AI moves into the prudential perimeter
On October 7 the Monetary Authority of Singapore issued formal Guidelines on Artificial Intelligence Risk Management covering all financial institutions and all forms of AI technology. Institutions must now manage AI risk at enterprise and application level, with boards defining accountability, risk appetite and governance, maintaining AI inventories and implementing controls across data governance, testing, human oversight, cybersecurity, monitoring and change management.

MAS explicitly recognises agentic AI — systems capable of operating autonomously and accessing external tools — and intends to develop further guidance during 2027. It also closes the third-party-model gap: firms remain accountable for externally built AI, and where assurance is insufficient should consider limiting, suspending or replacing the service. The framework takes effect in phases from October 7, 2027, with later provisions from October 2028.
signal: Outsourcing the model does not outsource the risk.

::section The Financial-Stability Front Is Widening | India joins the signal

The Reserve Bank of India raised its policy rate by 25 basis points to 5.5%, its first increase in almost four years, and shifted toward "calibrated tightening". The immediate drivers were broader inflation pressures, including energy and domestic price dynamics, rather than AI alone.

But RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra also highlighted elevated AI-related asset valuations as a downside risk to the global outlook and identified AI-enabled cyber threats as a growing central-bank concern. Monetary policy and financial stability are converging around the same AI cycle: *AI capex → asset valuations → corporate leverage → credit expansion → systemic concentration → cyber and model dependency → financial-stability risk.*

The Great Split is no longer just between firms that deploy AI and firms that do not. It increasingly separates financial systems capable of absorbing the AI transition from those exposed to its financing, energy and operational externalities.

::patterns The Central-Bank Paradox | three effects, three horizons

# Phase I — Capital Formation
AI investment rises. Compute, semiconductors, data centres, electricity, grids, construction and specialised labour become scarce. Demand increases faster than supply. **Inflationary.**

# Phase II — Financial Transmission
AI investment requires extraordinary financing. Corporate borrowing rises, governments issue more debt and capital becomes scarcer. Long-term yields and hurdle rates rise. **Monetarily restrictive.**

# Phase III — Industrial AI
AI diffuses through production. Automation, optimisation and accelerated computing reduce unit costs and increase output per worker. Supply expands. **Disinflationary.**

::section The Neutral-Rate Implication | the r* question deepens

If AI permanently increases desired investment, raises expected returns on capital and creates decades of infrastructure demand, the equilibrium real interest rate may be structurally higher during the build-out. Later, productivity diffusion may reduce inflation. Both propositions can be true — the mistake would be to confuse them.

The AI Supercycle could therefore produce ==higher r*== and ==lower structural inflation== — but at different points in time. That would mark a profound departure from the low-investment, low-productivity, low-neutral-rate world that dominated monetary policy after the Global Financial Crisis.

::section What It Means For Investors | a second Great Split

The investment regime is becoming increasingly selective. The key distinction is no longer simply AI winner versus AI loser. It is increasingly **AI productivity creator versus AI capital consumer.**

Companies capable of translating AI investment rapidly into productivity, operating leverage and free cash flow can tolerate a higher cost of capital. Companies dependent on perpetual refinancing cannot. The crucial equation becomes *return on AI capital > cost of AI capital*. If that inequality fails, valuation compression follows — a second Great Split, this time inside the AI ecosystem itself.

::section What It Means For Europe | importing the cost before the dividend

Europe faces the most difficult version of this transition. It is exposed to the global rise in energy costs, semiconductor scarcity, long-term yields, AI financing demand and technology dependence — but European AI capital formation remains materially below U.S. levels.

The danger is asymmetric: Europe imports the inflationary and financing costs of the AI Supercycle before it captures an equivalent share of the productivity dividend. That makes four policies increasingly inseparable — **energy policy, capital-markets union, industrial AI policy and monetary policy.** The objective cannot simply be more AI adoption; it must be faster conversion of European savings into European productive capital — compute, grids, storage, industrial automation, AI infrastructure and growth companies. Otherwise Europe risks the monetary cost of the Supercycle without owning enough of the assets that ultimately generate Industrial-AI deflation.

::takeaways Five Takeaways | where the regime now stands

- The Fed has crossed an important threshold: AI-related scarcity is now part of the inflation-persistence debate, not merely the productivity debate.
- The IMF has formalised the two-shock framework: energy is a negative supply shock; AI investment is a positive but inflationary demand shock.
- MAS has moved AI into formal prudential supervision — board accountability, lifecycle controls, third-party responsibility and explicit preparation for agentic AI.
- Financial stability is becoming inseparable from the AI cycle: valuations, leverage, cyber risk and model concentration increasingly interact.
- Industrial-AI deflation remains the destination — but the road to it may require structurally higher capital formation, higher real rates and tighter policy than markets originally assumed.

::close The Great Split is now a race between two clocks: how fast AI can create productivity, and how long economies can finance the build-out without generating too much inflation, leverage and instability. ==Whichever clock moves faster will determine the next monetary regime.==
