THE TWO-SHOCK REGIME HAS ARRIVED

The Big Take — 7 October 2026

A consequential shift has occurred in the central-bank debate.

The AI Supercycle is no longer being treated primarily as a future productivity event. It is increasingly being treated as a present macroeconomic shock — operating simultaneously through demand, inflation, capital formation, energy, financial markets and financial stability.

Three developments over the past 24 hours make the change explicit.

The Federal Reserve has inserted AI-related chip scarcity directly into its inflation-persistence test. The Monetary Authority of Singapore has moved AI from principles-based experimentation into formal prudential supervision. And the IMF has now framed the global economy as being hit simultaneously by two opposite but mutually reinforcing shocks:

ENERGY → NEGATIVE SUPPLY SHOCK

AI → POSITIVE DEMAND SHOCK

Both push inflation upward in the near term.

That is the critical development.

The Great Split is evolving into a monetary-policy regime in which Industrial-AI deflation remains the eventual destination, but AI capital formation is inflationary on the journey there.

⸻

THE FED: AI HAS BECOME AN INFLATION-PERSISTENCE TEST

San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly has now gone beyond describing AI as a source of productivity or investment growth.

On October 6, she explicitly included AI alongside tariffs and the Middle East energy shock in the set of disturbances determining whether further Federal Reserve rate hikes will be necessary.

Her distinction is crucial.

If these shocks behave conventionally — arriving, raising prices temporarily and then dissipating — further tightening may not be required.

But if they compound one another or persist longer than anticipated, the Fed may need to tighten again.

Daly specifically identified rising AI demand for semiconductors as a potential source of more persistent inflationary pressure. 

This changes the reaction function.

The Fed is effectively moving from:

AI → PRODUCTIVITY → DISINFLATION

to:

AI → CHIP SCARCITY → INPUT-COST INFLATION → PERSISTENCE TEST → MONETARY RESPONSE

The distinction between a level shock and a persistent propagation mechanism is what matters.

If AI infrastructure absorbs enough advanced chips, memory, electricity, construction capacity and skilled labour to raise costs elsewhere in the economy, AI ceases to be a sectoral investment story.

It becomes part of underlying inflation dynamics.

The second-order effect

The danger is not simply that AI chips become more expensive.

It is that AI demand competes for scarce productive inputs used by other industries.

That produces:

AI CAPACITY DEMAND
→ SEMICONDUCTOR SCARCITY
→ INPUT SUBSTITUTION
→ HIGHER PRODUCTION COSTS
→ BROADER GOODS INFLATION

At that point, the Federal Reserve cannot safely look through the shock.

The central variable becomes persistence.

⸻

THE IMF CODIFIES THE NEW REGIME

The broader monetary framework became even clearer today.

IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva described the global economy as being pulled simultaneously by a negative energy supply shock and a positive AI demand shock that is itself inflationary.

She explicitly argued that, after more than five years of above-target inflation, inflationary pressure is now being generated by several forces at once: AI investment, energy and food prices, tariffs, defence spending and rising debt-service burdens. She said many economies may now require a “prudently hawkish bias”, while describing recent tightening by the Federal Reserve, ECB and Bank of Japan as appropriate. 

This is a major conceptual step.

The AI Supercycle is now entering the same macroeconomic framework as energy shocks, fiscal expansion and trade fragmentation.

The new equation becomes:

**ENERGY SHOCK

• AI CAPITAL FORMATION
• DEFENCE CAPEX
• FISCAL BORROWING
    = STRUCTURALLY STRONGER DEMAND FOR CAPITAL AND REAL RESOURCES**

That combination can keep real rates and long-term yields higher even before central banks change policy rates.The IMF also warned that AI investment as a share of GDP could ultimately exceed previous infrastructure booms such as railways, electricity grids or telecommunications, while market concentration and high valuations create the possibility that disappointing earnings could generate a broader financial shock. 

The AI Supercycle therefore contains both sides of the financial cycle:

BOOM:
investment → demand → employment → inflation → higher yields

CORRECTION:
earnings disappointment → valuation compression → credit repricing → tighter financial conditions

Central banks must increasingly manage both.

⸻

MAS: AI MOVES INTO THE PRUDENTIAL PERIMETER

The most important regulatory development today comes from Singapore.

On October 7, the Monetary Authority of Singapore issued formal Guidelines on Artificial Intelligence Risk Management covering all financial institutions and all forms of AI technology.

The framework moves substantially beyond voluntary AI principles.

Financial institutions will now be expected to manage AI risk both at the enterprise level and at the level of individual applications. Boards and senior management must define accountability, risk appetite and governance. Institutions must maintain AI inventories, assess materiality and implement controls covering data governance, testing, human oversight, cybersecurity, monitoring and change management. 

The particularly important element is agentic AI.

MAS explicitly recognises that AI systems are becoming capable of operating autonomously and accessing external tools, and says it intends to develop further guidance on agentic AI during 2027. 

It also closes one of the most important emerging supervisory gaps: third-party models.

Financial institutions remain accountable for AI used in their services even when the technology is developed or operated by external providers. Where assurance is insufficient and risks cannot be brought within the institution’s risk appetite, MAS says firms should consider limiting, suspending or replacing the service. 

The framework becomes effective in phases beginning October 7, 2027, with later provisions applying from October 2028. 

This is consequential because it establishes a regulatory principle likely to spread:

OUTSOURCING THE MODEL DOES NOT OUTSOURCE THE RISK.

⸻

THE FINANCIAL-STABILITY FRONT IS WIDENING

India added another important signal today.

The Reserve Bank of India raised its policy rate by 25 basis points to 5.5%, its first increase in almost four years, and shifted its stance toward “calibrated tightening.” The immediate drivers were broader inflation pressures, including energy and domestic price dynamics, rather than AI alone. 

But alongside the monetary decision, RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra also highlighted elevated AI-related asset valuations as a downside risk to the global outlook and identified AI-enabled cyber threats as a growing central-bank concern. 

This matters because monetary policy and financial stability are converging around the same AI cycle.

The emerging transmission chain is:

AI CAPEX
→ ASSET VALUATIONS
→ CORPORATE LEVERAGE
→ CREDIT EXPANSION
→ SYSTEMIC CONCENTRATION
→ CYBER AND MODEL DEPENDENCY
→ FINANCIAL-STABILITY RISK

The Great Split is therefore no longer just between firms that successfully deploy AI and firms that do not.

It increasingly separates financial systems capable of absorbing the AI transition from those exposed to its financing, energy and operational externalities.

⸻

THE CENTRAL-BANK PARADOX

The central-bank problem can now be stated more precisely.

AI produces three different effects on three different time horizons.

Phase I — Capital Formation

AI investment rises.

Compute, semiconductors, data centres, electricity, grids, construction and specialised labour become scarce.

Demand increases faster than supply.

Inflationary.

Phase II — Financial Transmission

AI investment requires extraordinary financing.

Corporate borrowing rises, governments simultaneously issue more debt and capital becomes scarcer.Long-term yields and hurdle rates rise.

Monetarily restrictive.

Phase III — Industrial AI

AI diffuses through production.

Automation, optimisation and accelerated computing reduce unit costs and increase output per worker.

Supply expands.

Disinflationary.

The central-bank dilemma is that these phases do not occur simultaneously.

Central banks must manage Phase I and Phase II before they can rely on Phase III.

⸻

THE NEUTRAL-RATE IMPLICATION

This also deepens the emerging question around r*.

If AI permanently increases desired investment, raises expected returns on capital and creates decades of infrastructure demand, the equilibrium real interest rate may be structurally higher during the build-out.

Later, productivity diffusion may reduce inflation.

Both propositions can be true.

The mistake would be to confuse them.

The AI Supercycle could therefore produce:

HIGHER r*
and
LOWER STRUCTURAL INFLATION

but at different points in time.

That would mark a profound departure from the low-investment, low-productivity, low-neutral-rate world that dominated monetary policy after the Global Financial Crisis.

⸻

WHAT IT MEANS FOR INVESTORS

The investment regime is becoming increasingly selective.

The key distinction is no longer simply:

AI winner versus AI loser.

It is increasingly:

AI PRODUCTIVITY CREATOR
versus
AI CAPITAL CONSUMER.

Companies capable of translating AI investment rapidly into productivity, operating leverage and free cash flow can tolerate a higher cost of capital.

Companies dependent on perpetual refinancing cannot.

The crucial investor equation becomes:

RETURN ON AI CAPITAL > COST OF AI CAPITAL

If that inequality fails, valuation compression follows.

That creates a second Great Split — this time inside the AI ecosystem itself.

⸻

WHAT IT MEANS FOR EUROPE

Europe faces the most difficult version of this transition.

It is exposed to the global rise in:

• energy costs,
• semiconductor scarcity,
• long-term yields,
• AI financing demand,
• and technology dependence.

But European AI capital formation remains materially below U.S. levels.

The danger is therefore asymmetric:

EUROPE IMPORTS THE INFLATIONARY AND FINANCING COSTS OF THE AI SUPERCYCLE BEFORE IT CAPTURES AN EQUIVALENT SHARE OF THE PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND.

That makes four policies increasingly inseparable:

**ENERGY POLICY

• CAPITAL-MARKETS UNION
• INDUSTRIAL AI POLICY
• MONETARY POLICY**

For EU policymakers, the objective cannot simply be more AI adoption.

It must be faster conversion of European savings into European productive capital — compute, grids, storage, industrial automation, AI infrastructure and growth companies.

Otherwise Europe risks experiencing the monetary cost of the Supercycle without owning enough of the productive assets that ultimately generate Industrial-AI deflation.

⸻

FIVE TAKEAWAYS

1. The Fed has crossed an important threshold: AI-related scarcity is now part of the inflation-persistence debate, not merely the productivity debate. 
2. The IMF has formalised the two-shock framework: energy is a negative supply shock; AI investment is a positive but inflationary demand shock. 
3. MAS has moved AI into formal prudential supervision, including board accountability, lifecycle controls, third-party responsibility and explicit preparation for agentic AI. 
4. Financial stability is becoming inseparable from the AI cycle: valuations, leverage, cyber risk and model concentration increasingly interact.
5. Industrial-AI deflation remains the destination — but central banks are discovering that the road to it may require structurally higher capital formation, higher real rates and tighter policy than markets originally assumed.

THE GREAT SPLIT IS NOW A RACE BETWEEN TWO CLOCKS

THE FIRST CLOCK MEASURES HOW FAST AI CAN CREATE PRODUCTIVITY.

THE SECOND MEASURES HOW LONG ECONOMIES CAN FINANCE THE BUILD-OUT WITHOUT GENERATING TOO MUCH INFLATION, LEVERAGE AND FINANCIAL INSTABILITY.

Whichever clock moves faster will determine the next monetary regime.
