Jensen Huang’s departure transforms Nvidia’s governance crisis into a geopolitical and financial shock. New evidence reveals China’s continuing dependence on American computing—and the potential beneficiaries of a more restrictive AI order.
MATERIAL INFLECTION — VERY HIGH SIGNIFICANCE
THE BIG TAKE
A decisive inflection has occurred in the U.S.–China AI race. For the first time, the leadership crisis at the company dominating advanced computing is converging with a significant shift in American semiconductor policy expectations and an observable repricing of Chinese AI assets.
On November 12, Nvidia confirmed that Jensen Huang had resigned as chief executive and director. CFO Colette Kress became interim CEO, while Harvey Jones assumed the newly established board chairmanship. Nvidia says its product roadmap and customer commitments remain intact. NVIDIA Newsroom +1
Today, November 13, three developments make the consequences substantially more important.
First, Reuters identified more than $3.2 billion of Chinese AI-computing projects built around restricted Nvidia processors, after reviewing more than 150 procurement notices, investment documents and corporate disclosures. The amount includes supporting infrastructure and projects at different stages, not simply chip purchases or completed investments. Reuters
Second, bipartisan support for tighter semiconductor restrictions is gaining momentum in Washington. Congressional Republicans are considering attaching additional chip controls to the annual defense authorization legislation. No new restrictions have yet been enacted. Reuters
Third, financial markets are beginning to discriminate sharply between Chinese AI-model developers dependent on American computing and domestic semiconductor manufacturers that could benefit from technological substitution.

The hidden pattern: America controls the most strategically valuable computing chokepoint, while China’s diffusion strategy remains more dependent on that chokepoint than headline narratives about technological independence suggest.
1. THE NVIDIA INFLECTION — FROM KEY-PERSON RISK TO SYSTEMIC TECHNOLOGY RISK
Huang’s resignation removes the executive who helped build Nvidia’s technological ecosystem and advocated retaining commercial access to China.
The immediate risk is not that Nvidia’s accelerated-computing technology suddenly becomes uncompetitive.
It is that leadership succession, corporate governance scrutiny and export-policy uncertainty converge at a company central to global AI investment.
The allegations involving Alice Huang, a former Megaspeed executive suspected by U.S. authorities of Chinese intelligence connections, remain unproven. The U.S. Justice Department has not reached a conclusion concerning her alleged intelligence role. Nvidia has said it has no reason to believe its secrets or U.S. national security were compromised. Reuters +1
But the political consequences are becoming measurable.
House Speaker Mike Johnson has publicly endorsed stronger restrictions on China’s access to advanced chips. Meanwhile, the future of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is reportedly under discussion inside the White House, although President Trump has publicly expressed confidence in him. Reuters +1
The fundamental market risk is a transition from selective semiconductor controls to a broader regulatory regime governing hardware ownership, offshore computing and ultimate access to advanced intelligence.
2. THE CHINESE COMPUTING CONTRADICTION — $3.2 BILLION OF EXPOSED INVESTMENT
The new Reuters investigation provides the clearest evidence of the scale of China’s unresolved dependence on American advanced processors.
Projects across China incorporate or anticipate Nvidia H100, H200 and H800 accelerators.Some facilities are operating, others are under construction and still others remain planned.
The investigation does not establish that all restricted chips have been delivered or identify how every processor reached its intended user.
The findings nonetheless expose substantial potential costs if American restrictions tighten.
Projects designed around Nvidia hardware could face delayed commissioning, more expensive computing procurement or redesign around Chinese processors. Software portability, interconnection performance and developer ecosystems could complicate substitution.
China’s technological sovereignty strategy is advancing, but the transition cost is becoming more visible.
The distinction matters.
China can possess increasingly competitive AI models without yet possessing a completely independent industrial system for training and deploying them.
3. THE MARKET ROTATION — CHINA’S AI ECONOMY BEGINS TO SPLIT
The latest equity reactions are especially revealing.
According to the Financial Times, Alibaba and Tencent have lost a combined approximately $75 billion in market capitalization this week. Meanwhile, Chinese accelerator manufacturer Cambricon has advanced more than 20%, reflecting expectations of additional domestic semiconductor demand. ft.com
The resulting differentiation is strategically important.
Chinese model developers face computing-access risk. Domestic chip manufacturers may benefit from substitution demand.
This is not a straightforward victory for domestic semiconductor suppliers.
Replacing advanced Nvidia systems could involve lower performance, higher electricity consumption, more software-development work and costly manufacturing constraints.
But geopolitical restrictions can still redirect capital and procurement toward domestic companies.
The possible second-order effect is a new capital-allocation cycle:
Tighter American computing controls
Higher scarcity and replacement costs for Chinese AI developers
Accelerated Chinese investment in domestic accelerators and software
Greater competition among domestic hardware suppliers
Potential long-term reduction in dependence on U.S. infrastructure
Importantly, this is a potential evolutionary mechanism, not an established outcome. Industrial substitution will succeed only where Chinese alternatives become technologically and economically viable.
4. THE SEMICONDUCTOR COUNTERPOINT — SCARCITY COULD PERSIST THROUGH 2027
China’s largest contract chipmaker, SMIC, said on November 13 that AI-related semiconductor supply chains could remain constrained through 2027.
The company also highlighted equipment delivery delays exceeding 24 months for some orders and an expected increase of approximately 30% in depreciation expenses during 2026. Reuters
This introduces an important qualification to China’s opportunity.
Domestic semiconductor demand may rise sharply, but manufacturing capacity cannot necessarily expand at the same speed.
The result could be a period in which Chinese chip suppliers enjoy stronger demand while AI developers face higher system costs and constrained availability.
That would temporarily weaken the economics of China’s AI diffusion strategy, even as it strengthens the long-term rationale for semiconductor independence.
5. THE INVESTMENT VERDICT — THE NEXT AI ROTATION
The November 13 developments strengthen our thesis, but also refine it.
America continues to lead in important areas of frontier intelligence and accelerated computing.
China has demonstrated growing capabilities in inexpensive models and widespread commercial adoption.
Yet today’s evidence exposes a critical asymmetry: model efficiency does not eliminate dependence on advanced computing infrastructure.

No newly verified November 13 DeepSeek or other Chinese frontier-model release demonstrates a reversal of American frontier leadership. The decisive development is institutional and financial rather than a new model benchmark.
THE FINAL TAKE — THE NEW PRICE OF INTELLIGENCE
The U.S.–China AI competition is entering a more consequential stage.
The technological frontier remains predominantly American-led. But the economics of deploying intelligence increasingly depend on international access to computing, industrial capacity and politically acceptable supply chains.
China’s challenge is no longer simply to manufacture competitive models at lower prices.
It must demonstrate that its increasingly competitive intelligence ecosystem can operate at scale without depending on technologies Washington may progressively restrict.
America faces a different challenge.
It must protect its technological advantage without imposing excessive costs on the global industrial and financial ecosystem supporting that advantage.
For The Velocity Edge, the most important implication is the emergence of a new distinction between technological capability, control over computing infrastructure and the financial returns generated by AI deployment.
The next market rotation may increasingly favor businesses possessing scarce and defensible computing assets, companies capable of delivering materially lower system-level costs, and enterprises converting intelligence into measurable productivity rather than merely accumulating computing capacity.
The AI Supercycle is no longer simply repricing intelligence. It is repricing the right to produce, access and economically exploit intelligence.
America leads the frontier. China challenges the economics of its diffusion.
The decisive competitive advantage will belong to the system that can secure computing sovereignty without sacrificing capital productivity.
