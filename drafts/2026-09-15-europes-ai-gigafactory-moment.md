Why the ACS–Telefónica–Santander alliance signals that Europe may finally be moving from AI policy to AI-scale capital formation
August 21, 2026 | FDK
Europe’s AI debate is entering a different phase.
For much of the past three years, the central question was whether Europe could regulate artificial intelligence without regulating itself out of the race. Then came a second question: whether Europe possessed enough capital, energy and technological infrastructure to convert its formidable industrial base into an AI-native economy.
The news emerging from Spain this week suggests that a third—and more consequential—phase may now be beginning.
The European Commission has cleared the joint venture bringing together ACS, Telefónica, Banco Santander and Spain’s public technology investment vehicle behind Spain’s candidacy for a European AI gigafactory. The project envisages approximately €5 billion of investment, with the private sector controlling 52% of the vehicle and the Spanish government committing around €719 million. The proposed architecture spans Móra la Nova in Tarragona, with an additional technical site in San Fernando de Henares, Madrid. (El País⁠￼)
Seen in isolation, this is a large infrastructure project.
Seen through the lens of the AI Supercycle, it is something much more important.
It may be evidence that Europe is beginning to understand the fundamental economic law of the Next Economy:
Artificial intelligence cannot be scaled through software alone. Intelligence has become an infrastructure industry.
And infrastructure requires capital, electricity, networks, land, semiconductors, financing, construction capabilities and long-duration institutional commitment.
The European AI race is therefore beginning to migrate from declarations toward physical capital formation.
That distinction changes everything.
⸻
I. THE CHANGE IN PACE
The European Commission formally launched its tender on July 30 for up to seven AI Gigafactories. These facilities will be substantially larger than Europe’s existing AI Factories and are expected to incorporate massive clusters of advanced processors together with networking, software, cloud infrastructure and highly energy-efficient data centres. The Commission expects up to €10 billion of EU and national public funding to mobilise at least €20 billion of private investment—more than €30 billion overall. (Digital Strategy EU⁠￼)
That number needs to be read alongside the broader InvestAI ambition: €200 billion of AI-related investment, including the original €20 billion facility announced for AI gigafactories. (European Commission⁠￼)
But capital alone is not the key development.
What is changing is who is assembling the capital.
Spain’s consortium is particularly revealing because its participants correspond almost perfectly to the new industrial architecture of AI:
ACS provides infrastructure. Telefónica provides connectivity and digital platforms. Santander provides financial depth and capital-market capabilities. The state provides strategic coordination and de-risking. Technology specialists provide the intelligence layer.
This is a very different model from the European technology policy of the previous decade.
Europe is beginning to assemble industrial coalitions, not merely innovation programs.
And this is precisely how AI infrastructure will have to be built.
⸻
II. WHY IT MATTERS
The AI Supercycle is forcing the global economy through one of the largest capital reallocations since electrification.
AI models may be digital. Their industrialisation is not.
Every additional layer of intelligence requires physical systems:
accelerated computing,
electricity generation,
grids,
transformers,
cooling,
fibre,
memory,
networking,
land,
data centres,
and enormous quantities of financial capital.
The frontier is therefore moving away from the old assumption that technological leadership could be measured mainly through software companies and venture-capital ecosystems.The emerging strategic unit is the AI industrial system.
That is why the European Commission now describes AI infrastructure as a prerequisite for European productivity and technological autonomy. Gigafactories are expected to deploy processing capacity several times larger than Europe’s existing AI factories and support training, fine-tuning and inference for advanced models and industrial applications. (Digital Strategy EU⁠￼)
The underlying causality is increasingly clear:
AI demand → accelerated computing → electricity demand → grid investment → data-centre construction → fibre and connectivity → semiconductor demand → industrial AI deployment → productivity → higher returns on capital → further AI investment.
It is a recursive capital cycle.
And countries able to compress this chain will acquire an increasingly powerful Velocity Advantage.
⸻
III. WHY THIS TIME IS DIFFERENT
There is an important distinction between the Internet build-out of the late 1990s and the AI infrastructure boom of the 2020s.
The Internet primarily connected people and information.
AI increasingly connects intelligence to production.
That means AI infrastructure will progressively become embedded inside banking, pharmaceuticals, defence, logistics, automotive manufacturing, energy systems, railways, robotics and public administration.
The resulting demand therefore has a potentially much broader industrial base.
This also explains why the Spanish consortium matters symbolically.
A telecommunications company alone could not build the European AI economy.
Neither could a bank.
Neither could a construction company.
Neither could government.
But together they begin to form something much closer to the institutional structure required by the AI Supercycle.
It is the convergence of:
technology × infrastructure × energy × finance × sovereign capital.
That convergence may become one of the defining investment themes of Europe during the next decade.
⸻
IV. EUROPE IS MOVING—BUT THE RACE IS UNEVEN
Europe should not mistake acceleration for leadership.
The United States remains dramatically ahead in hyperscale AI investment.
Global spending by the largest technology platforms continues to reach extraordinary levels. Reuters estimates that spending by Google, Microsoft, Meta and other major hyperscale AI players could reach roughly $725 billion during 2026, while AI workloads are expected to consume around half of global data-centre capacity by 2030. (Reuters⁠￼)
Europe is therefore entering a race whose scale has already changed.
But Europe possesses assets that are becoming increasingly valuable as AI moves into the physical world:
industrial engineering,
electricity infrastructure,
specialised manufacturing,
telecommunications networks,
financial institutions,
large industrial companies,
and sophisticated capital markets.
The question is whether these assets can be recombined rapidly enough.
The European problem has rarely been the absence of assets.
It has been the velocity of their combination.
⸻
V. THE NEW EUROPEAN AI INFRASTRUCTURE MAP
Several countries are beginning to emerge as probable leaders.
France — The Energy Advantage
France may currently possess Europe’s strongest structural position.
Its nuclear fleet gives the country something extraordinarily valuable in the AI age: large-scale, relatively low-carbon and potentially competitively priced electricity.
France exported roughly 92 TWh of electricity in 2025, and its power advantage is attracting increasingly large data-centre commitments. At the 2026 Choose France summit, SoftBank outlined plans potentially reaching €75 billion, while Data4 expanded its investment ambitions to approximately €30 billion. (Reuters⁠￼)
France has also assembled its own AI-gigafactory coalition.
The AION consortium brings together Ardian, Artefact, Bull, Capgemini, EDF, iliad, Orange and Scaleway—linking capital, energy, telecommunications, cloud, hardware and industrial technology. (Newsroom Groupe Orange⁠￼)
France increasingly understands the equation:Cheap scalable energy + capital + sovereign cloud + infrastructure = AI industrial capacity.
⸻
Germany — The Industrial AI Advantage
Germany’s opportunity is different.
Its strategic asset is not merely digital infrastructure.
It is Europe’s largest concentration of advanced industrial demand.
Deutsche Telekom and NVIDIA have already launched a major Industrial AI Cloud in Munich, supported by an ecosystem including Siemens, SAP, Agile Robots, PhysicsX and other industrial players.
The infrastructure includes as many as 10,000 NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs, and Deutsche Telekom has said the initiative increases German AI processing capacity by approximately 50%. (Telekom⁠￼)
Germany therefore has the possibility of transforming AI infrastructure into something even more consequential:
the operating layer of European manufacturing.
If Germany successfully connects AI factories to machinery, automotive systems, robotics and industrial automation, its advantage will lie not in producing another consumer chatbot but in scaling Industrial AI.
⸻
Spain — The Infrastructure–Finance Convergence
Spain is emerging for different reasons.
Renewable-energy availability, land, telecommunications infrastructure, global construction capabilities and sophisticated banks create an unusually complementary ecosystem.
The ACS–Telefónica–Santander consortium therefore deserves close attention.
It combines three institutions whose competencies sit directly inside the emerging AI capital stack.
Spain’s planned gigafactory could mobilise around €5 billion and place the country inside the first generation of European sovereign AI infrastructure. (El País⁠￼)
More importantly, Spain could become one of the places where Europe demonstrates that AI infrastructure is not simply a technology-sector investment.
It is a national competitiveness platform.
⸻
The Nordics — The Energy and Cooling Advantage
Finland, Sweden and the broader Nordic region possess another potentially formidable combination:
renewable electricity,
cooler climates,
strong grids,
political stability,
engineering expertise,
and digitally sophisticated economies.
As AI infrastructure becomes increasingly constrained by energy economics, these characteristics will matter more.
The geography of European AI may therefore progressively shift away from traditional metropolitan hubs.
Reuters finds that new hyperscale facilities planned for 2026–2028 are being located on average around 175 kilometres from major urban centres, compared with only 46 kilometres during 2022–2025. (Reuters⁠￼)
This is an extraordinary shift.
AI is beginning to redraw economic geography.
⸻
Italy — The Industrial Opportunity, and the Risk of Delay
Italy possesses many of the ingredients required to participate meaningfully:
a large industrial base,
advanced manufacturing clusters,
energy infrastructure,
major utilities,
telecommunications networks,
banks,
engineering companies,
and strategic geographic positioning.
But Italy faces a familiar danger.
Its problem is not the absence of assets.
It is fragmentation.
The European AI gigafactory race rewards countries capable of assembling energy companies, banks, telecommunications operators, industrial groups, infrastructure companies and government around a common investment architecture.
If Italy approaches AI primarily as another digital-policy program, it risks missing the deeper capital transition underway.
Europe’s next industrial hierarchy may be determined by who builds infrastructure now, not by who writes the most ambitious strategy documents.
⸻
VI. THE HIDDEN PATTERN: FROM DATA CENTRES TO AI INDUSTRIAL CORRIDORS
The biggest mistake would be to interpret this race as simply a competition to build bigger data centres.
Something more profound may be emerging.
Europe could be witnessing the formation of AI Industrial Corridors.
Consider the logic.
A gigafactory creates concentrated demand for:
electricity,
transmission infrastructure,
fibre,
advanced semiconductors,
cooling systems,
engineering services,
cybersecurity,
cloud platforms,
and specialised labour.Companies then begin clustering around that capacity.
Research centres follow.
Industrial applications develop.
Capital follows those applications.
New companies emerge.
Productivity increases.
And eventually the infrastructure begins to shape the geography of investment itself.
The chain becomes recursive:
AI infrastructure → industrial AI → productivity → capital attraction → ecosystem formation → talent concentration → innovation → additional AI infrastructure.
This is how Velocity compounds.
⸻
VII. WHAT COMES NEXT
The next phase of the European race will increasingly revolve around five scarce resources.
First: electricity. AI infrastructure is turning access to scalable electricity into an industrial competitive advantage.
Second: grid connectivity. Generating electricity is insufficient if new facilities cannot obtain rapid grid connections.
Third: capital. Gigafactories are multi-billion-euro infrastructure assets requiring increasingly sophisticated financing structures.
Fourth: accelerated computing. Access to advanced processors, memory, networking and software ecosystems remains a strategic bottleneck.
Fifth: execution speed. Perhaps the most underrated variable of all.
The formal EuroHPC tender closes on November 12, 2026, with construction of the first selected facilities expected to begin in 2027. (EuroHPC⁠￼)
Between now and then, European countries will not simply be competing for gigafactories.
They will be revealing their ability—or inability—to mobilise complex industrial systems quickly.
⸻
VIII. CONCLUSION — EUROPE’S CAPITAL AWAKENING
For years, the European AI conversation focused excessively on regulation, start-ups and whether Europe could reproduce Silicon Valley.
That may have been the wrong framing.
Europe does not need to become California.
It needs to transform its own structural advantages into the architecture of the Next Economy.
Europe has electricity systems.
It has utilities.
It has world-leading industrial companies.
It has telecommunications networks.
It has engineering expertise.
It has banks.
It has infrastructure investors.
It has pension capital.
And it possesses one of the largest industrial markets on earth.
The strategic challenge is to connect them.
That is what makes the ACS–Telefónica–Santander development important.
It is not yet proof that Europe has closed the AI gap.
Far from it.
But it may represent something that Europe has lacked for too long:
the beginning of coordinated, large-scale AI capital formation.
And if this model spreads—from Spain to France, Germany, Italy and the Nordics—the European AI story could begin changing rapidly.
The defining question would then no longer be:
Can Europe compete in artificial intelligence?
It would become:
How rapidly can Europe convert its industrial, financial and energy base into intelligence infrastructure?
Because in the Next Economy, competitive advantage will increasingly belong not simply to those who invent intelligence.
It will belong to those who can industrialise it, finance it and deploy it at scale.
That is the race now beginning.
⸻
KEY TAKEAWAYS
For Investors
AI gigafactories should increasingly be understood as an infrastructure asset class, not merely a technology investment.
Watch the second-order beneficiaries: electricity generation, grids, transformers, cooling, fibre, networking, engineering and construction.
The geography of European returns may increasingly follow energy availability and infrastructure velocity rather than traditional technology clusters.
France, Germany and Spain are emerging through different comparative advantages; the Nordics could gain considerably as energy constraints intensify.
Expect increasing convergence between infrastructure funds, sovereign capital, banks and technology companies.
For CEOs and Boards
AI strategy can no longer be delegated entirely to the CIO.
Access to intelligence infrastructure is becoming an issue of corporate strategy, capital allocation and supply-chain resilience.Industrial companies should secure long-duration relationships with AI infrastructure providers before capacity becomes increasingly scarce.
Boards should begin measuring exposure to electricity, accelerated computing, data and AI infrastructure as strategic inputs.
The competitive gap between AI-native and AI-lagging industrial companies is likely to widen.
For European Policymakers
Europe must accelerate permitting, grid connections and infrastructure approvals.
Public capital should primarily de-risk and crowd in private capital, rather than substitute for it.
Sovereignty will come from industrial capability, not isolation.
Europe needs interconnected AI infrastructure corridors rather than fragmented national technology programs.
Gigafactories should ultimately serve the transformation of European industry—not become isolated computing islands.
For National Governments
AI infrastructure policy should sit alongside energy, industrial, financial and infrastructure policy.
Governments should identify national coalitions capable of combining energy + telecommunications + finance + construction + technology.
Regional development strategies should increasingly be connected to AI infrastructure geography.
Countries that cannot mobilise such ecosystems rapidly risk becoming consumers of intelligence generated elsewhere.
The next twelve to twenty-four months may determine much of Europe’s AI infrastructure map for the next decade.
THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
The first phase of the AI race was about models. The second was about chips. The third is about infrastructure. The fourth will be about who converts that infrastructure into industrial productivity fastest.
Europe may finally be entering Phase Three.
The decisive variable now is Velocity.
The strongest investment implication is that the emerging European contest is no longer simply “who gets a gigafactory?” It is becoming “which countries can assemble the cheapest, fastest and most financeable intelligence-production system?” That framing gives us an excellent basis for the next visual: a European map with France, Germany, Spain, Nordics and Italy positioned by Energy × Capital × Infrastructure × Industrial AI × Velocity.
