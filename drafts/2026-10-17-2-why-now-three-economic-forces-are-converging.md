The Apple–Meta collaboration comes at an unusually important moment.
First, AI infrastructure financing is becoming more expensive. The current market correction is exposing the vulnerability of capital-intensive AI companies whose future revenues must support substantial borrowing commitments.
Second, advanced models are becoming more efficient to deploy. Mixture-of-experts architectures, model compression, quantization and optimized inference software allow increasingly sophisticated models to operate with less active computation than their headline parameter counts suggest.
Third, agentic AI is changing the economics of usage. A conventional chatbot may answer a few questions. An autonomous coding or research agent can perform hundreds of model calls, read documents, invoke tools and execute extended workflows.
As agentic usage expands, the relevant cost is no longer simply the price of an individual model response.
It is the total cost of intelligence consumed across an entire workflow.
That creates a strong incentive to execute suitable tasks locally or within privately controlled infrastructure.
Reuters reported on September 22 that Apple was already pitching its new Macs against rented data-centre capacity, while Microsoft and Nvidia were preparing competing local-AI systems.
The economic mechanism is straightforward:
More agents → more inference → greater sensitivity to token costs → demand for local capacity → new competition over computing architecture.
This is why the timing matters.
Apple is not merely adding another model to its devices. It is positioning its hardware and software architecture to participate in a potentially fundamental redistribution of inference spending.
3 | Why this time is different: intelligence becomes an operating environment
Previous computing revolutions moved through identifiable transitions: mainframes to personal computers, desktop software to the internet, and local applications to cloud services.
The emerging AI transition could differ in one decisive respect.
The computer is evolving from a device that executes user commands into a system that can increasingly execute user intentions.
Agentic AI makes this possible by combining reasoning, memory, tools, software execution and persistent workflows.
The competitive implications are profound.
In conventional computing, the user selects applications and operates them.
In agentic computing, the user may increasingly delegate objectives to software that selects applications, coordinates services and completes tasks.
That changes the strategic role of the operating system.
The next platform controller may be the company determining which agents receive permissions, which models execute tasks, how personal and corporate data are accessed, and where computation occurs.
This creates a new category of competition:
AI-native pervasive computing
An environment in which intelligence is distributed across personal devices, enterprise systems, connected objects and cloud infrastructure, with workloads allocated according to cost, latency, privacy and performance.
The likely architecture is hybrid, not exclusively local.

4 | The Apple–Meta bargain: complementary assets, conflicting ambitions
The alliance is strategically rational precisely because the two companies possess different assets.
Apple controls the hardware environment. Its advantages include silicon design, unified memory, operating systems, developer tools, device distribution and integration between hardware and software.
Meta controls a model-development and distribution ecosystem.It brings frontier-model research, open-weight distribution, social platforms and growing ambitions in wearable computing.
Apple benefits from demonstrating that sophisticated external models can operate on its hardware without requiring Apple to develop every frontier model itself.
Meta benefits from reaching developers and enterprise workloads beyond its own consumer applications.
But the relationship also contains a contradiction.
Apple wants to preserve control over the user experience.
Meta wants its intelligence to become broadly available across platforms and devices.
Their cooperation is therefore best understood as competitive complementarity, not permanent strategic alignment.
The two companies can cooperate in model deployment while continuing to compete over the future interface to personal computing.
That distinction will become particularly important as smartphones, smart glasses, home systems and autonomous agents converge.

Microsoft is particularly exposed to the strategic challenge. Reuters cited IDC estimates placing Windows at approximately 91.3% of enterprise desktops, against Apple’s 4.6%. This gives Microsoft an enormous installed-base advantage—but also a powerful incentive to prevent a new computing architecture from weakening Windows’ central role.
Google has a different advantage: it can coordinate AI models, mobile operating systems, browsers, custom chips and cloud infrastructure.
Nvidia faces a more nuanced outcome. Local AI can expand the total market for accelerated computing even if it changes where some inference workloads run. Its data-centre franchise remains strategically important.
The greatest competitive pressure may fall on business models relying on persistent, high-margin access to remotely hosted intelligence without sufficient differentiation in model quality, reliability, workflow integration or proprietary data.
6 | The hidden pattern: the next Great Split moves to inference economics
The emerging competitive shift is not simply from cloud AI to local AI.
It is from centralized intelligence delivery toward economically optimized intelligence deployment.
That produces four second-order effects.
First — the inference cost curve becomes a competitive weapon. Companies that reduce the lifetime cost of AI workflows may gain market share even without possessing the single most capable model.
Second — memory architecture becomes strategically more valuable. Large locally deployed models place a premium on memory capacity, bandwidth, interconnects and power efficiency, not only raw accelerator performance.
Third — enterprise sovereignty acquires commercial value. Companies may pay for greater control over sensitive data, model customization, operational continuity and regulatory compliance.
Fourth — the interface becomes the strategic control point.As agents increasingly coordinate applications and services, the operating system and trusted orchestration layer may capture more bargaining power.
This creates a new economic transmission mechanism:
More efficient models → lower inference costs → wider agent deployment → more workflows automated → greater demand for computing and orchestration.
Lower cost per task can increase total AI usage. It does not necessarily reduce aggregate demand for cloud computing.
The result could be a larger AI market with a very different distribution of profits.
7 | What’s next: three scenarios through 2027–2028
01
Base case
Hybrid AI becomes the standard
Enterprises distribute workloads among local machines, private infrastructure and cloud providers. Model routing, security and orchestration become major sources of software value.
Trigger: measurable cost savings and reliable integration with enterprise workflows.
02
Upside case
Local agentic computing accelerates
Model efficiency, memory advances and software optimization bring sophisticated agents to smaller devices. Personal computing expands into a persistent, multimodal intelligence environment.
Trigger: substantial improvements in cost per completed task, battery efficiency and agent reliability.
03
Risk case
Cloud dominance remains stronger than expected
Hardware costs, maintenance, security concerns and rapid model obsolescence limit local deployment to specialized workloads. Cloud providers retain superior economics for many applications.
Trigger: weak local utilization, poor total cost of ownership or inadequate agent performance.
The first scenario appears the most plausible based on current evidence.
The second could create the greatest disruption to existing platform economics.
The third remains a material risk, particularly because a demonstration of local technical feasibility is not equivalent to proof of commercial viability at scale.
8 | The investor hierarchy
The Apple–Meta development should not be interpreted as a simple buy signal for either company’s shares.
Its importance is that it identifies the assets whose economic relevance could increase as inference becomes more distributed.
Priority Strategic exposure Investment thesis
I Efficient silicon, unified memory and advanced interconnects Lower cost of local and hybrid inference
II Operating systems and trusted agent orchestration Control over distribution and workflows
III Enterprise security, identity and data governance Safe deployment of autonomous agents
IV Open-weight model ecosystems and inference optimization Lower switching costs and greater deployment choice
V Industrial AI, robotics and intelligent mobility Conversion of intelligence into measurable productivity
The most vulnerable business models are those dependent on high per-token margins without durable differentiation, proprietary distribution or defensible enterprise integration.
Five key takeaways for investors
1. Follow inference economics, not parameter counts. The relevant metric is cost per successfully completed task, adjusted for latency, reliability and utilization.
2. Revalue memory and system architecture. AI performance increasingly depends on how processors, memory and interconnects work together.
3. Watch operating-system control. Apple, Microsoft and Google may capture value through agent distribution even when models become more interchangeable.
4. Distinguish cloud displacement from market expansion. Local inference could reduce some cloud spending while stimulating much greater total AI usage.
5. Invest in economic capture. The strongest opportunities will belong to businesses converting lower intelligence costs into recurring revenue, productivity and free cash flow.
Final Closing — The Next Computing Regime
The Apple–Meta alliance is not the end of the cloud.
It may mark the beginning of the end of the cloud as the unquestioned destination for every advanced AI workload.
That distinction matters.
The first AI race was about building intelligence.
The second was about acquiring accelerated computing capacity.The third is becoming a contest over where intelligence lives, how it acts and who captures the value it creates.
This changes the balance of power.
Apple can compete through integrated hardware and software.
Meta can compete through open-weight model distribution.
Microsoft can defend the enterprise operating environment.
Google can coordinate models, devices and cloud infrastructure.
Nvidia can supply computing architectures across multiple deployment environments.
And frontier-model companies must increasingly prove that their intelligence delivers enough incremental value to justify its price.
The competitive boundaries separating these companies are becoming less stable.
Former rivals can become partners at one layer while fighting for control at another.
That is the deeper meaning of the Apple–Meta announcement.
The next revolution in computing will not be defined by the location of the largest model. It will be defined by the economics of deploying intelligence everywhere.
The strategic asset of the next decade may no longer be the model alone.
It may be the system capable of making intelligence continuously available, secure, affordable and productive.
Velocity Insight: The first AI revolution made intelligence powerful. The next will make intelligence pervasive—and the greatest economic prize will belong to whoever controls its deployment.

THE GREAT AI POWER SHIFT
Why the Apple–Meta Alliance Signals the Rise of Sovereign Personal Computing
The Big Take
Apple and Meta have just crossed one of the most consequential competitive boundaries in the technology industry.
Two companies that spent years fighting over privacy, advertising, distribution and control of the digital consumer have joined forces to bring frontier-scale, open-weight artificial intelligence onto Apple’s computing architecture.
On October 15, Meta introduced Muse Spark Open, an 816-billion-parameter multimodal model optimized for sophisticated reasoning, software engineering and agentic tasks. Working with Apple, Meta demonstrated that this class of model can run locally across two Mac Studios powered by M5 Ultra chips, using Apple’s MLX framework.
The collaboration is confirmed by Meta, Apple executives and independent reporting. It is a technical and ecosystem partnership—not evidence of a merger, exclusive distribution agreement or integration into every Apple device.
The announcement appears modest compared with the hundreds of billions being invested in hyperscale AI infrastructure.
Its strategic implications are anything but modest.
For the first time, the emerging contest is not simply over who builds the most powerful intelligence. It is increasingly over who controls where that intelligence runs, who owns the interface, who retains the data and who captures the economics of inference.
This is the potential beginning of a new competitive regime.
The cloud will remain indispensable for training, large-scale services and demanding workloads. But its assumed dominance over advanced inference is now being challenged by another architecture: powerful, locally controlled intelligence embedded within personal and enterprise computing systems.
The Great Split is moving from the ownership of AI models toward control of AI deployment.
The fundamental question
What happens to the economics of Big Tech when advanced intelligence no longer has to be rented from the cloud for every task?
The answer could redraw the competitive boundaries separating Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Google, Nvidia, OpenAI and the next generation of enterprise AI platforms.
The most consequential possibility is not that local computing replaces hyperscale infrastructure.
It is that intelligence becomes portable across competing computing environments, reducing dependence on any single model provider or cloud platform.
That would shift bargaining power toward those controlling devices, operating systems, memory architectures, software distribution and trusted enterprise workflows.

The architecture is particularly important.
Apple’s unified-memory design allows up to 512 GB per Mac Studio. Two connected systems can accommodate the model’s memory requirements, while the model’s mixture-of-experts architecture activates only part of its total parameter count during inference.
Meta is distributing the model through multiple cloud platforms and inference frameworks, not just Apple hardware. The weights are available under a license with commercial restrictions; open-weight does not mean unrestricted open-source software.
The technical achievement is real. So are its limitations: the demonstrated setup is expensive, specialized and aimed primarily at developers and demanding professional workloads.
It does not yet establish that an equivalent model can run economically on an iPhone, smart glasses or an ordinary laptop.
But the direction is unmistakable.
The economic frontier of AI is beginning to move from remote computational capacity toward locally accessible computational capability.
