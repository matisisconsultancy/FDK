The AI Supercycle confronts its first systemic financial test. Global growth remains dependent on accelerated computing, while semiconductor valuations, credit markets and sovereign borrowing costs begin revealing the limits of capital-intensive expansion.
IN FOCUS — THE WORLD’S MOST IMPORTANT GROWTH ENGINE IS BECOMING ITS MOST CONSEQUENTIAL FINANCIAL RISK
Four numbers define Tuesday’s global financial markets.
3.1% :: The IMF’s new forecast for global economic growth in 2026, supported by exceptional investment in artificial intelligence.
10% :: The share of global investment-grade bond issuance now attributed to hyperscalers, compared with less than 1% in 2024.
$350 BILLION :: The market capitalization lost by Europe’s ten leading luxury companies since the beginning of 2026.
$18.68 BILLION :: Goldman Sachs’ third-quarter revenue, driven partly by extraordinary trading activity as investors reposition across increasingly volatile markets. 
The contradiction is profound.
The same technological investment cycle supporting global economic growth is creating new financial vulnerabilities.
The IMF warned today that a reversal in AI investment, accompanied by falling asset prices and tighter financing conditions, could reduce global economic output by 1.1% by 2028, including a 1.8% reduction in U.S. output, relative to its baseline scenario.
These are modeled downside effects, not forecasts of what will happen. 
Meanwhile, semiconductor shares suffered a global selloff. South Korea’s leading memory-chip companies declined sharply. Wall Street’s largest banks reported divergent earnings. European technology stocks retreated, while the sovereign-debt market remained sensitive to inflation and energy prices.
The hidden pattern is increasingly clear.
The financial system is beginning to distinguish between the capital required to produce intelligence and the economic returns generated when intelligence becomes productive.
This is the next phase of the AI Supercycle.
And it may trigger a structural rotation across global equities, credit markets and industrial investment.

PART I — THE FIVE SIGNALS
THE MARKET DIAGNOSIS: THE AI GROWTH ENGINE MEETS FINANCIAL DISCIPLINE
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
Can artificial intelligence continue supporting global growth when the financial system begins demanding higher returns from the very infrastructure on which that growth depends?
Tuesday delivered five consequential signals.
1. THE IMF WARNING — GLOBAL GROWTH IS BECOMING TOO DEPENDENT ON AI
The IMF’s October World Economic Outlook projects:
Indicator IMF forecast or scenario
Global GDP growth, 2026 3.1%
Global GDP growth, 2027 3.4%
Global inflation, 2026 4.7%
Global inflation, 2027 4.1%
Global GDP impact of an adverse AI correction by 2028 −1.1%
U.S. GDP impact under that scenario −1.8%
The Fund’s forecasts rely partly on continued AI investment and an eventual normalization of international energy transportation.
An adverse scenario involving oil prices above $130 through the first quarter of 2027 would reduce global growth in 2027 by approximately half a percentage point. 
The financial implications are more consequential than the headline growth forecast.
AI investment is increasingly supporting output across countries producing advanced semiconductors, electronics, computing equipment and associated infrastructure.
But the concentration of growth creates a vulnerability.
If expected AI productivity fails to materialize, investment could slow.
Slower investment could reduce earnings expectations.
Lower earnings expectations could compress valuations.
That could tighten financial conditions and further weaken capital expenditure.
The AI Supercycle is becoming sufficiently large to influence the global business cycle itself.
This represents a fundamental transition from a technology-sector investment theme toward a macroeconomic stability issue.
2. THE SEMICONDUCTOR SELLOFF — SCARCITY RENTS FACE A NEW CHALLENGETuesday’s most revealing equity-market development occurred in semiconductor stocks.
South Korea’s Kospi fell approximately 5.5%, while Samsung Electronics declined around 7.4% and SK Hynix lost approximately 10%.
In the United States, Micron declined roughly 4.5% during the session.
European semiconductor companies also suffered substantial losses.
The catalyst was renewed concern about competition following reports of progress by China’s ChangXin Memory Technologies, or CXMT, in developing a denser memory-chip architecture.
Commercial viability, production yields and competitive performance remain uncertain. 
The market nevertheless reacted aggressively.
Why?
Because semiconductor valuations have increasingly capitalized expectations of sustained scarcity, exceptional margins and strong pricing power.
A credible future competitor can alter those assumptions before it produces comparable commercial volumes.
The financial mechanism is straightforward.
Expected supply increases → weaker scarcity premiums → lower anticipated margins → valuation compression.
This is not proof that the semiconductor investment cycle has peaked.
Demand for accelerated computing remains substantial.
But it demonstrates how sensitive valuations have become to changes in perceived competitive advantage.
The decisive distinction is between demand growth and economic capture.
A market can grow rapidly while the profitability of its leading suppliers declines.
The next semiconductor competition will be about controlling the cost curve of intelligence—not simply expanding computing capacity.
3. WALL STREET BANKS — EXCEPTIONAL TRADING PROFITS, MORE SELECTIVE CORPORATE FINANCING
Tuesday’s U.S. banking results revealed an important divergence.
Goldman Sachs reported $18.68 billion in quarterly revenue and $5.29 billion in net earnings.
Equities trading revenue increased 72% year on year to $6.44 billion.
Investment-banking fees rose 21% to $3.22 billion.
Its annualized return on equity reached 18.7%. 
JPMorgan delivered a different signal.
Earnings reached $5.57 per share, below the LSEG consensus estimate of $5.85.
Investment-banking fees declined 8% sequentially, while equities trading revenue increased 49% year on year.
Net interest income excluding markets rose 6% to $24.7 billion.
The bank indicated that transactions were increasingly being postponed amid financial-market uncertainty. 
Citigroup also reported strong profitability, with quarterly revenue of approximately $23.8 billion and net income of $5.2 billion. 
The hidden financial pattern is important.
Market volatility can generate exceptional trading income for investment banks even as it complicates long-term corporate financing.
Institutional investors are repositioning portfolios.
Banks are benefiting from that activity.
But companies considering initial public offerings, acquisitions and large capital commitments face a more demanding financing environment.
This creates an unusual divergence between financial intermediation profits and capital-formation conditions.
Wall Street can earn more from market volatility while the real economy finds long-term financing increasingly expensive.
That distinction deserves close attention.
4. THE ENERGY–BOND COLLISION — CAPITAL BECOMES MORE EXPENSIVE
Brent crude traded around $106.10 per barrel during Tuesday’s U.S. session, easing modestly while remaining historically elevated.
The U.S. 10-year Treasury yield declined to approximately 5.22%, following last week’s peak above 5.36%.
The dollar also eased from recent highs. 
The immediate movement provided some relief.
The structural problem remains.
Elevated oil prices increase energy and transportation costs.
Persistent inflation pressures constrain central banks.
High sovereign borrowing requirements compete with corporate financing demand.
And infrastructure developers must commit substantial capital before projects become operational.The IMF’s Global Financial Stability Report identified a particularly important concern: the ability of financial markets to absorb growing sovereign and private-sector bond issuance.
Hyperscalers now account for approximately 10% of global investment-grade bond issuance, compared with less than 1% in 2024. 
That number changes the financial interpretation of the AI Supercycle.
Technology companies are no longer merely generating extraordinary cash flows from software.
They are becoming major participants in the global market for long-duration capital.
This creates a new competition for financing.
Governments need capital to refinance debt. Technology companies need capital to build intelligence. Industrial companies need capital to deploy it.
The price of financing all three will increasingly determine the speed of economic transformation.
5. EUROPE — TECHNOLOGY WEAKENS WHILE INDUSTRIAL DIFFERENTIATION ACCELERATES
European equities closed slightly lower on Tuesday.
The STOXX 600 declined approximately 0.1%, reversing part of Monday’s recovery.
Semiconductor companies led the weakness.
ASM International and Soitec fell more than 6%, while the broader technology sector suffered from the international repricing of AI-related companies.
Chemical companies advanced approximately 2%.
Luxury shares remained under pressure following disappointing results from LVMH. 
The more consequential development concerns Europe’s exposure to China.
Reuters estimates that the ten leading European luxury companies have lost approximately $350 billion in market capitalization during 2026.
The deterioration reflects persistent weakness in Chinese consumer demand and reduced expectations of a near-term recovery. 
This creates an important contrast.
Europe’s traditional high-margin consumer franchises are confronting weaker demand and valuation compression.
At the same time, European companies investing in industrial software, electricity infrastructure and advanced computing face potentially expanding strategic markets.
The next European opportunity may therefore emerge less from recovering yesterday’s growth model than from financing tomorrow’s industrial productivity.
Europe’s challenge is to convert its industrial strengths into competitive advantage within the emerging AI-native economy.

THE HIDDEN PATTERN
THE GLOBAL ECONOMY IS REPRICING THE PRODUCTIVITY OF CAPITAL
Tuesday’s developments reveal a common financial transformation.
Artificial intelligence continues supporting global economic growth.
But the companies producing AI infrastructure are increasingly exposed to competitive repricing.
Banks are generating substantial income from financial volatility.
Yet corporate financing remains selective.
Energy prices are keeping the cost of industrial activity elevated.
And the global bond market must absorb substantial financing requirements from governments and technology companies simultaneously.
The evolutionary mechanism has five stages:
1. AI investment accelerates economic activity.
2. Accelerated investment increases demand for computing, electricity and financial capital.
3. Scarcity raises costs and generates exceptional profits for selected suppliers.
4. Competition, financial discipline and technological innovation begin reducing scarcity premiums.
5. Capital reallocates toward businesses capable of generating durable returns from productive deployment.
The fifth stage is the most important.
It marks the possible transition from the infrastructure-building phase of the AI Supercycle toward the industrial-productivity phase.
The next market rotation may favor companies that transform intelligence into measurable economic output rather than those dependent primarily on selling the infrastructure needed to produce it.
That transition leads directly to the central capital-allocation question.

PART II — WHERE GLOBAL CAPITAL IS MOVING
FROM ACCELERATED COMPUTING TO ACCELERATED ECONOMIC RETURNS
THE EMERGING INVESTMENT HIERARCHYThe next phase of global capital formation will increasingly distinguish between four categories of assets.
Computing infrastructure: the processors, memory and networks producing intelligence.
Essential physical infrastructure: electricity, data centers and connectivity enabling deployment.
Industrial intelligence: software and agentic systems improving operational performance.
Financial infrastructure: banks and institutional investors mobilizing capital across these activities.
The opportunity is substantial.
But the distribution of returns will be increasingly uneven.
1. ACCELERATED COMPUTING — EUROPE BEGINS DIVERSIFYING ITS TECHNOLOGICAL DEPENDENCE
A consequential development emerged from France today.
Scaleway announced plans to build an AI supercomputer in Sweden using AMD Instinct MI355X processors.
The company described it as Europe’s first AMD-based supercomputer intended for commercial AI use.
The system will involve European supercomputer manufacturer Bull, alongside international partners supplying networking, storage, servers and cooling.
Scaleway selected Sweden partly because of its low-carbon electricity and established AI ecosystem. 
The strategic significance goes beyond another computing installation.
Europe is attempting to diversify the technological architecture supporting its AI development.
AMD’s ROCm software provides an open-source alternative to Nvidia’s proprietary CUDA ecosystem.
That difference matters.
It potentially reduces dependence on one semiconductor supplier and increases flexibility for developers.
But technological flexibility carries implementation costs.
Software compatibility, performance, reliability and integration determine whether competing hardware platforms deliver attractive economics.
The financial opportunity is therefore not simply diversification for its own sake.
It is the creation of a more competitive market for intelligence infrastructure.
Europe’s computing sovereignty will depend less on owning every component than on controlling the ability to choose, integrate and substitute competing technologies.
2. THE AI CREDIT MARKET — A NEW SOURCE OF SYSTEMIC INTERDEPENDENCE
The growing participation of hyperscalers in investment-grade debt issuance introduces a new dimension to the AI investment thesis.
Large technology companies possess substantial internal cash generation.
But the scale of their infrastructure expansion increasingly creates incentives to use external financing, leases and specialized investment vehicles.
This supports construction and accelerates deployment.
It also introduces additional financial sensitivity.
A rise of 100 basis points in the borrowing cost of an illustrative $10 billion floating-rate infrastructure loan increases annual interest expense by $100 million, assuming the entire principal remains outstanding and unhedged.
The calculation is straightforward.
Its consequences are not.
Higher debt-service costs can reduce equity returns.
Construction delays can postpone operating revenues.
Technological depreciation can shorten the economic life of computing equipment.
And changes in customer demand can undermine utilization assumptions.
These risks explain why the financial architecture of AI infrastructure increasingly matters as much as the underlying technology.
The strongest investment opportunities will combine reliable electricity, contracted demand, credible financing and realistic equipment-depreciation assumptions.
Computing capacity becomes financially valuable only when its productive utilization generates sufficient cash flow to compensate for capital and technological risk.
3. INDUSTRIAL AI — CAPITAL BEGINS MOVING TOWARD APPLICATIONS
Another important development illustrates a different destination for AI capital.
Nvidia agreed Tuesday to acquire a minority stake in French healthcare platform Doctolib.
The size and financial value of the investment were not disclosed.
The strategic objective is to support the development of AI capabilities within the healthcare platform.The transaction is small in disclosed strategic scale compared with the global AI infrastructure buildout.
Its significance is nevertheless substantial.
It illustrates the movement of semiconductor ecosystem investment toward applications that could generate recurring economic value.
The underlying logic extends beyond healthcare.
Industrial AI can potentially improve manufacturing throughput, logistics coordination, energy management and predictive maintenance.
Agentic AI can reduce the coordination costs associated with complex workflows.
Pervasive AI can connect intelligence directly to physical systems.
But the critical financial test remains unchanged.
Can these technologies generate measurable improvements in revenue, productivity or operating costs?
That is where the next investment premium may emerge.
The highest-value AI application is not necessarily the one consuming the most computing power. It is the one delivering the greatest risk-adjusted economic return.
4. ENERGY AND DEFENSE — THE RISE OF SYSTEM-LEVEL PRODUCTIVITY
The global energy challenge is increasingly one of deliverability.
Morgan Stanley previously estimated that U.S. data-center developers face a potential 32-gigawatt net power shortfall through 2028, equivalent to approximately 34% of projected requirements after accounting for alternative supply measures.
This is an analyst estimate rather than confirmed future unmet demand. 
The investment opportunity extends across electricity generation, transmission, storage, cooling and grid management.
But infrastructure expansion alone will not resolve every constraint.
Industrial AI can improve the utilization of installed infrastructure.
More effective demand forecasting can reduce operating inefficiencies.
Predictive maintenance can improve availability.
Intelligent energy management can help optimize consumption.
Defense faces a comparable challenge.
Europe’s Council formally adopted its €115 million AGILE defense-innovation program on October 12, supporting smaller companies developing and testing emerging defense technologies. 
The financial value lies in accelerating the transition from technological development to deployable capabilities.
Modern defense systems increasingly depend on sensors, software, communications and interoperability.
A relatively modest investment in system integration can potentially improve the effectiveness of a much larger physical equipment base.
The next defense and infrastructure advantage will come not simply from purchasing more assets, but from making entire operating systems more effective.
5. MOBILITY — THE EMERGENCE OF AUTONOMOUS OPERATING PLATFORMS
Mobility offers one of the clearest examples of the next Industrial-AI opportunity.
A recent partnership between Lucid and Bolt envisages the deployment of 25,000 autonomous vehicles across European cities, using Nvidia’s autonomous-driving architecture.
The agreement is a deployment plan, not evidence that the vehicles are already operating commercially. 
The potential economic transformation extends beyond autonomous driving.
Intelligent mobility requires the integration of vehicle technology, energy infrastructure, fleet scheduling, maintenance, payments, safety and passenger services.
The economically relevant asset becomes the operating platform.
That principle applies equally to railways, electric urban transport, logistics and multimodal mobility.
AI can potentially improve capacity utilization, reduce downtime and optimize energy consumption.
The critical valuation metric becomes lifecycle return on invested capital.
The next mobility leader will not necessarily be the operator with the largest fleet. It will be the operator generating the greatest economic output from its integrated mobility system.
6. EUROPEAN FINANCE — THE CAPITAL-FORMATION ENDGAME
Europe’s financial restructuring remains central to this transformation.
The proposed consolidation involving Intesa Sanpaolo and MPS, alongside UniCredit’s broader European ambitions, illustrates competing strategies for achieving greater banking scale.But the strategic prize extends beyond ownership.
Europe requires financial institutions capable of mobilizing private savings into productive investment.
AI infrastructure.
Energy systems.
Defense manufacturing.
Industrial modernization.
Intelligent mobility.
The next phase of European financial competition should therefore be evaluated through the quality of capital allocation rather than institutional size alone.
Banking consolidation can create efficiencies.
But integration costs, capital requirements and governance complexity can also destroy value.
The relevant financial tests include return on tangible equity, cost of equity, funding diversification and sustainable growth in productive lending.
Europe’s banking consolidation will become economically consequential only when greater financial scale translates into greater productive investment.

THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS
THE NEXT GREAT CAPITAL ROTATION
The financial evidence points toward five potential changes.
First — Scarcity premiums become more contestable. Technological competition can compress margins even while global demand for AI continues expanding.
Second — The cost of intelligence becomes a primary competitive variable. Performance per watt, inference economics and the utilization of computing assets increasingly determine profitability.
Third — AI-related financing becomes more sensitive to bond markets. The growing interaction between corporate infrastructure borrowing and sovereign issuance can amplify changes in interest rates.
Fourth — Industrial AI attracts capital seeking measurable productivity. Applications improving existing infrastructure may offer a different risk-return profile from entirely new capital-intensive construction.
Fifth — Geopolitical technological competition accelerates. The United States, China and Europe will increasingly compete through computing architectures, energy systems, financing capacity and industrial deployment.
The transition will not necessarily produce a generalized withdrawal from technology equities.
It is more likely to create wider valuation dispersion across the technology and industrial value chains.
The next rotation will distinguish companies that sell intelligence from those that can make intelligence economically productive.

PART III — THE NEXT TRADING DAY
WEDNESDAY, OCTOBER 14, 2026
FIVE CATALYSTS THAT COULD RESET GLOBAL MARKET EXPECTATIONS
Wednesday brings a convergence of inflation data, semiconductor earnings and financial-sector results.
These events will test the foundations of Tuesday’s market repricing.
1. U.S. INFLATION — THE DECISIVE MONETARY TEST
The September U.S. Consumer Price Index is scheduled for release at 08:30 Eastern Time / 14:30 CEST.
Economists expect headline inflation of approximately 3.7% year on year, with core inflation around 2.5%.
These are consensus estimates, not reported results. 
The critical distinction will be between energy-driven headline inflation and broader price pressure.
A stronger-than-expected core reading could revive concerns about additional Federal Reserve tightening.
A softer reading could reduce borrowing-cost pressure.
The market implications extend directly into technology valuations, corporate credit and infrastructure financing.
2. ASML — EUROPE’S MOST IMPORTANT SEMICONDUCTOR EARNINGS TEST
ASML will publish its third-quarter results before European trading begins.
Investors will focus on new orders, revenue guidance, customer investment plans and the outlook for advanced semiconductor manufacturing equipment. 
The key issue is whether the global semiconductor selloff reflects deteriorating demand or a reassessment of future competition and margins.
A strong order book could support the structural demand thesis.
Weak guidance would increase concern about the timing of returns on AI infrastructure investment.
3. BANK OF AMERICA AND MORGAN STANLEY — THE CREDIT AND CAPITAL-MARKETS TEST
Bank of America and Morgan Stanley are scheduled to report third-quarter earnings.Following Tuesday’s divergent results, investors will examine whether strong trading revenues are compensating for weaker debt-market and investment-banking activity.
The most consequential indicators will be credit quality, funding costs, advisory pipelines, wealth-management performance and return on equity.
Together, these results will help determine whether financial intermediation remains robust enough to support the next investment cycle.
4. ASIAN SEMICONDUCTORS — DOES THE SELLOFF CONTINUE?
Asian markets will provide an early test of the semiconductor repricing.
Watch Samsung, SK Hynix, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing and Japan’s semiconductor-equipment companies.
The central question concerns whether reports of technological progress by CXMT represent a meaningful future threat to established memory-chip margins.
Investors will also position for TSMC’s results on Thursday, October 15.
The distinction between technological competition and actual commercial disruption will be decisive.
5. ENERGY AND SOVEREIGN BONDS — THE FINANCING TRANSMISSION
The final catalyst concerns Brent crude, U.S. Treasury yields and European sovereign spreads.
A renewed increase in oil prices combined with stronger inflation could trigger higher long-term yields.
That would increase the financial hurdle facing data centers, utilities, industrial investment and mobility infrastructure.
Conversely, stabilization could support selected capital-intensive businesses.
The central question is whether Wednesday’s developments reinforce or weaken the financial conditions necessary to sustain the AI investment cycle.

THE NEXT 24 HOURS
THREE FINANCIAL CONFIGURATIONS
CONSTRUCTIVE STABILIZATION
U.S. core inflation moderates, ASML confirms resilient demand and bank results demonstrate continued financial-system strength.
Implication: Selective recovery in semiconductors, industrials and capital-intensive infrastructure.
SELECTIVE REPRICING
Inflation remains elevated, semiconductor valuations continue adjusting and earnings quality determines market leadership.
Implication: Capital increasingly favors companies with strong balance sheets, reliable cash flows and demonstrated productivity advantages.
FINANCIAL TRANSMISSION
Inflation exceeds expectations, sovereign yields resume rising and credit-market conditions tighten.Implication: Greater pressure on long-duration technology assets, leveraged infrastructure and companies requiring repeated external financing.
The third configuration is the most consequential because it would transform financial-market volatility into constraints on productive capital formation.

THE NEXT 90 DAYS
FIVE INVESTMENT CONSEQUENCES
1. AI infrastructure enters a more demanding valuation regime. Investors will increasingly evaluate computing capacity through cash conversion, depreciation, utilization and risk-adjusted returns.
2. Semiconductor competition widens beyond processing performance. Memory technology, power efficiency, software architectures and manufacturing economics may become increasingly important sources of differentiation.
3. The bond market becomes a critical determinant of AI expansion. Higher financing costs may increase the advantage of companies possessing strong internal cash generation and contracted demand.
4. Industrial AI gains strategic importance. Intelligent manufacturing, energy optimization, autonomous mobility and infrastructure management could attract investment seeking measurable returns from existing assets.
5. Europe’s competitive future becomes more dependent on capital formation. Financial integration, reliable energy and industrial AI deployment will increasingly determine whether the continent can sustain technological and economic relevance.
These developments point toward a market rotation driven by economic productivity rather than technological enthusiasm alone.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
FOR INVESTORS, BOARDS OF DIRECTORS AND POLICYMAKERS
1. Separate AI growth from AI profitability. Demand for intelligence can expand while valuations and margins decline.
2. Reassess capital expenditure through risk-adjusted returns. The cost of financing and the economic life of technological assets are becoming critical variables.
3. Treat electricity and connectivity as strategic financial infrastructure. Reliable physical capacity increasingly determines whether AI investment becomes productive.
4. Recognize Industrial AI as the next major investment frontier. The strongest applications will improve measurable operating performance rather than merely increase technological complexity.
5. Make capital formation the central objective of European industrial strategy. Banking scale, fiscal credibility and technological deployment must reinforce one another.

CLOSING VIEW
THE NEXT GREAT SPLIT WILL BE BETWEEN INTELLIGENCE AND ITS ECONOMIC RETURNS
Tuesday’s developments mark a potentially important turning point.
The IMF has confirmed that artificial intelligence is supporting global economic growth.
But it has also warned that the same investment cycle could become a source of systemic financial instability.
Semiconductor valuations are becoming more sensitive to future competition.
Technology companies are becoming more important borrowers in global credit markets.
Energy prices remain elevated.
Banks are benefiting from volatility while some forms of corporate financing become more selective.
And Europe is confronting the simultaneous deterioration of established consumer franchises and the need to finance a new industrial architecture.
These are not unrelated developments.
They represent the emergence of a more demanding economic regime.
The first phase of the AI Supercycle rewarded technological leadership.
The second rewarded control over scarce computing infrastructure.
The third rewarded the ability to mobilize extraordinary amounts of capital.
The next phase will reward the ability to generate superior economic returns from intelligence already deployed.
That distinction will redefine competitive advantage.
For investors, it means evaluating AI through cash flow, cost of capital and sustainable profitability.
For boards, it means redesigning investment decisions around measurable economic productivity.For governments, it means ensuring that financial markets, energy infrastructure and industrial policy support the deployment of intelligence throughout the productive economy.
And for Europe, it means moving decisively from technological dependence toward competitive Industrial AI.
The Age of Pervasive Intelligence is approaching.
But its financial rewards will not necessarily flow to the companies building the largest infrastructure.
They may increasingly accrue to those capable of integrating intelligence into entire operating systems, improving productivity and generating durable economic value.
The next Great Split will not be between those who possess intelligence and those who do not.
It will be between those who can finance intelligence, those who can make it productive, and those whose economics cannot sustain either.
VELOCITY INSIGHT
The first AI Supercycle was about producing intelligence. The next will be about turning intelligence into capital productivity—and the financial markets are beginning to price that distinction.
