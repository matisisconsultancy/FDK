THE GREAT POLITICAL REPRICING OF THE AI SUPERCYCLE
America’s Midterm Reversal, the New Infrastructure Bottleneck and the Battle for Global Capital
The AI Supercycle is not losing its technological momentum. But the November 3 U.S. midterm elections have changed the financial conditions under which its next generation of infrastructure may be constructed.
For the first time in this investment cycle, the political legitimacy of large-scale AI infrastructure has become a major market variable.
Democrats have secured control of both chambers of Congress, while candidates campaigning on electricity affordability and restrictions on data-centre development have won important state-level contests.
The results are already influencing capital markets.
American data-centre equities fell sharply on November 4, even as broader Wall Street indices rallied. South Korean technology shares declined approximately 5.3% on November 5, while European equities advanced toward record highs. Fidelity +2
Yet the industrial evidence tells a different story.
Legrand is reporting extraordinary demand for data-centre electrical infrastructure. Skanska’s construction backlog has reached an all-time high. Google is providing credit support to an Anthropic infrastructure project, while Microsoft is preserving investment flexibility by declining an optional expansion of an existing computing agreement.
The contradiction is fundamental: demand for accelerated computing remains powerful, but the ability to turn that demand into approved, financed and commercially productive infrastructure is becoming increasingly selective.
The U.S. electoral outcome adds another dimension.
The political debate is moving beyond the benefits of artificial intelligence toward the distribution of its infrastructure costs: who pays for additional electricity generation, water use, transmission networks and local development?
For financial markets, this is consequential.
A data centre can possess customers, capital commitments and sophisticated computing hardware while facing delays that materially reduce its investment returns.
Conversely, infrastructure already possessing grid connections, approvals and credible financing may acquire a competitive premium.
The next financial rotation could therefore occur within the AI value chain, rather than away from it.
The Great Split is entering a new phase: the division between those capable of financing and executing technological expansion under increasingly demanding conditions—and those whose access to capital depends on assumptions that may no longer hold.
THE BIG TAKE
The U.S. midterms have transformed the AI infrastructure debate from a question of technological capacity into a question of economic legitimacy and investment discipline.
Three structural consequences deserve attention.
First, the scarcity of computing infrastructure is becoming more complex. Semiconductors remain critical, but electricity, permitted land, construction certainty and public acceptance are increasingly important complementary resources.
Second, financial markets may distinguish more sharply between operating infrastructure and speculative development pipelines. A megawatt of commissioned computing capacity has different economic characteristics from a megawatt announced for future construction.
Third, political and financing constraints may reinforce the advantages of companies already possessing strong balance sheets, reliable energy contracts and operational facilities.
The next market cycle may not reward the largest AI investment commitments. It may reward the most credible conversion of investment commitments into cash-generating capacity.
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
WILL POLITICAL SCRUTINY MAKE THE AI SUPERCYCLE MORE PRODUCTIVE—OR CONCENTRATE ITS OWNERSHIP AMONG THOSE WHO CAN AFFORD THE HIGHER COST OF BUILDING IT?
Both outcomes are possible.Greater scrutiny could eliminate inefficient projects, improve electricity-cost allocation and reduce the burden on households.
But prolonged uncertainty could delay productive investment, increase financing costs and disadvantage smaller infrastructure developers.
The crucial financial test is whether political institutions improve the quality of capital allocation or simply reduce the quantity of capacity delivered.
The difference will determine whether the next phase of the AI Supercycle broadens economic opportunity or accelerates the concentration of productive assets and financial returns.

FIVE SIGNALS SHAPING THE NEXT ECONOMY
SIGNAL I — THE U.S. MIDTERMS HAVE CREATED A NEW INFRASTRUCTURE RISK PREMIUM
Verified developments
The November 3 elections resulted in Democratic control of both the House and Senate.
Reuters reported a Senate majority of at least 54–44 based on the races called when it published its November 4 account, alongside substantial Democratic gains in the House. Some individual races and subsequent official certification processes remained separate from those projections. Fidelity
The electoral shift extends to major data-centre markets.
In Texas and Georgia, successful gubernatorial candidates had campaigned in part on restrictions or temporary pauses affecting new facilities.
At the local level, Reuters reported that voters supported 33 of 34 initiatives aimed at restricting data-centre construction.
The immediate market reaction was differentiated: broader American equities rose, while several data-centre developers declined sharply. Fidelity +2
FDK interpretation
The AI Supercycle has entered the economics of political permission.
Until recently, the principal constraints were advanced semiconductors, high-bandwidth memory, electricity connections and infrastructure construction.
These constraints remain.
But political and community acceptance is becoming another factor influencing whether proposed investment can be converted into operational assets.
The financial transmission is:
Political opposition → Approval uncertainty → Longer commissioning periods → Higher financing exposure → Deferred revenue → Lower expected returns.
The mechanism is project-specific.
The election result does not itself cancel existing data centres or create nationwide federal restrictions.
New legislation will also face institutional constraints, including presidential veto powers, Senate procedures and the division of authority between federal and state governments.
Nevertheless, state permitting and utility regulation can have substantial practical effects without new federal legislation.
The financial significance
The market is increasingly likely to distinguish between three forms of computing capacity.Operational capacity is already generating or capable of generating commercially contracted revenues.
Permitted capacity has cleared important regulatory obstacles but may still require financing, energy and construction.
Announced capacity may remain dependent on multiple approvals and commercial assumptions.
The difference is economically substantial.
A project whose completion is delayed may incur additional financing expenses and face technological replacement before it reaches expected utilization.
By contrast, existing powered and contracted facilities may benefit from reduced competition if new development slows.
Second-order effect
Political permission could become a scarce complement to computing capacity—and scarcity could migrate toward owners of already viable infrastructure.
But the effect need not favor all incumbents.
Utilities, regulators and communities may demand stronger commitments on electricity pricing, water consumption and environmental performance.
Those obligations could reduce margins even for established operators.
The next competitive advantage will increasingly depend on reducing the combined cost of approval, construction, energy and capital—not merely securing the largest computing order book.
SIGNAL II — LEGRAND AND SKANSKA REVEAL THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN AI DEMAND AND AI FINANCING RISK
Verified developments
Legrand raised its 2026 growth guidance following strong data-centre demand.
The company reports that data-centre-related activities now account for approximately one-third of revenue.
Chief Executive Benoît Coquart told Reuters that growing public scrutiny could increase demand for energy-efficient equipment, recycled materials and local electricity generation.
He reported that energy-saving products represent approximately 40% of the company’s data-centre sales. Reuters
Skanska’s third-quarter results provide another significant signal.
Construction orders reached SEK 68 billion, compared with SEK 39.9 billion a year earlier.
The order backlog reached SEK 328.3 billion, a company record.
Construction operating margin increased from 4.2% to 5.0%.
Management also emphasized a more selective approach toward newer data-centre developers whose financing and execution capabilities are less established. Skanska +1
FDK interpretation
The industrial supply chain is demonstrating stronger demand than the equity valuations of some infrastructure developers might suggest.
This is not contradictory.
Legrand generates revenues by providing electrical infrastructure.
Skanska generates construction revenues as projects advance.
Infrastructure developers must finance construction and subsequently monetize the completed facilities.
These business models have different risk exposures.
The critical distinction is the timing of cash flow.
A supplier can receive payment during the construction cycle.
An owner may need to wait years before earning its targeted investment return.
The second-order effect
The elections could increase demand for technologies that reduce infrastructure’s impact on electricity systems and local communities.
Higher energy-efficiency requirements may benefit certain equipment suppliers while increasing development costs for operators.
The investment opportunity could therefore migrate toward businesses controlling productive solutions to the new constraints.
The market should distinguish between a reduction in speculative development and a reduction in economically necessary infrastructure spending.
The former could improve capital discipline.
The latter could impede the productivity benefits of accelerated computing.
The investor test
The central measures are not simply order growth or exposure to AI.
They are:
Contracted demand and customer credit quality.
Operating margins and free-cash-flow conversion.
Exposure to project delays and financing failures.
Returns on capital employed.
Dependence on speculative versus established infrastructure developers.The next AI infrastructure premium will belong to those who can monetize demand while controlling the financial risks required to deliver it.
SIGNAL III — MICROSOFT AND GOOGLE REVEAL THE NEW FINANCIAL HIERARCHY OF AI INFRASTRUCTURE
Verified developments
Two contrasting developments reveal how the largest technology platforms are managing infrastructure commitments.
Microsoft will not exercise an option to more than double its existing computing agreement with Nscale at a Texas data centre.
According to Reuters, the decision was made several months ago, before Nscale’s IPO filing. The existing contract remains intact.
It should therefore not be interpreted as a newly announced cancellation of Microsoft’s broader infrastructure strategy. live
Google, meanwhile, has agreed to provide credit support connected with TeraWulf’s Kentucky data-centre development for Anthropic.
TeraWulf’s regulatory filing describes approximately $19 billion in contracted long-term lease commitments and planned facilities delivering about 401 megawatts of critical IT load.
Google’s support applies to specified obligations under the leases and is subject to contractual conditions. It is not an unconditional guarantee of every project cost or future revenue assumption. SEC +2
FDK interpretation
The next AI financing advantage may depend on the credibility of the underlying customer as much as on the value of the physical asset.
The developments illustrate two different financial capabilities.
Microsoft is preserving optionality by declining an incremental commitment.
Google is using its credit standing to support a partner’s access to project financing.
Both can be rational strategies under conditions of substantial infrastructure investment.
Neither automatically establishes a decline in AI demand.
The underlying distinction concerns who bears the financial risk of infrastructure expansion.
An infrastructure owner must finance construction, secure electricity and meet debt obligations.
A customer contracting for future capacity can help support financing through long-term payment commitments.
A highly rated third party can improve the bankability of those commitments.
The resulting structure may allow the project to obtain capital on more favorable terms.
The emerging financing hierarchy
FROM FINANCING QUALITY TO COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE
Strong counterparty and credible contracts
Lower financing uncertainty
More reliable construction and deployment
Earlier commercial utilization and cash generation
FDK analytical transmission framework
The mechanism has important limitations.
Contracted demand does not eliminate construction risk.
Credit support does not guarantee profitability.
And long-term computing contracts remain exposed to changes in technology, pricing and customer requirements.
Nevertheless, financing structures can materially affect which projects proceed.
Second-order effect
Financial credibility is becoming a productive resource.
Companies able to combine strong counterparties, electricity availability, contractual cash-flow visibility and disciplined capital structures may achieve faster deployment.
More speculative developers may struggle to finance capacity even when aggregate computing demand remains substantial.
The result could be increasing consolidation.
But financing advantages are sustainable only if completed infrastructure generates adequate economic returns.
The next AI infrastructure winners will not necessarily be the companies that borrow the most. They will be those capable of financing productive capacity at an economic cost below the returns that capacity can generate.
SIGNAL IV — THE U.S. ELECTIONS ARE REPRICING ENERGY, TECHNOLOGICAL SOVEREIGNTY AND GLOBAL CAPITAL FLOWS
Verified developments
The elections have altered expectations for American energy and environmental policy.
Reuters reports that the incoming Democratic congressional majorities may increase scrutiny of large data centres, utility ratepayer protections and infrastructure environmental requirements.Potential support for selected renewable-energy measures has also attracted investor attention.
At the same time, President Trump retains significant executive authority and veto power, limiting the scope for assuming that every proposed legislative change will become law. MarketScreener +1
Another important development concerns international energy prices.
Brent crude declined sharply on November 4 amid changing expectations for the U.S.–Iran conflict and prospects for diplomacy.
On November 5, prices were around $96 per barrel in the European morning, according to the day’s market reporting. Financial Times
Asian markets responded differently to the post-election environment.
South Korea’s KOSPI declined 5.3%, while Japan’s Nikkei and Taiwan’s benchmark also fell. European equities, by contrast, advanced. AP News
FDK interpretation
The midterms have introduced two potentially opposing forces into the economics of accelerated computing.
The first is more restrictive development oversight.
The second is the possibility of lower energy risk premiums and a different policy environment for power generation and international trade.
These forces could have different effects on infrastructure returns.
Greater permitting uncertainty can delay revenue generation.
Lower electricity and financing costs can improve project economics.
The net effect will be specific to each development.
The physical constraint
Artificial intelligence cannot be deployed independently of the energy system.
Computing facilities require electricity generation, transmission, cooling and operating resilience.
When substantial new electricity demand is concentrated geographically, costs may be borne by developers, utilities, ratepayers or public authorities.
The allocation matters.
If developers must fund more infrastructure directly, project capital requirements rise.
If utilities recover costs through broader tariffs, households and other businesses can face higher electricity bills.
If investments produce wider grid improvements, some costs may be offset by system-wide benefits.
The core political dispute is therefore also a corporate-finance dispute: who bears the infrastructure cost, who assumes the risk and who receives the future cash flow?
The global dimension — U.S.–China technological fragmentation
The international AI economy faces another set of constraints.
Reuters reports that approximately 600,000 Nvidia H200 processors are being held in Hong Kong pending approval for large-scale import into mainland China.
Beijing’s restrictions reflect a conflict between near-term access to advanced computing and longer-term domestic technological independence. Reuters
This illustrates an important structural distinction.
A country can seek technological sovereignty while temporarily increasing the cost of accessing computing capacity.
That additional cost may encourage domestic technological development.
But it can also reduce near-term competitiveness if alternative technologies are less efficient or expensive.
Second-order effect
The next geography of AI investment could be shaped simultaneously by political permission in the United States and technological restrictions between the United States and China.
Europe may benefit from selected opportunities to attract investment or supply infrastructure equipment.
But the effect is not automatic.
European projects also face electricity-grid, permitting, financing and industrial-execution constraints.
The next global competition will be determined by which jurisdictions combine technological access, reliable energy, regulatory predictability and financially sustainable deployment.
SIGNAL V — EUROPE’S SOVEREIGN AND MONETARY POSITION BECOMES AN INDUSTRIAL OPPORTUNITY AND A FINANCIAL RISK
Verified developments
European equities strengthened after the American midterm results as investors reassessed geopolitical and trade risks.
The STOXX Europe 600 advanced 1.8% on November 4 and gained further during November 5 trading, approaching its previous record.Lower oil prices contributed to improving expectations for European inflation and industrial costs. Financial Times
Yet the monetary environment remains demanding.
The Bank of England increased its policy rate by 25 basis points to 4%, responding to persistent inflation pressures associated with the energy shock.
The decision demonstrates that falling oil prices do not immediately reverse the effects of previous inflation increases. AP News
FDK interpretation
Europe faces a potentially important opportunity, but the market rally should not be confused with a completed improvement in industrial competitiveness.
A sustained reduction in energy costs could strengthen household purchasing power, reduce operating expenditure and improve expected returns on infrastructure projects.
But these gains depend on the durability of energy-price relief.
Europe also faces structurally different financing conditions across its member economies.
France remains exposed to fiscal uncertainty and elevated sovereign financing premiums.
Germany’s industrial outlook is sensitive to export demand and investment execution.
Italy possesses substantial specialized engineering capabilities but faces challenges in financing industrial adoption at scale.
Spain combines renewable-energy and infrastructure opportunities with exposure to the broader European financing environment.
The Netherlands remains strategically important to semiconductor equipment and advanced industrial technology, while facing domestic grid constraints.
These are different economic exposures, not evidence that every country faces identical risks.
The transatlantic opportunity
The U.S. electoral shift could make Europe relatively attractive for selected international investments.
But attracting data centres is not sufficient to create sustainable competitive advantage.
A successful European strategy requires combining infrastructure investment with industrial AI adoption.
That means funding the use of intelligence in manufacturing, logistics, energy, transportation and enterprise services.
The true productivity dividend emerges when computing infrastructure changes the economics of established productive activities.
The financial implication
The next capital-allocation decision may be between financing additional computing capacity and financing the productive applications that ultimately justify that capacity.
Both are necessary.
But their financial risks differ.
European firms that deploy AI into existing operations may create attractive incremental economic returns without assuming the full cost of developing frontier computing infrastructure.
This opens a potential route toward value capture through industrial productivity rather than hyperscale ownership alone.
Second-order effect
Europe’s opportunity is to turn the financial selectivity of the AI Supercycle into an industrial investment advantage.
Legrand and Skanska demonstrate that European companies can capture revenues from the infrastructure buildout.
The next challenge is to extend the resulting productivity benefits throughout the European industrial economy.
The most consequential financial objective is not simply more AI capital expenditure.
It is higher sustainable cash generation per unit of capital invested across European industry.
THE HIDDEN PATTERNS BEYOND THE HEADLINES
THE GREAT SPLIT IS BECOMING A COMPETITION FOR THE RIGHT TO DEPLOY CAPITAL PRODUCTIVELY
Four structural mechanisms connect the U.S. election results, AI infrastructure demand, credit markets and Europe’s investment opportunity.
FIRST — SCARCITY IS MIGRATING
The initial scarcity of the AI Supercycle was advanced computing capacity.
That scarcity generated extraordinary demand for semiconductors, memory and networking systems.
As investment expanded, electricity, grid connections, cooling and construction capacity became increasingly important.
The November 3 elections have revealed another constraint: the political and institutional ability to approve new infrastructure.
The binding constraint is moving through the value chain.The location of economic value may migrate with it.
The next opportunity is to identify the resource or capability whose scarcity limits the entire system.
This is the financial logic of the Bottleneck Economy.
SECOND — FINANCIAL OPTIONALITY IS BECOMING A STRATEGIC ASSET
Microsoft’s unexercised expansion option illustrates the importance of preserving flexibility.
Google’s credit support for TeraWulf illustrates the value of financing credibility.
Skanska’s cautious selection of developer counterparties illustrates how execution risk influences commercial decisions.
Together, these developments suggest that the relevant advantage is not simply access to capital.
It is the ability to deploy capital selectively as technological, regulatory and financing conditions change.
The next financial winners may be those capable of accelerating investment when returns justify it—and withholding investment when they do not.
THIRD — THE COST OF INFRASTRUCTURE IS BECOMING A DISTRIBUTIONAL QUESTION
The U.S. elections have highlighted public concern about the relationship between private AI investment and household electricity costs.
This creates a new dimension of The Great Split.
The benefits of AI infrastructure may accrue to technology companies, customers, workers and investors.
Its physical costs may fall on developers, electricity consumers and local communities.
The economic distribution matters because political acceptance depends partly on whether communities perceive sufficient benefits.
The sustainability of the AI Supercycle may increasingly depend on the fairness and credibility of its underlying infrastructure economics.
FOURTH — CAPITAL CONCENTRATION COULD BECOME INDUSTRIAL CONCENTRATION
Financially resilient companies can maintain investment through regulatory and market uncertainty.
Weaker competitors may delay projects or sell assets.
The stronger firms can potentially acquire productive infrastructure at more attractive valuations.
If successful, the additional capacity creates further financial returns and investment flexibility.
The resulting process can compound.
Financial strength → Faster productive deployment → Greater cash generation → More capital → Stronger competitive position.
The reverse is equally significant.
Financing constraints → Delayed deployment → Weaker returns → Higher risk premiums → Reduced investment capacity.
Neither mechanism is inevitable.
But their interaction provides a framework for understanding how The Great Split could widen despite continuing technological progress.

Technological velocity remains high. Infrastructure deployment is becoming more dependent on financial quality, physical readiness and political acceptance.
The AI Supercycle remains capable of generating substantial economic value.
But investors can no longer treat announced capacity, financed capacity and commercially productive capacity as equivalent.
The crucial transition is:
Committed capital → Permitted infrastructure → Energized computing capacity → Commercial utilization → Sustainable cash flow.The time and risk associated with each stage will increasingly determine investment returns.
The next competitive advantage will belong to those who can compress that sequence without compromising financial resilience.

FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS AND BOARDS
1. Reprice political permission as a financial variable. Infrastructure approval, utility-cost allocation and community acceptance must enter project valuation alongside electricity and financing costs.
2. Distinguish AI demand from AI infrastructure profitability. Strong semiconductor and construction demand does not guarantee attractive returns for all owners of computing capacity.
3. Treat financing optionality as a source of competitive advantage. Strong companies can preserve capital, renegotiate commitments and finance new opportunities when economics become attractive.
4. Anticipate value migration toward scarce complements. Powered land, grid access, energy efficiency, credible customer contracts and engineering execution may capture increasing shares of economic value.
5. Prepare for capital concentration without assuming it is inevitable. Financing advantages can compound, but technological efficiency, competition and better policy design can broaden productive access.
THE CLOSE
THE GREAT REORDERING OF GLOBAL ECONOMIC POWER
The Next Market Cycle Will Determine Who Gets to Build the Future—and Who Captures Its Returns.
The November 3 U.S. elections have not ended the AI Supercycle. They have exposed a fundamental weakness in the assumption that technological ambition automatically translates into productive investment.
Computing demand remains extraordinary.
Industrial suppliers are reporting substantial growth.
Financial institutions continue to mobilize capital.
Yet the political and financial conditions governing infrastructure deployment are becoming more demanding.
The next phase will require more than advanced semiconductors and ambitious capital-expenditure programs.
It will require access to energy, reliable financing, credible commercial demand, regulatory certainty and infrastructure that can operate productively at scale.
The market consequence is clear: the economic value of intelligence will increasingly depend on the quality of the capital and infrastructure required to deliver it.
Three developments could reshape the next investment cycle.
First, the AI infrastructure market will become more selective. Investors may increasingly reward operational assets, credible counterparties and demonstrated returns rather than speculative construction pipelines.
Second, political and financial constraints could alter the geography of investment. Projects will increasingly compete on electricity availability, infrastructure costs, development certainty and financing resilience.
Third, the ownership of productive capacity may become more concentrated. Companies capable of sustaining investment through uncertainty may acquire infrastructure and customers that financially weaker competitors cannot retain.
The most consequential second-order effect is that temporary financial differences could become persistent competitive differences.
Companies that continue investing may strengthen their productive positions.
Those forced to delay projects may lose customers, technological relevance or financial flexibility.
But regulatory scrutiny can also create economic value when it prevents misallocated investment and produces infrastructure that communities can support.
The decisive question is whether the new political environment improves the productivity of capital—or restricts access to productive capital so severely that it reinforces existing market power.
For equity investors, the next opportunity is to distinguish companies benefiting from the construction of intelligence from those earning durable returns through its operation and deployment.
For credit investors, the priority is identifying who ultimately bears construction, utilization and refinancing risk.
For boards, the challenge is protecting financing flexibility while securing the physical and institutional capabilities necessary for future competitiveness.For Europe, the opportunity is to connect its industrial strengths with competitive energy infrastructure, disciplined financing and productive AI adoption.
The Great Split is entering a more consequential phase.
It will increasingly distinguish companies capable of transforming technological opportunity into productive cash flow from those whose investment ambitions exceed their financial and institutional capacity.
The next trillion dollars will not simply determine how much intelligence the world produces. It will influence where that intelligence can be deployed, who controls the productive assets and who captures their returns.
The next great rotation may therefore move beyond conventional technology-sector boundaries.
Toward infrastructure that can actually operate.
Toward financing that can actually endure.
Toward intelligence that can actually generate economic value.
In the Age of Velocity, technology creates possibility. Capital creates capacity. Political permission determines what can be built. But productive cash flow determines who ultimately captures the value.
The future will not belong to those who announce the largest AI investments. It will belong to those who can turn those investments into productive assets before the financial and political cost of building them overwhelms the returns.
