AI HAS MOVED FROM A PRODUCTIVITY STORY INTO THE INFLATION REACTION FUNCTION
The most consequential new signal comes from the Bank of England’s 17 September meeting and the Reserve Bank of Australia on 18 September. In both cases, AI is no longer being discussed primarily as a future productivity shock. Policymakers are now explicitly treating the AI build-out itself as a source of current inflation pressure.
That is an important escalation of the thesis we have been tracking.
The Bank of England has now put AI inside an actual rate vote
The BoE held Bank Rate at 3.75% by 6–3, but the three dissenters — Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill — wanted an immediate 25bp increase to 4%. Crucially, the minutes state that AI supply constraints were among the global factors adding inflation pressure. Greene specifically cited AI-related supply constraints as a source of persistent inflation; Pill said the broader AI build-out was magnifying energy and other global inflation risks. Even members voting to hold, including Sarah Breeden and Dave Ramsden, referred to AI-related global supply shocks as part of the upside inflation distribution. 
That makes this more consequential than a speech.
AI has entered the live monetary-policy debate.
The emerging chain is:
AI infrastructure demand
→ semiconductors + electricity + construction + capital demand
→ supply constraints
→ input-price inflation
→ second-round wage/price risk
→ tighter monetary policy.
This is exactly the opposite of the simplistic assumption that AI automatically produces lower inflation through productivity.
The BoE is simultaneously separating the short rate from the long end
The same meeting contained another important decision. The BoE adopted a multi-year QT plan that will unwind the remaining £368 billion of monetary-policy gilts at an average pace of £46 billion per year through 2034, with only £20 billion of annual active sales. The Bank estimates QT itself explains only about 20–30bp of the roughly 200bp rise in UK long-term term premia since 2022, and explicitly said it wants Bank Rate to remain the active policy instrument while making QT more predictable and less disruptive to bond markets. 
That has a second-order implication for the AI Supercycle.
Central banks may increasingly pursue a two-instrument architecture:
Short rate → control inflation
while
Balance-sheet policy → avoid unnecessary amplification of long-duration financing stress.
That matters enormously for an AI build-out increasingly dependent on long-dated debt, infrastructure finance and private credit.
The policy objective may therefore become:
tight enough at the front end to prevent AI-capital inflation from becoming generalized, but not so destabilizing at the long end that infrastructure capital formation collapses.
That is a much more sophisticated reaction function than the traditional “AI raises productivity, therefore rates eventually fall.”
⸻
Australia confirms the same mechanism
On 18 September, RBA Governor Michele Bullock told lawmakers that upside inflation risks were materialising and explicitly identified the AI boom as one of them.
Her formulation is particularly revealing: the global AI boom is producing stronger growth in economies central to the AI supply chain while simultaneously driving higher prices for supply-constrained AI technologies. Australia’s own data-centre investment boom is adding to domestic demand at the same time that oil prices remain elevated. Markets now assign roughly a 93% probability to another RBA hike at the 28–29 September meeting, from 4.35% to 4.60%. 
This gives us a much clearer global pattern.
A year ago, central banks were asking:
Will AI raise productivity?
They are now beginning to ask:
Can the world physically build AI fast enough without creating inflation first?
That is a fundamentally different question.
⸻
THE GREAT SPLIT HAS BECOME A MONETARY-POLICY SPLIT
The increasingly visible macroeconomic regime is:
AI-capable economies / sectors
→ capital expenditure→ employment demand in constrained skills
→ electricity demand
→ semiconductor demand
→ higher investment
→ higher productivity
while simultaneously:
non-AI / capital-sensitive sectors
→ higher interest rates
→ weaker investment
→ weaker hiring
→ margin pressure.
The same monetary stance can therefore be too loose for the AI economy and too tight for the Old Economy.
This is the Great Split moving directly into monetary transmission.
And it explains why employment data may become progressively harder to interpret.
Aggregate employment could appear stable while underneath it:
AI infrastructure / energy / engineering employment ↑
and
routine services / administrative / lower-productivity employment ↓.
Central banks will increasingly have to distinguish structural labour reallocation from cyclical weakness.
⸻
THE ECB NOW HAS AN EVEN HARDER PROBLEM
Euro-area consumer inflation expectations released on 18 September moved higher: one-year expectations rose to 3.0%, three-year expectations to 2.9%, and five-year expectations to 2.5%. The immediate cause is primarily the energy shock, not AI. But the timing matters because AI infrastructure is adding a second source of structural resource demand precisely as inflation expectations are becoming less comfortable. 
The European risk loop is therefore becoming:
**Energy shock
AI infrastructure demand
higher global capital costs
    → inflation expectations ↑
    → ECB tightening
    → European cost of capital ↑**
while Europe still captures less AI productivity than the United States.
That remains the most dangerous asymmetry in the Great Split:
EUROPE CAN IMPORT BOTH THE ENERGY SHOCK AND THE AI COST-OF-CAPITAL SHOCK BEFORE CAPTURING THE PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND.
⸻
INVESTOR IMPLICATION — THE HURDLE RATE IS BECOMING PHYSICAL
The central-bank evidence increasingly confirms that the critical metric for AI investments is no longer simply revenue growth.
It is:
AI RETURN ON INVESTED CAPITAL > AI-ADJUSTED COST OF CAPITAL
The companies best positioned are those able to convert AI investment into cash flow rapidly enough to absorb:
higher yields, rising electricity costs, supply-constrained chips, grid connection costs and greater debt requirements.
The dangerous category is becoming clearer:
**high AI capex
weak free cash flow
long duration
external financing dependence
constrained power access.**
That combination increasingly sits directly in the path of tighter central-bank reaction functions.
⸻
IMPLICATION FOR EU POLICY — ENERGY IS NOW MONETARY POLICY
The message for European policymakers is becoming unavoidable.
Europe cannot treat AI policy, energy policy, capital-markets policy and monetary policy as separate silos.
The causality is increasingly:
Insufficient power
→ AI infrastructure scarcity
→ higher marginal AI cost
→ inflation pressure
→ higher rates
→ higher WACC
→ slower AI diffusion
→ weaker productivity.
The positive loop is the reverse:
**Energy abundance
grids
European capital markets
AI infrastructure
    → adoption
    → productivity
    → falling unit costs
    → lower structural inflation
    → greater monetary-policy space.**
This is why electricity generation, grids, storage and the Savings and Investments Union are becoming components of the same European macroeconomic strategy.
⸻
THE VELOCITY EDGE — CENTRAL BANK INSIGHT
The decisive change this week is not another 25bp rate move.
It is the language of the reaction function.
The Federal Reserve has already associated AI capital formation with stronger investment and capital demand. The Bank of England is now explicitly discussing AI supply constraints and the AI build-out as inflation risks inside an MPC rate decision. The RBA is explicitly saying the AI boom is raising prices in supply-constrained technologies and strengthening demand in AI supply-chain economies. 
That gives us a much stronger general equation:
AI CAPITAL FORMATION
↓
COMPUTE + POWER + INFRASTRUCTURE + CAPITAL SCARCITY
↓
INFLATION / HIGHER R*
↓
MONETARY TIGHTENING
↓
SELECTION BY COST OF CAPITAL
↓
PRODUCTIVITY DIFFUSION
↓
INDUSTRIAL AI DEFLATIONThe world is still predominantly in the upper half of that chain.
That is the key point.
Industrial AI may ultimately be deflationary. Building the Industrial AI economy is proving inflationary first.
And central banks are beginning to respond accordingly.
