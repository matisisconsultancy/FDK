Spain Attracts a €7.4 Billion Proposal, a New AI Infrastructure Platform Emerges, and Europe’s Energy Shock Changes the Investment Equation
Five consequential developments have emerged in Europe’s AI infrastructure race. The most important is not another GPU announcement. It is the increasing economic value of electricity infrastructure that can actually support commercial operations.
Spain has attracted a proposed €7.4 billion data-center investment. A new European AI cloud platform has announced access to substantial power capacity and initial customer contracts. An Icelandic experiment has demonstrated the potential for flexible AI electricity consumption. A Gulf-backed consortium is entering Greece’s AI infrastructure market.
Meanwhile, European natural gas prices have exceeded €97/MWh for the first time since 2022.
The central investment thesis: Europe’s AI Gigafactory race is becoming a competition for bankable, power-ready infrastructure rather than simply announced computing capacity.
1 | SPAIN — A €7.4 BILLION PROJECT EXPOSES THE GRID BOTTLENECK
October 23 — New financing partner and revised development plan.
Omani-backed O-Green, working with Spanish developers, is reportedly supporting a proposed €7.4 billion, 570 MW data-center campus at San Mateo de Gállego, near Zaragoza.
The proposal replaces an earlier development arrangement involving Vantage Data Centers.
The project’s most revealing characteristic is its electricity position.
The first phase targets 90 MW and potentially could begin operating in 2029. Documentation identifies grid-access permissions totaling 90 MW.
Additional capacity has been requested, but the full 570 MW remains dependent on future allocations.
Environmental procedures are also preliminary. The €7.4 billion represents projected investment, not a verified financial close. 
Why this matters
Spain continues attracting international infrastructure capital because of its renewable-energy resources, land availability and growing digital ecosystem.
But the difference between 90 MW of documented grid access and a 570 MW development ambition reveals the central constraint.
Investment appetite is moving faster than electricity infrastructure.
This creates an important distinction between announced project value and deliverable computing capacity.
For investors, the relevant question is not whether a developer announces €7.4 billion.
It is how much of that investment can reach commercial operation with secured electricity, customers and financing.
For Spain’s Gigafactory consortium involving Telefónica, ACS and Santander, the broader competitive implication is significant: site readiness and electricity certainty may increasingly determine project economics.
The Zaragoza development is not a confirmed EuroHPC Gigafactory award.
2 | EUROPE — SWI LAUNCHES A NEW AI COMPUTE PLATFORM
October 26 — New operating platform, hardware orders and customer contracts.
European investment group SWI has launched SWI Compute, a dedicated AI cloud infrastructure business.
The group reports access to 3.6 GW of secured power capacity across 24 locations in Europe and the United States.
Its first major deployment is planned near Cambridge, United Kingdom.
The initial project involves 30 MW, approximately 7,000 NVIDIA Rubin GPUs and 3,500 Vera CPUs.
The broader Cambridge campus is planned to expand toward 530 MW.
SWI also reports approximately $1 billion in advanced hardware orders and contracts with initial customers. The first deployment is targeted for completion by the end of 2027.
The 3.6 GW figure represents the group’s reported international power portfolio, not operational European AI capacity. 
The hidden pattern
SWI illustrates an emerging business model.
Traditional infrastructure investors are moving beyond ownership of land and data centers toward integrated computing services.
POWER RIGHTS → COMPUTE INFRASTRUCTURE → CUSTOMER CONTRACTS → RECURRING REVENUESThe transition matters because infrastructure investors increasingly need to capture value from both physical assets and computing utilization.
The proposed model could strengthen European AI capital formation by combining real-asset investment expertise with commercial computing operations.
However, access to power does not automatically establish operational capacity, and announced hardware orders do not demonstrate future profitability.
Investment implication: Investors should increasingly value the combination of enforceable power rights, credible customers and financing durability.
3 | NORDICS — ICELAND DEMONSTRATES FLEXIBLE AI POWER DEMAND
October 23 — New operational evidence.
Emerald AI, NVIDIA and Nscale reported results from an experiment at Nscale’s Icelandic data center.
By integrating Emerald Conductor with NVIDIA’s DSX power-management technology, the companies reduced the electricity consumption of participating AI server racks by approximately 25% for 30 minutes before restoring normal operation.
The experiment demonstrates short-duration demand flexibility, not a permanent 25% reduction in energy consumption.
The companies have not established that the result can be generalized to all Gigafactory workloads or operating conditions. 
Why this matters
Europe has largely treated data-center electricity demand as an inflexible requirement.
This experiment points toward a different architecture.
FLEXIBLE COMPUTE → GRID STABILITY → GREATER USABLE CAPACITY → POTENTIALLY FASTER DEPLOYMENT
The second-order implication could be substantial.
If operators can reduce electricity demand temporarily during system stress, electricity networks may eventually be able to accommodate more computing capacity without requiring an equivalent increase in permanently reserved peak power.
This could improve the investment case in Germany, Italy and Spain, where grid-access constraints are important.
For utilities and policymakers, flexible computing could become part of the grid-planning framework.
For operators, the commercial challenge is demonstrating that demand flexibility does not materially undermine contracted computing performance.
Strategic implication: Europe’s AI infrastructure competitiveness increasingly depends on electricity productivity and flexibility—not electricity volume alone.
4 | GREECE — G42 ENTERS THE EUROPEAN AI INFRASTRUCTURE RACE
October 26 — New strategic partnership.
Abu Dhabi’s G42 has announced memoranda of understanding with Greece’s Copelouzos Group to explore AI infrastructure development.
The proposed industrial configuration involves G42 subsidiaries Khazna Data Centers and Core42.
Khazna would provide physical data-center development expertise, while Core42 would contribute cloud and computing capabilities.
No project location, investment amount or binding construction commitment has been announced. 
Why this matters
The significance extends beyond Greece.
Gulf capital and technology platforms are increasingly seeking positions in Europe’s AI infrastructure ecosystem.
That creates an additional source of potential financing, but also raises questions concerning governance, technology control, data sovereignty and regulatory compliance.
The emerging pattern is that European AI infrastructure may increasingly be financed through cross-border partnerships linking energy investors, infrastructure developers and global technology platforms.
For policymakers, attracting capital and preserving strategic autonomy must be treated as complementary objectives.
For European infrastructure groups, these partnerships offer potential opportunities to combine local market knowledge with international investment capacity.
5 | EUROPE — THE ENERGY SHOCK CHANGES GIGAFACTORY ECONOMICS
October 26 — European gas prices reach their highest level since 2022.
Benchmark European natural gas futures exceeded €97/MWh.
European storage was approximately 75% full, substantially below typical seasonal levels.
French month-ahead electricity futures also exceeded €200/MWh, according to Bloomberg’s October 26 reporting.This is the development with the broadest potential consequences for Europe’s Gigafactory economics.
The transmission mechanism is:
ENERGY PRICE SHOCK → HIGHER OPERATING-COST RISK → LOWER EXPECTED MARGINS → TOUGHER FINANCING CONDITIONS → GREATER PROJECT SELECTIVITY
The severity of the effect depends on each project’s electricity procurement structure.
Facilities with fixed-price long-term power agreements, dedicated low-carbon supply or effective hedges may be substantially better protected than operators exposed to wholesale prices.
For investors, this creates a stronger incentive to distinguish between announced megawatts and economically secured megawatts.
For CEOs and boards, energy procurement becomes a central capital-allocation decision.
For European policymakers, the latest energy shock reinforces the importance of coordinating digital infrastructure policy with electricity generation, network development and industrial competitiveness.

FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS
1. INVESTORS — Power readiness becomes a valuation differentiator.
A project with secured electricity, contracted customers and financing certainty may possess greater economic value than a larger project still dependent on future grid allocations.
2. EUROPEAN BANKS — Energy-price sensitivity belongs at the center of underwriting.
AI infrastructure credit assessments should incorporate power-contract duration, electricity-price exposure, customer concentration and hardware replacement requirements.
3. CEOs AND BOARDS — Infrastructure integration is becoming essential.
The winning industrial model may combine power procurement, computing capacity, cloud services and customer relationships within one commercially coherent operating system.
4. INDUSTRIAL AI — Flexibility creates new capacity.
The Icelandic results suggest a potentially important route toward increasing productive computing capacity without proportionate expansion of peak electricity requirements.
5. POLICYMAKERS — Capital formation must be coordinated with energy infrastructure.
European Gigafactory funding will be more effective when projects demonstrate executable power arrangements, credible demand and financial resilience.
THE BIG TAKE
The European AI infrastructure race is revealing a deeper economic transformation.
Electricity is moving from an operating expense to a strategic determinant of capital formation.
Access to advanced semiconductors remains essential. Financing remains indispensable.
But neither can generate productive intelligence without deliverable electricity.
The result is an emerging hierarchy of economic value.
ANNOUNCED CAPACITY < SECURED POWER < OPERATIONAL COMPUTE < PROFITABLE INTELLIGENCEEurope’s competitive opportunity lies in integrating all four stages.
The latest developments demonstrate both sides of that opportunity.
Spain continues attracting substantial investment proposals. European infrastructure investors are developing integrated cloud platforms. The Nordics are demonstrating new approaches to power flexibility.
Yet the latest energy-price shock makes the financial distinction between executable and speculative capacity increasingly consequential.
Velocity Insight: Europe’s next AI infrastructure champions will not simply control the most megawatts. They will control the megawatts capable of generating the highest sustainable returns.
