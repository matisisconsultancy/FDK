U.S. jobs contract. Chinese household inflation weakens. Equities sit at records. European profits accelerate. Nvidia moves into power infrastructure. The global economy is not slowing uniformly — capital formation and labor formation are moving in opposite directions.
The Fundamental Question
What if the next economic regime is neither recession nor soft landing, but accelerating capital formation inside an economy that requires progressively less incremental labor?
That is the question markets should carry into Monday.
Friday delivered one of the clearest manifestations yet of The Great Split.
The United States lost 23,000 jobs in July, against expectations for an 80,000 increase. May and June were revised down by another 103,000 jobs, leaving average monthly employment growth over the past three months at only 20,000. Yet the S&P 500 closed at a record 7,757.64, gaining 0.62% on Friday and 3.58% over the week; the Nasdaq gained 5.19% for the week.
Then came this morning’s Chinese inflation data.
China’s July CPI slowed to just 0.5% year-on-year, its weakest reading in six months. Core inflation was only 0.9%, food prices fell 1.5%, and producer inflation slowed from 4.1% to 3.5%. Yet just two days earlier China reported July export growth of 23.9%, high-tech exports up 40.7% year to date, and a $112.5 billion monthly trade surplus.
Two economies are increasingly visible inside both the United States and China.
One is driven by employment, household demand and conventional consumption.
The other is driven by AI, industrial investment, exports, infrastructure, capital intensity and productivity.
Velocity Interpretation
The defining divergence may no longer be simply between winners and losers within financial markets.
It is becoming a divergence between two different mechanisms of economic growth.
Labor formation is slowing.
Capital formation is accelerating.
That distinction could become the central macroeconomic variable of the Age of Industrial AI.

The Five Overnight Signals
Wall Street is beginning to price labor weakness as a financial asset
The U.S. lost 23,000 jobs in July. The unemployment rate nevertheless fell from 4.2% to 4.1% because 264,000 people exited the labor force.Yet equities rallied because weaker employment reduced the probability of Fed tightening.
That is the first major signal.
Markets are treating labor weakness as a lower discount rate rather than a lower earnings event.
For now.
Velocity Interpretation
This is The Great Split in market form:
employment ↓ rate expectations ↓ equity valuations ↑
The traditional relationship between Main Street momentum and asset prices is weakening.
⸻
China now has its own Great Split
China’s July exports increased 23.9%, supported by exceptionally strong demand for high-tech products; high-tech exports are up 40.7% year to date, while semiconductor exports have nearly doubled.
Yet July CPI inflation slowed to 0.5%, core inflation to 0.9%, and food prices declined 1.5%.
Velocity Interpretation
China is developing an increasingly clear K-shaped productive structure.
On the upper arm:
AI infrastructure semiconductors advanced manufacturing electrical equipment automobiles industrial exports.
On the lower arm:
household consumption property traditional manufacturing domestic pricing power.
China can therefore become more powerful industrially while remaining weaker domestically.
That creates a second fundamental ratio:
Export Velocity / Domestic Demand Velocity
The wider that ratio becomes, the more pressure China will place on international industrial markets.
⸻
Nvidia may be crossing the boundary from selling accelerated computing to owning its bottlenecks
Nvidia is reportedly preparing to invest as much as $3 billion in Lancium, the developer behind the first operational Stargate data-center site in Abilene, Texas.
The phrase that matters is not $3 billion.
It is:
grid hookups.
Yesterday’s scarcity:
GPU capacity.
Today’s scarcity:
HBM + networking + electricity.
Tomorrow’s scarcity:
grid connection + permitted land + firm power + cooling + financing capacity.
If Nvidia’s reported move proceeds, it would represent something larger than conventional vertical integration.
It is bottleneck integration.
The chip company becomes an infrastructure company.
The infrastructure company becomes an energy company.
The energy company becomes part of the intelligence supply chain.
⸻
The AI boom has moved from CapEx to fixed financial commitments
Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, Amazon and Alphabet have disclosed approximately $1.09 trillion in payments under leases that have not yet commenced, mainly associated with data centers.
Including additional Meta agreements signed after quarter-end increases the known pipeline to around $1.16 trillion.
This changes the analytical framework for valuing AI.
The key question is no longer:
How much are hyperscalers spending?
It is:
How much future cash flow has already been pre-committed to infrastructure whose utilization has not yet been proven?
That gives us the third critical ratio:
Incremental AI Cash Flow / Fixed AI Commitments
AI is converting companies historically valued as asset-light platforms into increasingly asset-heavy industrial systems.
High fixed cost.
Low marginal cost.
Huge economies of scale.
High utilization requirements.
And potentially brutal economics if utilization falls.
AI is industrializing the technology balance sheet.
⸻
The market is still treating Hormuz as a negotiation. The physical economy cannot afford to.
Iran said Saturday that an agreement with Oman concerning the Strait of Hormuz was close, but that such an agreement alone would not reopen the waterway.
Markets are trading the probability of diplomacy.
Industrial companies must manage the probability of physical disruption.
Those are different risk functions.
This distinction is particularly important for Europe.
Europe’s Q2 corporate earnings are projected to rise 22.4%, with ex-energy earnings up a still-strong 11.5%.
But energy profits are expected to surge 135.8%.
That is another manifestation of The Great Split:
higher energy profits / higher consumer energy costs.
stronger corporate earnings / weaker household purchasing power.
record equities / greater macroeconomic fragility.

AI deflation is producing infrastructure inflation
Marginal Intelligence Cost ↓↓↓
while
Fixed Infrastructure Cost ↑↑↑
The lower the price of intelligence becomes, the more applications become economically viable.
More applications generate more tokens.
More tokens require more accelerated computing.
More accelerated computing requires more electricity.
More electricity requires more generation, transmission and grid connections.
So falling AI prices do not necessarily reduce capital requirements.
They can increase them.
This is Jevons’ paradox applied to intelligence.

Sovereign capital is moving toward ownership of productive capacity
Sovereign capital is migrating from passive portfolio ownership toward direct ownership of:
compute energy land data centers semiconductors industrial AI infrastructure.
This is not simply asset allocation.
It increasingly resembles national positioning inside the productive architecture of the Next Economy.

Europe may have a larger Industrial AI opportunity than its software rankings suggest
Europe does not need to defeat every U.S. frontier model to become strategically relevant to AI.
Its opportunity may lie in the physical layer:
power equipment grid technologies industrial automation cooling optics transport infrastructure semiconductor equipment energy systems advanced manufacturing.
Industrial AI requires an industrial base.
Europe already owns parts of it.
The policy problem is one of orchestration, scale and capital formation.

The Fundamental Analytics
Three ratios deserve to move onto every institutional dashboard.
Capital Formation / Labor Formation
If AI investment keeps accelerating while employment formation weakens, an economy can simultaneously produce stronger profits and greater social divergence.
This is the core macroeconomic ratio of The Great Split.
Incremental AI Cash Flow / Fixed AI Commitments
With roughly $1.16 trillion of known future lease commitments already contracted across five technology giants, cash-flow conversion becomes as important as revenue growth.
AI utilization will increasingly determine financial returns.
Intelligence Produced / Dollar × Watt × Unit of Memory
The competitive frontier is shifting away from model size alone.
The winning architecture will increasingly be the one capable of producing the greatest useful intelligence for the lowest combination of:
capital cost, energy consumption, memory intensity, and inference cost.
That is the economics of scale dimensionally transformed.

Implications for Investors
The market should increasingly distinguish between AI exposure and AI economic quality.
The first phase rewarded thematic participation.
The next phase will reward superior economics.
The critical assets increasingly include:
grid-connected land, firm power, transformers, memory, optical infrastructure, cooling systems, semiconductor equipment, contracted data-center capacity.
But investors should simultaneously become much more demanding about cash-flow conversion.
Scarcity deserves a premium. Capacity alone does not.

Revenue per MW

Infrastructure utilization
The CEO question is no longer merely:

It becomes:Where does AI increase return on invested capital faster than it increases the capital required to operate the business?

Implications for Policymakers
The coming monetary-policy dilemma is becoming increasingly difficult.
U.S. employment formation is weakening sharply, while inflation remains materially above target.
That creates an unconventional combination:
**weak labor formation
persistent inflation
extraordinary capital formation.**
For Europe, the policy agenda must also move beyond subsidies to individual AI models.
Industrial intelligence requires:
energy × grids × accelerated computing × memory × connectivity × capital markets.
The states capable of orchestrating those elements will increasingly determine where intelligence is produced.

The Velocity Insight
The market has spent much of the AI era asking one question:
How intelligent can machines become?
The economically decisive question is now different:
How much capital will be required to produce that intelligence — and how much labor will still be required once it is produced?
The answers appear to be diverging.
More capital.
Less incremental labor.
At the same time:
intelligence becomes cheaper, infrastructure becomes more expensive, corporate profits rise, employment formation slows, and productive assets become more concentrated.
Those are not separate phenomena.
They are increasingly one causal system.
This is why The Great Split should no longer be understood merely as a description of inequality or market polarization.
It is becoming a capital regime.
A regime in which the economy can become simultaneously:
more productive, more profitable, more capital-intensive, and less labor-intensive.
That is not a contradiction.
It may be the defining economic architecture of the Age of Industrial AI.
And the strategic agenda for Monday morning is therefore clear:
Do not ask simply where growth is accelerating. Ask who owns the capital required to produce it — and who is becoming less necessary in the process.
That is where the next redistribution of economic power begins.
