The most consequential number of the morning is not the Federal Reserve’s expected 25-basis-point increase.
It is:
2.16 GIGAWATTS.
Anthropic has signed its first Australian data-center lease, covering capacity at a planned 2.16-GW campus about 250 kilometers from Brisbane, according to Reuters. The project is expected to begin coming online in 2027. Crucially, the facility is intended for inference rather than training. 
That announcement arrives days after Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei called for a slower pace of frontier-model capability development—an idea supported by OpenAI’s Sam Altman and xAI’s Elon Musk. This morning European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen backed efforts to “pace the frontier” and said she would convene leading AI laboratories to discuss model evaluation, verification and security. Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg, however, explicitly rejected the need for coordinated slowing, arguing that competition and liability already give companies sufficient incentives to build safely. 
At exactly the same time, the physical AI economy continues to expand.
The U.S. 10-year Treasury is around 5%. Markets assign about a 92.5% probability to a Fed hike tonight. Brent remains near $108 per barrel despite easing this morning. European stocks are modestly higher after two difficult sessions. And SK Hynix is discussing with Intel the possibility of manufacturing memory in the United States for the first time as the AI boom produces acute memory scarcity. 
The deeper signal is emerging clearly:
**FRONTIER VELOCITY CAN SLOW
WHILE DEPLOYMENT VELOCITY ACCELERATES.**
That may define the next phase of the AI Supercycle.

FIVE SIGNALS SHAPING THE NEXT ECONOMY
1. THE AI SUPERCYCLE IS ROTATING FROM TRAINING TOWARD INFERENCE
Verified facts. Anthropic’s Australian agreement covers capacity at a planned 2.16-GW data-center campus near Brisbane. The site is expected to start operating in 2027 and would use renewable power arrangements and closed-loop air cooling. Reuters reports that the Anthropic capacity would be used for inference, not training. The project still requires approval from Australia’s Foreign Investment Review Board. 
Australia is already attracting a broader AI infrastructure wave: economists cited by Reuters estimate data-center investment could reach A$150 billion by 2030. 
FDK interpretation.
This is the crucial distinction.
The market has spent the past week asking whether calls to slow frontier-model development threaten AI infrastructure demand.
Anthropic itself is now providing part of the answer.
**SLOWER FRONTIER TRAINING
DOES NOT MEAN SLOWER AI DEPLOYMENT.**
The causality chain may instead become:
**FRONTIER MODELS
→ ENTERPRISE ADOPTION
→ AGENTIC AI
→ BILLIONS OF INFERENCES
→ ACCELERATED COMPUTING
→ DATA CENTERS
→ ELECTRICITY.**
That would fundamentally broaden the Supercycle.
Training is concentrated among a handful of laboratories.
Inference propagates through the entire economy.
If that transition accelerates, the largest economic opportunity may no longer lie solely in producing the next model.
It may lie in producing the lowest-cost reliable intelligence per transaction.
That means:
COST PER TOKEN AT INDUSTRIAL SCALE
becomes increasingly important.

2. MEMORY IS BECOMING A SOVEREIGN ASSET
Verified facts. SK Hynix is discussing a potential agreement with Intel that could involve manufacturing memory chips in the United States for the first time. One option would involve leasing part of Intel’s long-delayed Ohio facility; another could involve a venture linking SK Hynix, Intel and major cloud companies seeking secure memory supply. Talks are exploratory and no decision has been reached. 
SK Hynix is the leading supplier of high-bandwidth memory, a critical component of AI accelerators. Intel shares rose 5.2% pre-market, while SK Hynix closed 4.1% higher in Seoul. Washington is pressuring foreign semiconductor producers to build more capacity inside the United States, while South Korea is simultaneously seeking to retain strategically sensitive chip manufacturing at home. 
FDK interpretation.
The AI chip race has moved beyond GPUs.
The bottleneck chain has become:
**GPU
→ PACKAGING
→ HBM
→ FAB LOCATION
→ NATIONAL SECURITY.**
Once high-bandwidth memory becomes scarce enough, the location of memory production becomes strategic.
That produces a new form of industrial geography.
The question is no longer simply:
Who designs the best accelerator?
It becomes:
WHO CONTROLS THE FULL PHYSICAL STACK REQUIRED TO FEED IT?
Memory.
Packaging.
Lithography.
Power.
Networking.
Cooling.
Fabrication.
This is why the possible Intel–SK Hynix arrangement matters far beyond Intel’s Ohio economics.
It suggests that accelerated-computing sovereignty is spreading upstream through the semiconductor chain.

4. EUROPE’S AI PROBLEM IS NOW VISIBLY AN ENERGY-MARKET PROBLEM
Verified facts. Europe plans to create seven large AI computing hubs, with 18 EU governments bidding to host them and collectively pledging around €3 billion in future computing purchases. By comparison, six largely U.S.-based hyperscalers are expected to spend more than $1.3 trillion by 2027, according to S&P Global Ratings figures cited by Reuters. 
Europe’s structural disadvantage is energy. Large EU industrial consumers paid an average of roughly $107/MWh in 2025, more than twice the U.S. level and about 57% above China. About 40% of European distribution grids are more than 40 years old, and the European Commission estimates that €584 billion of grid investment will be required by 2030. A data center can be constructed in roughly two years, while connection to Europe’s electricity network can take as long as seven. 
The geography already reflects these economics. Of 68 major EU data-center projects announced since 2024, 43 are concentrated in France, Spain, Finland and Sweden. A 100-MW facility is estimated to incur annual electricity costs of roughly €254 million in Germany, €153 million in Spain and only €91 million in Finland. 
FDK interpretation.
This is one of the clearest examples yet of the New Geography of AI.
Europe does not merely have an AI funding problem.
It has an electron-allocation problem.
The continent’s AI architecture increasingly follows:
**CHEAP ELECTRICITY
→ GRID AVAILABILITY
→ DATA CENTERS
→ ACCELERATED COMPUTING
→ CAPITAL FORMATION
→ PRODUCTIVITY.**
That explains Google’s €13 billion Finland investment.
It explains why northern Europe is becoming disproportionately important.
And it explains why Europe cannot treat energy policy, AI policy and capital-market policy as separate dossiers.
They are now one strategy.
Without a functioning continental electricity market, Europe’s ambition to become an AI power risks becoming seven gigafactories looking for electrons.

5. THE ENERGY SHOCK HAS MIGRATED FROM CRUDE INTO THE REAL ECONOMY
Verified facts. Brent has eased about 0.6% today toward $108 per barrel after Saudi Arabia offered additional crude via Oman and U.S. industry data showed a 7.1-million-barrel increase in domestic crude inventories. But refined-product markets remain exceptionally tight. Asian 10-ppm diesel refining margins have exceeded $87 per barrel, versus roughly $22 before the war, while near-term diesel spreads are above $11 per barrel.Asian refiners are increasing throughput and shipping diesel as far as Africa to replace disrupted supplies, while concerns over Saudi crude availability are beginning to raise the risk premium even in Asian markets. 
FDK interpretation.
This is precisely why looking only at Brent can be misleading.
The physical-economy shock increasingly travels through:
**CRUDE
→ REFINING
→ DIESEL
→ FREIGHT
→ LOGISTICS
→ FOOD
→ INDUSTRIAL COSTS
→ INFLATION.**
The margin between crude and diesel is telling us that scarcity has migrated downstream.
This matters for monetary policy.
A temporary easing in crude does not eliminate inflation risk if refined-product scarcity remains extreme.
And it matters for AI.
Because the AI Supercycle is now competing for physical capital inside an economy where:
transportation,
construction,
grid equipment,
backup generation
and infrastructure logistics
are becoming more expensive.
The distinction is critical:
**INTELLIGENCE MAY BE DEFLATIONARY.
THE BUILD-OUT REQUIRED TO INDUSTRIALIZE IT IS NOT YET DEFLATIONARY.**

THE HIDDEN PATTERNS BEYOND THE HEADLINES

This is today’s central insight.
Anthropic can simultaneously argue for slower recursive model improvement and reserve capacity inside a 2.16-GW inference campus.
There is no contradiction once the AI stack is separated into layers.
Frontier training determines the maximum capability available.
Inference determines how widely that capability propagates through the economy.
The next phase can therefore look like:
**SLOWER CAPABILITY VELOCITY
FASTER PROPAGATION VELOCITY.**
This would be profoundly important.
Because the economic impact of AI does not ultimately depend on how often a frontier model is retrained.

The market has spent three years valuing the creation of intelligence.

Slow the frontier.
Build 2.16 gigawatts.
Those two actions initially appear incompatible.
They are not.
They reveal where the AI Supercycle may be going next.
For three years, the center of gravity was the race to create intelligence.
The next decade may be dominated by the race to deploy it.
Into factories.
Cars.
Railways.
Banks.
Hospitals.
Cities.
Scientific laboratories.
Energy systems.
Corporate workflows.Autonomous agents.
And billions of everyday decisions.
That requires a different infrastructure architecture.
Not merely ever-larger training clusters.
But a distributed industrial network capable of producing inference cheaply, securely and continuously.
The Great Split therefore moves again.
Not:
AI vs. non-AI.
Not:
frontier acceleration vs. frontier slowdown.

**INTELLIGENCE CREATION

Because models can slow while inference accelerates.
