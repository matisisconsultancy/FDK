CHINA REVEALS THE OTHER SIDE OF THE AI SUPERCYCLE

20 September 2026 | FDK

The most consequential new development is coming from China, and it materially sharpens the framework we have been building.

Until now, the dominant central-bank problem has been:

AI capital formation → electricity + infrastructure + credit demand → inflation pressure → higher neutral rates.

China is showing the mirror image.

On 19 September, Huang Yiping, a member of the People’s Bank of China’s Monetary Policy Committee, warned that AI could actually worsen China’s structural imbalance between strong supply and weak domestic demand. AI is already strengthening Chinese exports and productive capacity, but if consumption does not keep pace, faster automation and productivity could intensify excess supply rather than generate inflation. 

That gives us a much more powerful formulation of the Great Split:

THE SAME AI SHOCK CAN BE INFLATIONARY IN CAPITAL-SCARCE ECONOMIES AND DEFLATIONARY IN DEMAND-SCARCE ECONOMIES.

This is important because it means there may be no single global AI monetary regime.

⸻

THE U.S.–CHINA MONETARY DIVERGENCE IS BECOMING STRUCTURAL

In the United States, the dominant sequence increasingly looks like:

AI investment
→ data centres
→ electricity
→ semiconductors
→ debt issuance
→ stronger capital demand
→ higher yields / higher R*.

In China, Huang is identifying a different sequence:

AI adoption
→ productivity + manufacturing capacity
→ exports + supply expansion
→ weak domestic absorption
→ excess capacity
→ downward price pressure.

That is a major conceptual advance.

**America may experience AI first as a capital-demand shock.

China may experience AI first as a supply shock.**

And central banks would logically react very differently.

⸻

TODAY’S PBOC DECISION FITS THAT PATTERN

China kept its benchmark Loan Prime Rates unchanged on 20 September for the sixteenth consecutive month:

1-year LPR: 3.00%
5-year LPR: 3.50%.

The decision reflects competing pressures. Domestic credit demand remains weak, but policymakers have limited room to ease aggressively because the Fed has moved in the opposite direction, the U.S.–China yield gap is close to historical extremes, bank margins remain constrained, and China appears to be moving from outright deflation toward mild inflation. 

That means the PBOC faces an unusual AI-era dilemma:

too much easing risks currency and capital-market pressure;

too little easing leaves domestic demand too weak to absorb the productivity and capacity AI is creating.

This is essentially the inverse of the Fed’s problem.

The Fed worries that AI investment demand is too strong.

China increasingly worries that AI-enabled supply may be too strong relative to demand.

⸻

A NEW GENERAL THEORY OF AI AND INFLATION

We can now refine the central-bank framework considerably.

AI does not have an intrinsically inflationary or deflationary effect.

Its monetary effect depends on where the bottleneck lies.

Regime 1 — Scarcity Economy

If the constraint is:

capital + electricity + GPUs + grids + construction

then:

AI → investment boom → scarcity → inflation → higher rates.

This increasingly describes parts of the United States, UK and Europe.

Regime 2 — Demand-Constrained Economy

If the constraint is:

consumption + credit demand + household confidence

then:

AI → productivity → excess supply → lower prices → easier monetary conditions required.

That increasingly describes China.

This is a much more useful way to think about Industrial AI deflation than asking simply whether AI is inflationary or deflationary.

⸻

EUROPE SITS DANGEROUSLY BETWEEN THE TWO REGIMES

This development is particularly important for Europe.

An IMF paper presented to EU finance ministers this weekend estimates that AI could lift European productivity by roughly 1% over five years, but warns that uneven adoption could increase divergence across European economies and that energy infrastructure is becoming a major constraint.Around 60% of workers in advanced European economies are in occupations highly exposed to AI. 

The IMF is therefore reinforcing the same policy architecture now emerging from the ECB debate:

AI policy cannot be separated from energy, capital markets, labour markets and the Single Market.

The danger for Europe is that it could simultaneously suffer:

American-style scarcity inflation

without achieving enough

Chinese-style manufacturing productivity

or

American-style AI capital formation.

That would be the worst combination.

⸻

THE INVESTOR IMPLICATION

The macro framework is becoming two-dimensional.

Investors should increasingly ask two questions about every AI economy and sector:

1. WHERE IS THE BOTTLENECK?

Capital? Power? Compute? Labour? Demand?

2. WHERE DOES THE PRODUCTIVITY SURPLUS GO?

Into:

higher margins, higher wages, lower prices, more investment—or excess capacity?

That second question may become decisive.

AI productivity does not automatically create corporate value.

If AI accelerates supply faster than demand, productivity can become deflationary competition.

China is the first major economy where a central-bank policymaker is explicitly articulating that risk.

⸻

THE VELOCITY EDGE — CENTRAL BANK INSIGHT

The central-bank debate has therefore moved one step further.

The old question was:

Will AI raise productivity?

Then:

Will AI be inflationary before it becomes deflationary?

The new question is:

WHERE DOES AI CREATE SCARCITY—AND WHERE DOES IT CREATE SURPLUS?

That determines the monetary reaction function.

The emerging global architecture is:

UNITED STATES
AI → Capital Scarcity → Higher R*

CHINA
AI → Supply Abundance → Deflation Risk

EUROPE
AI → Infrastructure Scarcity + Slow Diffusion → Competitiveness Risk

And this may ultimately become one of the most important dimensions of the Great Split.

**AI does not create one global business cycle.

It is beginning to create different monetary regimes inside the same global economy.**

That is today’s material inflection.
