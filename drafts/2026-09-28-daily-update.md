The Evidence
The overnight signal is increasingly clear: AI velocity remains strong, but the financial hurdle is rising faster.
China delivered the most important new measured datapoint. Industrial profits rose only 4.2% y/y in August, down from 11.2% in July, while January–August growth slowed to 15.7% from 17.6%. Yet profits in computer, communications and electronics manufacturing remained up 110% over the first eight months. The AI economy is accelerating even as the broader industrial economy loses momentum. 
Meanwhile, the cost of capital moved against the cycle. Brent rose 1.6% to about $106, the US 30-year Treasury yield reached 5.5185%, and markets price a 66% probability of another Fed hike in October. Japan’s Nikkei gained 0.8%, South Korea fell 0.6%, and broader Asia-Pacific equities slipped 0.2%. These are provisional market signals, not yet closing-price inputs to the GVI. 
AI is still accelerating. Conversion is becoming harder.

Institutional Interpretation
The emerging divide is no longer simply AI leaders versus laggards.
It is becoming a divide between economies and companies that can convert AI intensity into productivity and cash flow—and those that cannot.
China illustrates the pattern perfectly: electronics profits +110%; aggregate industrial-profit momentum sharply weaker. The United States shows the mirror image: AI-related capital formation remains powerful, but oil and long-term yields are steadily raising the price of every future dollar of return. 
For investors, the preference shifts toward scarcity assets with current cash flow—power, memory, networking, cooling and high-utilisation infrastructure—rather than long-duration capacity promises.
For boards, AI investment should now be judged through one chain:
CAPACITY → UTILISATION → PRODUCTIVITY → CASH FLOW → ROIC > WACC
For policymakers, the competitive frontier is increasingly energy + capital + adoption, not models alone.
