China delivered the most important new measured datapoint, illustrating an economy where the AI engine accelerates even as the broader industrial base loses momentum. Meanwhile, the cost of capital has moved against the cycle.

::section The Evidence | What the overnight tape shows
China's industrial profits rose only 4.2% y/y in August, down from 11.2% in July, while January–August growth slowed to 15.7% from 17.6%. Yet profits in computer, communications and electronics manufacturing remained up 110% over the first eight months. The AI economy is accelerating even as the broader industrial economy loses momentum.

The cost of capital moved against the cycle. Brent rose 1.6% to about $106, the US 30-year Treasury yield reached 5.5185%, and markets price a 66% probability of another Fed hike in October. Japan's Nikkei gained 0.8%, South Korea fell 0.6%, and broader Asia-Pacific equities slipped 0.2%. These are provisional market signals, not yet closing-price inputs to the GVI.

::section Institutional Interpretation | Where the real divide is opening
The emerging divide is no longer simply AI leaders versus laggards. It is becoming a divide between economies and companies that can convert AI intensity into productivity and cash flow — and those that cannot.

China illustrates the pattern perfectly: electronics profits +110%; aggregate industrial-profit momentum sharply weaker. The United States shows the mirror image: AI-related capital formation remains powerful, but oil and long-term yields are steadily raising the price of every future dollar of return.

For investors, the preference shifts toward ==scarcity assets with current cash flow== — power, memory, networking, cooling and high-utilisation infrastructure — rather than long-duration capacity promises.

For boards, AI investment should now be judged through one chain: **CAPACITY → UTILISATION → PRODUCTIVITY → CASH FLOW → ROIC > WACC**.

For policymakers, the competitive frontier is increasingly energy + capital + adoption, not models alone.
