The Commission explicitly states that its potential-output estimates do not incorporate an adjustment for AI-driven total factor productivity.
This is the central distinction between expected technological transformation and official macroeconomic forecasts.
Europe’s exports are benefiting from AI-related global demand for industrial machinery and technology inputs, while domestic investment in AI remains comparatively weak. Elevated energy costs and higher financing rates continue to weigh on the investment outlook.
Meanwhile, new UK productivity data released November 17 report output per hour increasing 2.5% year on year in the third quarter, supported by 2.0% growth in gross value added and a 0.5% decline in hours worked. This is evidence of stronger measured productivity, but not proof that AI caused the improvement. Economy and Finance +1
IV. THE HIDDEN PATTERN — EUROPE MAY IMPORT AI FINANCIAL RISK BEFORE CAPTURING THE PRODUCTIVITY RETURN
Three mechanisms are converging:
CAPITAL CHANNEL
European long-term savings → US AI bonds → American infrastructure investment
CONTAGION CHANNEL
US AI earnings disappointment → European credit repricing → tighter investment financing
PRODUCTIVITY CHANNEL
Slow European industrial adoption + high energy costs → weaker domestic productivity → limited capacity to absorb higher real rates
This is a potential source of the Great Split: Europe could remain exposed to the financial risks of the AI Supercycle while capturing a smaller proportion of its industrial returns.
The monetary implication is not necessarily an imminent policy reversal. It is that the ECB may confront stronger global capital-demand pressures without a corresponding increase in European potential growth.
V. IMPLICATIONS FOR INVESTORS AND EU POLICYMAKERS
For investors, the emerging priority is to examine exposure to US hyperscalers across both equity and euro credit portfolios, particularly long-dated bonds. Credit diversification should be evaluated against common sensitivity to AI earnings expectations, not merely issuer ratings or sector labels.
For EU policymakers, deeper capital markets remain essential, but they must finance domestic productive assets as well as foreign technology issuers. Energy infrastructure, industrial AI deployment, workforce adjustment and competitive access to growth capital should be treated as connected priorities.
For central-bank supervisors, concentration risk, maturity structures and correlations across asset classes increasingly deserve joint surveillance.

THE BIG TAKE
Three consequential developments on November 16–17 reveal a new phase in the monetary and financial evolution of the AI Supercycle.
The ECB has documented how US technology companies are increasing their presence in Europe’s long-duration credit markets. Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh has introduced three explicit AI macroeconomic scenarios into the Fed’s analytical preparations. And the European Commission’s new forecasts expose a structural contradiction: Europe faces elevated inflation and weak productivity growth while relying increasingly on AI-related global investment to support industrial exports.
The hidden pattern is a potential transatlantic transfer of capital, risk and productive returns.
Europe is helping finance American AI infrastructure through its bond markets, but has yet to demonstrate comparable economy-wide AI productivity gains of its own.

This research advances yesterday’s ECB warning about competition between sovereign debt and AI financing.
Long-dated euro investment-grade credit is becoming more exposed to the American technology investment cycle. The ECB also identifies greater sensitivity of European credit spreads to US equity-market movements, potentially reducing the diversification benefits of holding both equities and investment-grade bonds.
The crucial mismatch is between long-duration liabilities and rapidly depreciating AI equipment.
Long-term debt remains outstanding regardless of whether successive generations of chips, data centres and software platforms generate their anticipated revenues.
The ECB finds only limited early evidence of investors shifting portfolios toward hyperscaler bonds, not proof of widespread crowding out. But it warns that additional issuance could increasingly compete with other borrowers and exert upward pressure on long-term sovereign yields. European Central Bank
The second-order implication: a reassessment of American AI revenues could simultaneously affect European corporate credit spreads, pension portfolios and the financing environment for sovereigns.
