The Fundamental Question
Can the AI infrastructure cycle continue to justify rising debt, capex and project-finance commitments if returns on incremental capital fail to outpace the full cost of financing, power and execution?
The AI investment cycle remains extraordinarily strong. But its financing structure is changing.
Big Tech could invest more than $6 trillion in AI infrastructure through 2030. Morgan Stanley estimates AI-related global debt issuance could approach $570 billion in 2026, while Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta alone are expected to deploy roughly $700 billion this year.
At the same time, banks are tightening the conditions under which data-centre projects become financeable. Permitting, zoning, electricity availability, community opposition and construction readiness are increasingly entering credit decisions directly.
At least 75 U.S. data-centre projects worth approximately $130 billion faced local opposition in the first quarter alone.
The implication is financial, not technological:
AI does not have a shortage of capital. It has an emerging shortage of projects capable of converting capital into cash flows at returns above their full economic cost.
That is the new phase of the AI Supercycle.

Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy
AI Is Moving From a Capex Cycle to a Financing Cycle
For the first phase of the AI boom, capital expenditure itself was interpreted as evidence of strategic strength.
The larger the investment programme, the stronger the signal of expected demand.
That relationship is becoming less automatic.
As hyperscaler spending approaches hundreds of billions of dollars annually, internal cash generation is increasingly supplemented by:
corporate bonds;
project finance;
private credit;
leases;
infrastructure funds;
and sovereign capital.
The economic consequence is fundamental.
Once external financing becomes a larger component of the AI production function, the relevant benchmark is no longer simply revenue growth.
It becomes:
Incremental ROIC − incremental WACC.
A company creates economic value only when the first remains sustainably above the second.
The AI Supercycle is therefore moving from a race to deploy capital toward a race to earn on capital.
⸻
Project Bankability Is Becoming a New AI Bottleneck
Banks financing U.S. data centres are increasingly incorporating permitting, zoning, environmental approvals, electricity availability and community opposition into lending decisions.Some financing structures now restrict construction drawdowns until permits and leases have been secured.
This changes the causal chain:
capital committed → permits secured → power contracted → financing released → construction completed → capacity energized → utilization achieved → cash flow generated.
Each additional step introduces:
duration risk;
execution risk;
interest expense;
and potential impairment.
A delayed permit is therefore not simply an administrative inconvenience.
It changes project NPV.
A delayed grid connection increases capitalized interest.
A delayed energization postpones revenue.
And every additional month increases the probability that the technology installed inside the facility becomes economically obsolete sooner.
The scarce AI asset is becoming bankable, energized capacity—not announced capacity.
⸻
The Price of Intelligence Is Falling While the Capital Required to Produce It Is Rising
At the model layer, competitive pressure continues to reduce the cost of intelligence.
Open-weight systems are proliferating.
Chinese model developers are compressing token prices.
Inference efficiency continues improving.
Models capable of sophisticated agentic tasks increasingly operate on smaller and cheaper hardware.
That is profoundly deflationary.
But the industrial system surrounding those models is moving in the opposite direction.
AI requires increasing investment in:
accelerated computing;
memory;
optical networking;
data centres;
power generation;
transmission;
cooling;
storage;
and financing.
This creates the defining economic paradox of Industrial AI:
Intelligence is becoming cheaper. Producing intelligence at industrial scale is becoming more capital intensive.
The implication for margins is consequential.
If the unit price of intelligence falls faster than infrastructure productivity improves, returns on incremental capital eventually compress.
The next competitive advantage will therefore come from reducing the capital required per economically useful unit of intelligence.

Markets Are Validating AI Earnings—But No Longer Ignoring Capital Productivity
J.P. Morgan raised its year-end S&P 500 target to 8,000, increased its 2026 earnings estimate to $365 and lifted its 2027 estimate to $420.
The bullish argument is straightforward:
AI investment is increasingly converting into cloud revenue, contracted backlog and corporate earnings.
But J.P. Morgan maintained a forward multiple near 20× rather than assuming further substantial valuation expansion.
Why?
Because investors must simultaneously absorb:
higher Treasury yields;
large corporate debt issuance;
equity issuance;
geopolitical risk;
and unprecedented infrastructure requirements.
That distinction is important.
The next stage of the market is unlikely to be driven primarily by higher multiples.
It must increasingly be driven by:
earnings growth;
cash conversion;
capital efficiency;
and balance-sheet quality.
The equation is shifting from:
AI exposure × multiple expansion
toward:
AI earnings × cash conversion × capital productivity.
That is a materially harder test.

Sovereign Capital Is Moving From Subsidizing Technology to Building Industrial Systems
South Korea has announced a new ₩5 trillion semiconductor fund, alongside additional trade financing for suppliers.
More important than the fund itself is the infrastructure surrounding it.
South Korea intends to secure approximately 14.7 GW of electricity for the Yongin semiconductor cluster by 2041, while private Korean companies are associated with hundreds of billions of dollars of planned semiconductor investment.
Europe is attempting something similar through its AI Gigafactory programme:
€10 billion of public financing → at least €20 billion of private capital → accelerated computing → sovereign AI capacity.
The direction is correct.
But the decisive metric is not public money committed.
It is the private productive capacity generated per euro of public capital.
The national equation should therefore become:Sovereign Capital × Private Capital Mobilization × Infrastructure Execution × Industrial Adoption = Economic Sovereignty.
Without the final two variables, sovereign AI risks becoming publicly financed computing capacity without sufficient economic return.

Hidden Patterns Beyond the Headlines
The Great Split Is Becoming Capital Formation Versus Capital Productivity
For two years, markets rewarded companies capable of mobilizing extraordinary amounts of capital.
The next stage will differentiate among:
capital announced;
capital financed;
capital deployed;
capital energized;
capital utilized;
capital earning above WACC.
These are fundamentally different economic states.
Only the final one creates sustainable economic value.
⸻
The Financial Cost of Capital and the Economic Cost of Capital Are Diverging
Weak U.S. employment has reduced expectations of additional Federal Reserve tightening.
Treasury yields have moderated.
That reduces the financial discount rate.
But simultaneously:
power scarcity;
permitting delays;
construction inflation;
community opposition;
grid congestion;
and technological obsolescence
are increasing the real economic cost of deploying infrastructure.
The result is an unusual divergence:
Financial capital may become cheaper while productive capacity becomes more expensive.
That distinction will increasingly determine AI valuations.
⸻
AI Is Becoming Project Finance
The hyperscaler model historically resembled software economics:
high upfront development expenditure followed by extraordinary operating leverage.
Industrial AI increasingly resembles infrastructure economics.
The relevant variables become:
capacity utilization;
asset lives;
contract duration;
depreciation;
power contracts;
leverage;
credit spreads;
and residual value.
That transformation matters because infrastructure markets impose much greater discipline on capital than software markets traditionally did.
⸻
Permission Is Becoming a Financial Variable
Permitting was historically treated as an operational issue.
That is changing.
If a permit determines when construction begins, when financing can be drawn and when revenue starts, then permitting directly affects:
NPV;
IRR;
interest expense;
debt-service coverage;
and ultimately credit quality.
The same applies to grid connections.
The economic value of land increasingly depends not simply on location but on the probability and timing of energization.
⸻
The Scarcity Rent Is Migrating Outward
The first scarcity rent belonged to frontier models.
Then GPUs.
Then memory.
Then power.
Now the rent increasingly spreads across:
grid connections;
cooling;
electrical equipment;
land with secured power;
permitting capability;
project finance;
and long-duration contracted infrastructure.
The investment opportunity surrounding AI may therefore become substantially larger than AI software itself.

The Velocity Edge
The financial debate around Artificial Intelligence is becoming much more rigorous.
The question is no longer whether AI will generate extraordinary demand.
It already is.
The question is what happens after that demand requires trillions of dollars of physical capital.
Debt must be issued.
Electricity must be secured.
Grid connections must be obtained.
Projects must be permitted.
Infrastructure must be built.
Capacity must be utilized.
And ultimately the cash generated by that capacity must exceed the full economic cost of creating it.
That is where the next Great Split begins.
Between capital formation and capital productivity.
Between capacity announced and capacity energized.
Between revenue growth and free-cash-flow conversion.
Between ROIC and WACC.
The investment rule is therefore becoming considerably harder—and considerably clearer:
Do not reward capex simply because it is AI capex.
Do not confuse backlog with return.
Do not confuse cheaper financing with better economics.
Do not measure the size of the infrastructure before measuring what that infrastructure earns.
The AI Supercycle is entering its capital-productivity phase. The winners will not be those capable of raising or spending the most capital. They will be those capable of converting each incremental dollar into productive capacity, free cash flow and returns above WACC before depreciation, competition and technological obsolescence destroy the spread.
That is where the next trillion dollars of value will be won—or lost
