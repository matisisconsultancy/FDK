THE SCALE PARADOX
AI demand is accelerating again just as the physical system underneath it is becoming harder to expand. Meta’s agent has reignited the equity trade, Alibaba is answering with a 5–10-trillion-parameter model, proprietary silicon and a 20-GW cloud ambition, while Texas freezes new data-center permits and Europe begins measuring the energy and water cost of the build-out. Intelligence can scale almost instantly. Infrastructure cannot.
Markets enter Tuesday with the AI trade firmly back in command. The Nasdaq closed Monday at a record 27,122.09, +2.26%, the S&P 500 gained 1.49%, the Philadelphia semiconductor index jumped 4.3%, and Meta rose 11.4% as investors responded to strong early demand for its Muse autonomous assistant. AMD rose roughly 10% and crossed $1 trillion in market value for the first time. Asian technology markets followed: MSCI Asia-Pacific ex-Japan was up about 0.75%, while South Korea at one point gained around 2%. 
But the rally is occurring inside a much less forgiving macro regime. Brent is still around $101/bbl, the dollar index is near a seven-week high at 100.4, USD/JPY is around 157.47, and markets now price approximately a 56% probability of another Fed hike in October, up from 43.5% a week ago. ECB Chief Economist Philip Lane said the latest energy shock may last longer than previously expected and that inflation may only converge back toward target from around mid-2027. 
And overnight, the physical scale of the AI competition moved higher again.
Alibaba says it plans models of 5–10 trillion parameters, unveiled a new proprietary AI chip delivering three times the performance of its predecessor, says a cluster could support as many as 500,000 cards, and wants Alibaba Cloud’s global data-center capacity above 20 GW by 2032. At almost the same moment, Texas suspended new state permits for data centers while it audits a queue exceeding 470 GW—more than five times the state’s peak electricity demand. 
The contradiction is the story.
DIGITAL DEMAND IS ACCELERATING FASTER THAN PHYSICAL SUPPLY CAN RESPOND.
That is today’s Scale Paradox.

THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
What happens when intelligence scales in weeks, while electricity, grids, data centers, nuclear plants, financing and permits scale in years?
The AI economy is developing two radically different clocks.
The digital clock moves at software speed:
Models improve → Agents diffuse → Usage rises → Tokens multiply.
The physical clock moves at industrial speed:
Power plant → Grid connection → Data center → Cooling → Chips → Financing → Permits.
The emerging equation is therefore:
**AI DEMAND VELOCITY
÷ PHYSICAL CAPACITY VELOCITY
= SCARCITY RENT**
The larger that ratio becomes, the more value migrates away from generic intelligence and toward the scarce infrastructure required to deliver it.
This is the deeper evolution of The Great Split.
It is no longer simply:
AI winners vs. AI losers.
It is increasingly:
**THOSE WHO CAN SCALE INTELLIGENCE
vs.
THOSE WHO CAN SCALE THE PHYSICAL SYSTEM BENEATH IT.**
The second group may ultimately control the economics of the first.

THE FIVE MOST IMPORTANT OVERNIGHT SIGNALS
1. THE AGENTIC ECONOMY HAS PRODUCED ITS FIRST CLEAR MARKET DEMAND SIGNAL
Verified fact: Meta’s Muse assistant has seen strong early demand following its U.S. launch. Muse can autonomously interact with applications to send emails, sell items, book travel and perform other persistent tasks. Meta shares surged 11.4% Monday, while the broader AI complex rallied sharply. 
This matters because AI adoption may be entering a different economic phase.
The first generation of generative AI waited for humans to ask questions.
Agentic AI performs tasks.
That changes consumption.
A chatbot interaction may require seconds of compute.
A persistent agent can operate for minutes, hours or continuously.
The demand function therefore becomes:
**USERS ↑
× TASKS PER USER ↑
× DURATION PER TASK ↑
× AGENTS PER USER ↑
= COMPUTE CONSUMPTION ↑↑↑**
FDK interpretation
This is potentially the beginning of Agentic Elasticity.
Better and cheaper AI does not necessarily reduce total compute spending.
It can do the opposite.
As the marginal cost of an intelligent task falls, the number of tasks economically worth automating expands.
This is the Jevons Paradox applied to cognition:
CHEAPER INTELLIGENCE → MORE INTELLIGENCE CONSUMED.
That is extremely bullish for the infrastructure layer—but potentially much less straightforward for application margins.

2. ALIBABA HAS TURNED CHINA’S AI STRATEGY INTO A FULL-STACK INDUSTRIAL PROGRAM
This morning’s Alibaba announcement is one of the most consequential signals from China this year.
Verified fact: Alibaba says upcoming Qwen models could scale to 5–10 trillion parameters, compared with 2.4 trillion for its current flagship. It unveiled the Zhenwu V900 AI chip, which it says delivers three times the performance of its predecessor, and says systems based on it could scale to clusters of as many as 500,000 cards. Alibaba also targets more than 20 GW of global data-center capacity by 2032. 
CEO Eddie Wu also said supply constraints across the AI data-center chain are limiting Alibaba Cloud’s ability to grow as quickly as customer demand.
That sentence may matter more than the model announcement.
Alibaba is effectively confirming the structural equation we have been tracking:
Model capability is no longer the binding constraint.
Infrastructure availability increasingly is.
FDK interpretation
China’s competitive architecture is becoming unmistakable:
**MODELS
DOMESTIC SILICON
SUPERNODES
CLOUD
POWER
DATA CENTERS**
This is not a model race.
It is a system race.
The U.S. remains far ahead across important parts of frontier silicon and hyperscale ecosystems, while Chinese firms remain constrained by export controls. But China is increasingly responding by vertically integrating the stack and scaling system architecture around those constraints. 
The strategic unit of competition is moving from the chip to the complete intelligence factory.

3. TEXAS HAS MADE PERMISSION A HARD CONSTRAINTYesterday, we argued that permission would become one of the next scarcity factors.
Texas has now made that visible.
Verified fact: Governor Greg Abbott barred the state environmental agency from issuing new permits for data centers until regulators finish a wide-ranging audit of their power, tax and water impact. More than 470 GW of data centers and other large users were waiting for connections when the state recently froze new grid hookups—more than five times Texas’s peak electricity demand. 
This is a major change.
Texas became an AI infrastructure magnet because it possessed exactly what the industry wanted:
land,
power,
gas,
favorable regulation,
and speed.
Now the growth itself is creating political constraints.
The causality chain is becoming:
**AI DEMAND ↑
→ DATA CENTERS ↑
→ GRID QUEUE ↑
→ ELECTRICITY / WATER PRESSURE ↑
→ POLITICAL RESISTANCE ↑
→ PERMITTING ↓
→ CAPACITY SCARCITY ↑**
FDK interpretation
The economic unit investors should increasingly value is not:
one megawatt of theoretical capacity.
It is:
ONE PERMITTED, POWERED, FINANCED AND CONNECTED MEGAWATT.
Those are very different assets.
This is why existing infrastructure with secured power, grid interconnection and permits may command increasing strategic premiums relative to announced capacity.

4. EUROPE IS MOVING FROM AI REGULATION TO AI INFRASTRUCTURE REGULATION
Verified fact: the European Commission proposed an EU-wide disclosure framework for data centers of at least 500 kW, requiring standardized reporting on energy and water efficiency, local water stress and their ability to support energy systems, including through waste-heat reuse. Data centers currently account for around 2.5% of EU electricity consumption, while capacity is expected to expand from 12 GW in 2025 to 28 GW by 2030. 
This is important because Europe’s AI debate is finally becoming physical.
The question is no longer only:
How should models be regulated?
It is increasingly:
How should intelligence infrastructure interact with energy, water and cities?
FDK interpretation
Transparency is useful.
But Europe must avoid solving the wrong sequencing problem.
The continent does not need only better measurement of data centers.
It needs faster creation of the infrastructure feeding them.
A European AI strategy therefore requires:
REPORTING + PERMITTING + POWER + GRID + CAPITAL
—not reporting instead of capacity.
Europe’s competitive metric should increasingly be:
TIME TO POWER.
How many months from investment decision to a fully connected, operating AI facility?
That figure may matter more to competitiveness than another regulatory scorecard.

5. SOVEREIGN CAPITAL IS MERGING WITH POWER INFRASTRUCTURE
A remarkably important state-capital signal arrived from South Korea overnight.
Verified fact: Seoul has selected a proposed 6.3-GW gas-fired power complex in Texas, reportedly valued above $20 billion, as the first project it intends to pursue within a $350 billion U.S. investment package negotiated alongside bilateral trade arrangements. The plant is intended to support AI data centers and semiconductor facilities. South Korea is also discussing nuclear investments, including Korean-designed APR1400 reactors, and a potential stake in Westinghouse. 
This reveals how sovereign industrial strategy is evolving.
Countries are no longer negotiating only tariffs.
They are negotiating the physical production architecture behind AI:
Power.
Nuclear.
Gas.
Semiconductors.
Shipbuilding.
Capital.
FDK interpretation
The sovereign-capital model is moving from:
financial allocation
toward:
STRATEGIC CAPACITY FORMATION.
That is a profound change.
Capital is being deployed not merely because an asset has an attractive financial return, but because it increases:
energy security,
industrial capacity,
technology access,
or geopolitical leverage.
This will increasingly blur the lines between infrastructure investing, industrial policy and foreign policy.

FIVE HIDDEN PATTERNS BEYOND THE NEWS
First — the real AI demand curve may be far steeper than model forecasts implyPersistent autonomous agents potentially transform consumption from session-based to continuous.
If one user eventually operates several agents simultaneously, total inference demand may expand much faster than user counts suggest.
The relevant metric becomes:
COMPUTE HOURS PER PERSON
—not simply AI users.


⸻


Second — the AI race is converging on vertical integration
Nvidia.
Meta.
Google.
Microsoft.
Amazon.
Alibaba.
Huawei.
OpenAI.
All are increasingly moving beyond isolated layers of the stack.
The economic logic is straightforward.
As bottlenecks proliferate, relying entirely on external suppliers exposes growth to somebody else’s scarcity.
Vertical integration becomes a hedge against bottlenecks.
The new competitive advantage is therefore:
CONTROL OF THE BOTTLENECK MIGRATION.


⸻


Third — electricity has become an AI input and an inflation input simultaneously
AI infrastructure competes for electricity.
Energy scarcity raises the cost of compute.
Energy prices also influence inflation.
Higher inflation produces higher rates.
Higher rates increase AI financing costs.
The system feeds back on itself:
**AI CAPEX ↑
→ POWER DEMAND ↑
→ ENERGY COST ↑
→ INFLATION ↑
→ INTEREST RATES ↑
→ AI HURDLE RATE ↑**
ECB Governing Council member Fabio Panetta explicitly warned Monday that central banks must understand how AI productivity gains are distributed because the distribution will influence aggregate demand and inflation. 
That strengthens the thesis behind our Central Banks & AI Supercycle framework: the first phase of the AI revolution may generate inflation before later productivity gains become broadly disinflationary.

Fourth — geopolitics is beginning to regulate the speed of AI capital formation
The Trump–Xi summit later this week is expected to include AI alongside trade and other bilateral issues. U.S. officials have proposed a notification mechanism for AI incidents reaching national-security significance, although export controls were explicitly excluded from those discussions and Beijing’s position on the proposed mechanism remains unclear. 
The important structural point is not any expected summit outcome.
It is that AI has entered state-to-state strategic dialogue.
That means frontier AI increasingly sits in the same category as:
critical minerals,
semiconductors,
energy,
trade access,
and strategic infrastructure.

Fifth — the Great Split is becoming a speed-of-construction split
Software can scale globally overnight.
Infrastructure cannot.
That difference is becoming economically decisive.
The winners of the next phase may be less differentiated by who announces the largest AI ambition than by who can turn it into operating capacity fastest.
The relevant scoreboard becomes:
MW permitted.
MW connected.
MW financed.
MW operating.
Utilization.
Revenue per MW.
Tokens per watt.
Everything else risks becoming narrative capacity.

IMPLICATIONS FOR INVESTORS
Yesterday’s equity rally confirms that investors still assign enormous value to AI adoption. But the infrastructure evidence says the opportunity set is narrowing toward businesses capable of converting demand into scarce, operating capacity.
The most attractive economics increasingly sit where demand elasticity meets supply rigidity.
That includes accelerated computing, HBM and memory, advanced packaging, optical networking, power generation, nuclear, grid infrastructure, transformers, storage, cooling and permitted data-center capacity.
The key distinction is no longer simply:
AI winner vs. AI loser.
It is:
SCARCITY OWNER vs. SCARCITY CUSTOMER.
The first captures the rent.
The second pays it.
The market will increasingly need to distinguish between AI companies whose growth improves free cash flow and those whose growth merely raises dependence on expensive infrastructure.

Not a generic AI strategy.
A map of the few constraints capable of stopping the strategy.
For most enterprises, those constraints will increasingly sit across:compute availability,
electricity access,
model access,
data rights,
cybersecurity,
capital,
permitting,
and organizational execution.
The strategic question is:
WHICH INPUT CANNOT SCALE AS FAST AS OUR AI AMBITION?
Find that input first.
Because the slowest layer determines the speed of the whole system.
That is classic strategy.
And it is becoming the defining management problem of the AI Supercycle.

IMPLICATIONS FOR EUROPE AND POLICYMAKERS
Europe should pay particular attention to today’s contrast.
Alibaba targets 20 GW.
Texas already has 470 GW of large-load projects seeking connection.
The entire EU had roughly 12 GW of data-center capacity last year and expects about 28 GW by 2030. 
These numbers are not directly comparable—Texas’s queue includes other large loads and proposed capacity rather than operating capacity—but the scale difference is still instructive.
Europe’s AI problem is becoming a capital-formation problem.
The continent needs simultaneously to improve:
power supply,
cross-border grids,
capital markets,
permitting,
AI factories,
and physical-AI deployment.
The opportunity remains especially strong in the sectors where Europe already possesses large installed systems:
mobility,
railways,
manufacturing,
energy,
robotics,
healthcare,
logistics.
Europe should not attempt merely to recreate Silicon Valley.
It should build the best environment in the world for:
AI APPLIED TO THE PHYSICAL ECONOMY.
But physical AI requires physical infrastructure.
There is no shortcut around that fact.
