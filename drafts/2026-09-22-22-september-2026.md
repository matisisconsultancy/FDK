THE AI SUPERCYCLE HAS ENTERED MONETARY POLICY
Fundamental Question:
What happens when the largest technology investment cycle in decades becomes large enough to alter aggregate demand, inflation, bond supply—and therefore the reaction function of central banks?
THE THESIS
The Great Bifurcation is becoming visible.
AI is producing two different macroeconomic regimes at two different speeds:
Phase I — AI Infrastructure Inflation:
Accelerated computing → data centers → electricity → grids → construction → financing → labor → higher aggregate demand and capital costs.
Phase II — Industrial-AI Deflation:
AI diffusion → automation → productivity → lower unit costs → expanded supply → disinflation.
The critical issue is timing.
Central banks are confronting Phase I before economies have captured the full productivity dividend of Phase II.
And yesterday brought an important confirmation.


⸻


SIGNAL 1 — THE FED IS STARTING TO SEE AI AS A DEMAND SHOCK
Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee explicitly connected booming AI investment with unusually strong U.S. demand and persistent inflationary pressure. He argued that, if inflation is increasingly demand-driven rather than simply the residue of supply shocks, monetary policy may need to respond more forcefully. 
That matters enormously.
Until recently, AI was predominantly discussed by central banks as a future productivity story.
It is increasingly becoming a current macro-demand story.
The BIS reached a similar structural conclusion in July: the AI boom is generating a large investment surge that is increasingly debt financed, while simultaneously affecting both aggregate demand and potential supply. 
The monetary-policy equation is changing:
AI CapEx ↑ → Investment Demand ↑ → Financing Demand ↑ → Aggregate Demand ↑ → Inflation Persistence ↑ → Neutral Rate / Policy Constraint ↑


⸻


SIGNAL 2 — THE FED’S NUMBERS CONFIRM THE CONSTRAINT
The September FOMC projections underline the problem.
Median projections now show:
2026 GDP growth: 2.3%
2026 PCE inflation: 3.7%
2026 Core PCE: 3.4%
End-2026 federal funds rate: 4.1%
Perhaps more important, the projected end-2026 policy rate has moved sharply higher from the 3.8% median projected in June. 
This is the paradox:
AI can raise tomorrow’s potential growth while simultaneously making today’s monetary easing harder.
The stronger the investment boom becomes before productivity arrives, the greater the possibility that monetary policy must restrain the rest of the economy to accommodate it.


⸻


SIGNAL 3 — EUROPE HAS ENTERED THE SAME EQUATION
The ECB raised its three key rates by 25 basis points on September 10, citing persistent inflation pressures associated primarily with the Middle East energy shock. Its baseline now projects euro-area inflation of 3.0% in 2026, 2.5% in 2027 and 2.1% in 2028. 
But underneath the energy story, something more structural is emerging.
The ECB explicitly reported increased AI-related activity in:
digital services → business investment → exports.
And Lagarde made an unusually important observation about long-term yields: AI-related activity is becoming a major consumer of financing, increasingly extending from equity into bonds and private credit. 
This is where the AI Supercycle begins interacting directly with the cost of capital.


⸻


SIGNAL 4 — THE BOTTLENECK IS MOVING FROM COMPUTE TO CAPITAL
The first AI race was about GPUs.
The next is increasingly about financing the physical system surrounding them.
Accelerated Computing
↓
Power + Grid
↓
Data Centers
↓
Infrastructure
↓
Debt Financing
↓
Duration
↓
Cost of Capital
AI therefore creates a second-order effect rarely incorporated into conventional technology analysis:
AI competes for global savings.
The enormous capital requirement of the buildout can increase demand for long-duration financing at precisely the moment governments themselves are issuing large quantities of debt.Recent analysis of rising global long-term rates similarly points toward stronger investment demand combined with increasing long-duration debt supply. 
The AI race is therefore migrating from the semiconductor market into the global capital market.


⸻


SIGNAL 5 — THE GREAT BIFURCATION
The policy dilemma can now be stated clearly.
2025–2027
AI infrastructure buildout
→ CapEx
→ energy demand
→ construction
→ skilled-labor scarcity
→ credit creation
→ inflationary pressure
2027–2030+
AI diffusion
→ automation
→ productivity
→ lower marginal costs
→ supply expansion
→ disinflationary pressure
Central banks risk having to tighten against the investment necessary to produce the productivity that could ultimately reduce inflation.
That is the Great Bifurcation.

The Bank of England held its lending rate at 3.75% on September 17 despite rising inflation, illustrating the broader problem facing central banks: inflation persistence is limiting policy flexibility even before the full AI productivity effect appears.

QUICK TAKE
1. AI is no longer only a technology variable.
It is becoming a macroeconomic variable.
2. The first-order AI shock is increasingly inflationary.
Compute, electricity, infrastructure and capital are scarce.
3. The second-order shock should be disinflationary.
But productivity diffusion takes longer than infrastructure deployment.
4. Central banks therefore face a timing mismatch.
They may encounter the demand shock years before the productivity shock.
5. The decisive variable becomes the cost of capital.
The AI Supercycle must now generate returns capable of surviving structurally higher hurdle rates.
VELOCITY INSIGHT
The AI race has crossed another boundary: from technology into monetary policy.
The first phase was about who controlled compute.
The second was about who controlled energy.
The emerging phase is about who can finance the physical AI economy without allowing its cost of capital to overwhelm its productivity gains.
That is where the AI Supercycle and central banking now collide.
