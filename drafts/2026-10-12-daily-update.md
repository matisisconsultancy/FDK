THE RESILIENCE PREMIUM
The AI Supercycle Enters the Age of Strategic Infrastructure
THE VELOCITY EDGE | IN FOCUS
Global Velocity Index: 108.0 | Daily change: 0.0 provisional | Ranking changes: None*
Evidence cut-off: 12 October 2026, 04:41 CEST
THE BIG TAKE
The AI Supercycle is entering a consequential new phase: the capacity to produce intelligence is no longer sufficient. The ability to protect, finance and continuously operate that capacity is becoming a source of competitive advantage.
Three developments establish the shift.
First, AI infrastructure has become a direct target of geopolitical conflict. On October 11, Yandex confirmed the shutdown of its Vladimir-region data centre following a drone attack. Two other Yandex facilities had been damaged or disrupted in preceding days, including a site hosting two of the three supercomputers used to develop the company’s AI model. The episode provides concrete evidence that digital infrastructure can become vulnerable to physical conflict. 
Second, demand for accelerated computing continues to expand. TSMC reported September revenue of NT$511.86 billion, up 54.6% year-on-year. Revenue for the first nine months increased 41.1%. Its October 15 earnings release is expected to provide further evidence of the semiconductor industry’s profitability and investment requirements. 
Third, the financing divide is widening. World Bank President Ajay Banga disclosed on October 11 that the institution is discussing potential crisis assistance with 30–40 countries facing energy shocks, higher borrowing costs and mounting debt pressure. Developing economies face approximately $400 billion in external debt payments during 2026. 
The implications extend beyond technology.
Intelligence infrastructure is becoming a strategic asset whose economic value depends increasingly on energy security, operational continuity, geopolitical resilience and access to long-duration capital.
The next competitive advantage will not belong simply to those who build the most intelligence. It will belong to those who can keep intelligence operating—and earning—under adverse conditions.

II. FIVE SIGNALS DEFINING GLOBAL VELOCITY
1 — Intelligence Infrastructure: Reliability becomes economic capacity
The disruption of three Yandex data-centre facilities introduces a new dimension into the investment equation.Until now, the market has predominantly evaluated AI infrastructure through computing capacity, installed GPUs, power availability and construction pipelines.
Physical disruption makes another variable impossible to ignore:
Operational continuity.
A facility that cannot operate cannot generate its expected economic output, regardless of the capital invested in it.
The emerging investment framework must therefore distinguish nominal capacity from usable capacity.
COMMISSIONED CAPACITY × AVAILABILITY × SECURITY = EFFECTIVE OPERATING CAPACITY
This is a conceptual framework, not yet a calculated GVI indicator. It will require comparable data on uptime, redundancy, disruption duration and capacity restoration.
The implications are particularly important for data-centre operators, insurers, power companies and infrastructure investors.
2 — South Korea and Taiwan: Semiconductor demand remains exceptionally strong
The latest TSMC revenue figures reinforce the strength of the semiconductor investment cycle.
September revenue increased 54.6% year-on-year, while the company reported record third-quarter sales.
Analysts surveyed by Reuters expect third-quarter net profit to increase approximately 65% to NT$746.3 billion. That profit figure is a forecast, not a reported result. 
At the same time, geopolitical risks remain relevant to the region.
On October 12, Taiwan reported monitoring Chinese live-fire military exercises in several coastal areas. The exercises were not taking place directly around Taiwan, and no semiconductor-production disruption has been established. 
For the GVI, the distinction matters.
Strong semiconductor revenues are measured. Geopolitical interruption remains a risk scenario.
South Korea’s score remains unchanged.
Taiwan is not currently included in the 15-country GVI universe, making its semiconductor ecosystem an important external indicator that deserves systematic monitoring.
3 — China: Long-duration financing becomes a competitive instrument
Tencent announced on October 12 that it is considering a new bond offering denominated in US dollars and offshore renminbi.
Potential maturities extend to 20 years for dollar securities and 30 years for offshore-yuan securities.
No transaction size has been established, and the issuance remains subject to market conditions and investor demand. 
The financial significance lies in the proposed financing architecture.
Long-duration borrowing can provide substantial investment flexibility for companies with credible cash-generation capacity.
It can also create refinancing and capital-allocation risks if expected returns fail to materialise.
The ability to secure long-duration capital is becoming an important differentiator within the AI ecosystem.
China’s national score remains unchanged because a proposed corporate bond offering does not demonstrate a new economy-wide improvement.
4 — Global Capital: The development financing divide becomes more pronounced
The World Bank’s latest assessment reinforces the uneven distribution of financial resilience.
In fiscal 2026, the institution mobilised a record $112 billion in private capital, compared with $35 billion in fiscal 2022.
But only approximately $3 billion reached low-income countries through this mobilisation channel. 
The contrast is consequential.
Advanced economies and stronger emerging markets can attract increasing amounts of private capital for infrastructure and industrial investment.
The most financially vulnerable countries continue to face substantially greater constraints.
With energy costs elevated and debt-service requirements increasing, the risk is that investment in future productive capacity becomes concentrated in countries already possessing the strongest financial institutions.
Financial resilience may increasingly determine technological participation.
5 — Smart Capital: Energy and sovereign debt remain the principal macroeconomic constraintsThe IMF and World Bank meetings beginning this week in Bangkok are taking place against a difficult combination of elevated energy costs, public debt and geopolitical uncertainty.
According to Reuters, the IMF expects global public debt to exceed 100% of GDP before 2030. It also anticipates that the consequences of the energy shock will affect countries unevenly. 
This creates a structural tension.
AI investment requires long-duration financing and abundant energy.
Governments must simultaneously manage increasingly expensive debt and competing public expenditure obligations.
The potential transmission mechanism is:
ENERGY COSTS → INFLATION → FINANCING COSTS → INVESTMENT HURDLES → DEPLOYMENT SPEED
These relationships are economically plausible, but their magnitude varies across countries and cannot be inferred from the latest headlines alone.

IV. SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS — WHAT COMES NEXT
1. A resilience premium emerges in AI infrastructure
The next valuation distinction may increasingly separate infrastructure with strong operational redundancy from infrastructure exposed to concentrated physical, energy or geopolitical risks.
Investors will need to examine:
Geographic diversification and operational redundancy.
Electricity supply resilience and backup capability.
Cybersecurity and physical protection.
Disaster recovery and restoration times.
Contractual responsibility for prolonged outages.
The effect on valuations remains to be demonstrated, but the economic logic is clear.
Reliable capacity can command greater economic value than nominal capacity carrying substantial interruption risk.
2. Strategic infrastructure becomes part of national security
The Yandex disruptions illustrate how computational infrastructure can become strategically relevant during armed conflict.
This may accelerate government attention to the geographic distribution of data centres, grid protection, communications networks and sovereign computing capacity.
The second-order consequence could be an expansion of security requirements across the infrastructure ecosystem.
That would create opportunities for providers of resilience technology while increasing costs for some operators.
AI infrastructure is becoming part of the physical architecture of national security.
3. Financial resilience reinforces industrial concentration
Companies with strong balance sheets, diversified infrastructure and access to long-duration borrowing may be better positioned to withstand both financing shocks and operational disruptions.
Less resilient operators face a more difficult combination.
HIGHER OPERATING RISK → HIGHER FINANCING REQUIREMENTS → LOWER INVESTMENT FLEXIBILITY
The result could be greater concentration of infrastructure ownership.
However, competition, technological innovation and new financing structures may offset this tendency.The outcome remains conditional rather than predetermined.
4. Europe faces a new opportunity in infrastructure resilience
Europe possesses substantial strengths in power systems, electrical engineering, industrial automation, grid technologies and infrastructure management.
These capabilities could become increasingly valuable as AI infrastructure operators seek to improve operational reliability.
The opportunity extends beyond constructing additional computing capacity.
It includes the industrial systems that ensure capacity remains available.
The next European AI opportunity may lie as much in securing and operating intelligence infrastructure as in producing intelligence itself.
5. The next investment rotation incorporates reliability
The AI investment sequence is evolving:
MODELS → COMPUTE → POWER → INFRASTRUCTURE → RESILIENCE → PRODUCTIVITY
Reliability does not replace the importance of technological leadership.
It determines how much productive benefit can actually be realised from installed infrastructure.
The ultimate financial question becomes:
How much usable productive output does each unit of AI capital generate over its operating life?

V. FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS
1 — Investors: introduce resilience into AI valuation models.
Nominal computing capacity is insufficient. Assess operational availability, redundancy, energy exposure, customer contracts and free cash flow after infrastructure-maintenance requirements.
2 — Boards: measure usable capacity, not installed capacity alone.
The relevant indicators should include uptime, disruption recovery, utilisation, operating cash generation and resilience-adjusted returns.
3 — CEOs: make operational continuity a strategic responsibility.
The financial consequences of interruptions should be incorporated into infrastructure investment, procurement and business-continuity decisions.
4 — Policymakers: recognise intelligence infrastructure as strategically important.
Energy security, secure communications, diversified infrastructure and reliable access to computing should become integrated components of industrial competitiveness policy.
5 — Capital allocators: expand the investment discipline.
CAPITAL → SECURE CAPACITY → RELIABLE UTILISATION → PRODUCTIVITY → CASH FLOW → ROIC > WACC
The chain must hold under adverse conditions—not simply under optimistic operating assumptions.
