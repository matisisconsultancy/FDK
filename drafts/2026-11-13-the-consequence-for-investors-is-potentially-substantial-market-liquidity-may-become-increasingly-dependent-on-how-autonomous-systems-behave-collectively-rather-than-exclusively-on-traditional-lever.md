III. THE FED’S REACTION FUNCTION — THE POLICY DEBATE INTENSIFIES

On November 12, Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack argued for gradual rate increases rather than aggressive tightening, despite persistent inflation.

On the same day, New York Fed President John Williams reiterated that further policy action would be required to restore inflation to 2%, without specifying the pace. Reuters +1

The distinction matters because AI investment currently supports growth and increases demand for capital, while the timing of productivity-led disinflation remains uncertain.

A more consequential institutional test is approaching.

On November 16, Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh is scheduled to address the Fed’s Productivity and Jobs Task Force conference in San Francisco. The initiative is examining how AI and other general-purpose technologies may alter productivity, employment and monetary-policy assessments. Calendar: November 2026 +1

No new formal AI-specific neutral-rate estimate or policy-rule change has been confirmed in today’s review.

IV. THE NEXT FINANCIAL-MARKET DIVIDE

The combined evidence identifies three increasingly important transmission channels.

CAPITAL ALLOCATION

AI investment → concentrated productive assets → potentially higher corporate returns

INCOME DISTRIBUTION

Automation → shifting labour demand → different wage and consumption outcomes

FINANCIAL STABILITY

Agentic investment decisions → correlated positioning → possible liquidity amplification

These channels could reinforce one another, but the magnitude and timing remain uncertain.

For investors, company selection must increasingly distinguish between productivity improvements that expand total output and those that primarily redistribute income from labour to capital. Concentration risk, competitive access to AI and the resilience of market liquidity deserve greater attention.

For EU policymakers, the challenge is broader than financing AI infrastructure. Europe needs competitive AI access for smaller enterprises, workforce adaptation, reliable energy, deeper capital markets and supervisory capabilities appropriate for increasingly autonomous systems.

THE FINAL TAKE — WHO WILL OWN THE PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND?

The latest central-bank interventions make the Great Split more economically consequential.

AI may raise the productive capacity of entire economies without distributing the gains proportionately across workers, companies and countries.

That would alter consumption, corporate profitability, capital demand and ultimately monetary-policy transmission.

The Federal Reserve is beginning to articulate the distributional implications. The Bank of England is examining the financial consequences of autonomous decision-making.

The next test is whether these insights begin to influence formal policy frameworks.

For investors, the central question is shifting from how much productivity AI creates to how much of that productivity becomes sustainable, investable cash flow.

For Europe, the strategic imperative is to prevent the AI productivity dividend from becoming an exclusively foreign-owned financial return.

THE GREAT AI DIVIDEND SPLIT

The Federal Reserve Warns That AI May Reward Capital Before Labour — While the Bank of England Confronts Autonomous Financial Risk

Where Monetary Policy, Industrial AI and the Distribution of Economic Value Collide

THE BIG TAKE

Two consequential central-bank interventions reveal a new dimension of the AI Supercycle: the productivity revolution may deepen the divide between capital and labour before its benefits spread across the economy.

On November 12, Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr warned that AI could produce substantial employment disruption and increasingly concentrate economic returns among the owners of technology and capital. Meanwhile, a November 11 Bank of England speech provided detailed evidence of how financial institutions are already adopting agentic AI, and why supervisory frameworks must evolve.

These interventions extend the monetary debate beyond inflation and interest rates.

The question is no longer simply whether AI raises productivity. It is who captures the resulting income, how that changes aggregate demand, and whether increasingly autonomous financial systems remain stable.

The declining labour share predates the current AI boom. The data do not establish that AI caused the decline. Federal Reserve Board

Barr identifies two competing possibilities. Broad, inexpensive AI access could allow smaller businesses to raise productivity, expand output and create employment. Alternatively, concentrated ownership of computing infrastructure, frontier models and data could direct an increasing share of economic gains toward a relatively small group of companies and shareholders.

The latter scenario would intensify the Great Split, particularly if AI substitutes for entry-level and knowledge-intensive employment before new opportunities emerge.

Barr emphasised that large-scale displacement has not yet materialised and that the outcome remains uncertain. Reuters

The hidden monetary consequence is important: higher productivity does not necessarily mean proportionately higher household purchasing power. If labour’s income share contracts, consumer demand may weaken even while corporate profitability improves.

That complicates the relationship between productivity, inflation, employment and the appropriate policy rate.

II. THE BANK OF ENGLAND — FROM AGENTIC ADOPTION TO SYSTEMIC RISK

NOVEMBER 11 | JONATHAN HALL, FINANCIAL POLICY COMMITTEE

In a significant speech on autonomous financial systems, Jonathan Hall reported that a recent Bank of England–FCA survey found approximately 80% of respondents using some form of agentic AI, but only 2% of agents enjoying broad autonomy.

The emerging supervisory challenge concerns correlated behaviour. AI agents deployed by otherwise independent financial institutions may react similarly to adverse market conditions, potentially amplifying selling pressure and reducing liquidity.

Hall identified access controls, identifiable trading agents, monitoring and system-wide stress analysis as important safeguards.

He also highlighted BIS Project Logos, an existing collaboration with the Bank of England and Bundesbank designed to study AI portfolio managers in simulated financial markets. Bank for International Settlements
