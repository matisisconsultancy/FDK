A more coherent central-bank reaction function is emerging. The latest signal comes from the Reserve Bank of Australia, and it is unusually explicit: Governor Michele Bullock said on 22 September that there are still "very few signs" AI is lifting the supply side of the economy, while there is substantial evidence it is already boosting demand. She described this as an "awkward sequencing" in which AI investment adds to excess demand before the productivity dividend arrives.

That is a major confirmation of the two-stage AI Supercycle thesis: AI capital formation drives demand, scarcity and inflation first — with AI diffusion delivering productivity, lower unit costs and industrial-AI deflation only later.

Bullock's intervention goes further. She said the transition could temporarily worsen the trade-off between inflation and unemployment and even lift the effective NAIRU for a period as workers are displaced before new jobs and business models emerge. In other words, AI may initially make the central-bank problem harder, not easier.

::signals The New Central-Bank AI Reaction Function | three independent developments, one direction
# The RBA: sequencing risk
Governor Bullock identified the core problem — AI raises demand before supply. Investment adds to excess demand before productivity arrives, temporarily worsening the inflation–unemployment trade-off and potentially lifting the effective NAIRU.
signal: AI is a demand shock first; the productivity dividend comes later.

# The ECB: valuation and concentration risk
In an Economic Bulletin analysis published on 22 September, ECB staff found that strong realised and expected earnings have supported US equities, but investor compensation for equity risk has compressed sharply. Concentration now sits above the historical 95th percentile for the S&P 500 and above the 75th percentile for IT. The tails of technology-sector risk pricing have begun to widen, and the ECB warns that underperformance by a small number of mega-caps could transmit into a broader correction — especially if optimistic profit assumptions or debt-financed AI investment prove unsustainable.
signal: Concentration and leverage amplify the cost of any productivity disappointment.

# Europe's energy constraint
ECB Chief Economist Philip Lane said a second wave of energy-price increases means euro-area inflation is likely to stay higher for longer. He acknowledged Europe is not at the centre of the AI boom, though European firms can still benefit from diffusion.
signal: Energy is the binding intermediate constraint delaying the pivot to productivity deflation.

::section The Financial Chain, With Precision | the risk is no longer simply "AI valuations are high"
The chain now runs from AI expectations to high earnings expectations, to low risk premia, to market concentration, to debt-financed capex — and finally to any earnings miss and systemic repricing.

The risk is no longer simply that AI valuations are high. It is that ==market concentration and financing leverage amplify the consequences== of any disappointment in the productivity path. The important change at the RBA is parallel: the possibility that the AI boom proves a bubble, and that a disorderly unwind could damage both the financial system and the real economy, has now entered the formal central-bank vulnerability set.

::section Europe's Double Scarcity Problem | energy and infrastructure before productivity and deflation
Europe risks facing energy scarcity plus AI-infrastructure scarcity before it captures AI productivity and industrial-AI deflation. The decisive variable is becoming the lag between capital formation and productivity realisation. The longer that lag, the more central banks must treat AI as inflationary; the shorter it becomes, the faster the monetary regime can pivot toward the productivity dividend.

::patterns Investor Implication | four variables to watch together
# Realised AI earnings
The distinction is no longer AI winners versus losers, but companies where productivity is already converting capex into cash flow versus those where valuation and leverage run ahead of realised productivity.

# Free-cash-flow conversion
Watch how effectively investment translates into cash. Thin risk premia make disappointment more expensive precisely when higher rates make refinancing more difficult.

# Funding structures
The financing behind infrastructure expansion — data-centre debt, private credit, refinancing sensitivity — determines how a miss propagates.

# Time-to-diffusion
The interval required for productivity gains to reach the broader economy. For rates, AI is not yet a clean disinflation trade: where investment demand and energy constraints dominate, the first-round effect remains upward pressure on inflation and neutral rates.

::takeaways Key takeaways | what the central-bank consensus now says
- AI is a demand shock first and a productivity shock later — the sequence, not the direction, drives policy.
- The bubble question has formally entered the central-bank vulnerability set at the RBA.
- The ECB has quantified thin risk premia and record concentration as the amplifiers of any AI disappointment.
- Europe faces a double scarcity — energy and AI infrastructure — ahead of any productivity dividend, with inflation now targeted only from mid-2027.
- Europe's priority: compress the transition interval between AI capital formation and industrial-AI productivity, while adding AI-specific scenarios to financial-stability surveillance.

::pull FINANCIAL FRAGILITY
The AI Supercycle is becoming a race not merely to build AI fastest, but to convert investment into productivity before inflation, leverage and concentration impose the cost of waiting.

::close The debate has moved beyond "inflationary or deflationary?" — the answer is that ==it depends on the sequence==, and that is today's material inflection.
