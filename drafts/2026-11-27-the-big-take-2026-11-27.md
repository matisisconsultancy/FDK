THE NEW SCARCITY PREMIUM
How the AI Supercycle Is Repricing Strategic Assets, Reshaping the Cost of Capital and Redrawing the Global Investment Landscape
3-minute read
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
What happens to financial markets when access to computing, electricity and capital becomes more valuable than the ability to finance their expansion?
THE BIG TAKE
The AI Supercycle is creating a new hierarchy of financial value. As technological investment accelerates, scarcity is shifting toward the infrastructure, financing capacity and market access required to make intelligence productive.
Three developments reveal this transformation.

The Bank of England's November 26 financial-stability assessment highlights the increasing importance of AI-related debt issuance and interconnected financing arrangements. Bank of England Financial Stability Report
Meanwhile, newly reported US semiconductor restrictions are disrupting selected Asian infrastructure-financing arrangements.
And Europe is confronting the need to connect strategic capital formation with competitive electricity, industrial infrastructure and commercially productive intelligence.
The defining financial shift is from the availability of capital toward the economic value of securing scarce productive assets.
This is not evidence that global AI demand is collapsing.
It is evidence that the conditions required to monetize that demand are becoming more consequential.
1 | THE GREAT SPLIT: THE PRICE OF CAPITAL IS CHANGING
The AI Supercycle is increasingly influencing mainstream credit markets.
The Bank of England forecasts more than $305 billion in hyperscaler bond issuance during 2026, approximately 8% of global investment-grade issuance.
This represents extraordinary capital formation, but it does not establish a generalized credit crisis.
What changed: The growing concentration of AI financing has become an explicit financial-stability concern.
The potential transmission is:
AI Investment → Debt Issuance → Financing Concentration → Higher Sensitivity to Credit Conditions → More Selective Capital Allocation.
The second-order consequence may be a widening differentiation between companies financing expansion through substantial internal cash generation and those dependent on repeated external borrowing.
For investors, the relevant indicators are credit spreads, contractual obligations, free-cash-flow conversion and incremental returns on invested capital.
The next valuation premium may increasingly accrue to companies capable of sustaining technological growth without sacrificing financial resilience.
2 | ACCELERATED COMPUTING: THE GEOPOLITICAL PREMIUM
The US restrictions on advanced computing access, effective November 19, introduced additional licensing requirements affecting selected activities involving Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil.
New reporting indicates that Nvidia has instructed manufacturing partners to pause certain server shipments to four Southeast Asian markets while assessing compliance.
Approximately $500 million of financing for one affected project has reportedly been suspended.
These reports concern selected transactions, not a universal semiconductor-export prohibition.
What changed: Regulatory uncertainty is beginning to affect specific commercial and financing decisions.
The causal mechanism is:
Technology Restrictions → Reduced Procurement Certainty → Potential Project Delays → Higher Financing Risk → Lower Expected Returns.
For investors, computing access and customer eligibility are becoming important components of infrastructure valuations.
For Europe, regulatory predictability and diversified technological access could become competitive advantages—provided they are accompanied by commercially sustainable costs.
3 | ENERGY: THE SCARCITY PREMIUM MOVES INTO ELECTRICITY
Japan's JERA is exploring additional data-centre developments involving American technology companies.
Its previously announced Chiba project envisages approximately 400 MW of computing capacity, with initial operations targeted for 2028 and total planned investment exceeding $15 billion.
The project is planned, not operational. Latest customer discussions do not establish new signed contracts.
The opportunity lies in connecting electricity generation directly with computing infrastructure.
Electricity Access → Faster Potential Commissioning → Earlier Commercial Utilization → Potentially Higher Project Value.
The same dynamics are visible in Europe.
Spain's approximately €17.1 billion electricity transmission plan identifies 9.3 GW of anticipated data-centre demand within a much broader program of grid modernization through 2030.
However, planned demand and grid investment are not equivalent to commissioned electricity capacity.
The financial opportunity extends to power equipment, energy storage, electricity networks and industrial engineering.
The economic value of energy infrastructure is increasingly determined by its ability to make computing capacity commercially productive.
4 | INDUSTRIAL AI: THE PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND REMAINS THE TEST
Bloomberg reported on November 26 that SAP is considering a restructuring potentially affecting more than 10,000 positions as it adapts to AI-driven changes.
The plan remains unconfirmed.
It cannot be treated as an established reduction in employment or proof of productivity gains.

5 | EUROPE 2030: THE COST OF INSTITUTIONAL DELAY
Europe's proposed €50 billion Sovereign Tech Equity Fund remains under discussion and is not committed or deployed capital.
The potential opportunity lies in complementing public financing with deeper private capital markets.
But technological sovereignty cannot be achieved through financing announcements alone.
The European implementation priorities are clear: secure competitive electricity, accelerate commercially viable computing infrastructure and scale AI-native industrial companies.
EuroHPC's Gigafactory procurement deadlines remain December 3, 2026, and February 4, 2027.
No new Gigafactory award or operational facility has been verified. EuroHPC procurement notice
Europe's strategic challenge is to reduce the time between capital commitments and productive economic returns.

THE NEW RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS
THE STRATEGIC ACCESS PREMIUM
Could companies controlling reliable access to computing, electricity, financing and commercially eligible customers command a persistent valuation premium?The hypothesis predicts that scarce, difficult-to-replicate access may improve commissioning certainty, cash-flow resilience and risk-adjusted returns.
NER-C should test four dimensions: computing access, electricity availability, financing conditions and regulatory market access.
The empirical indicators should include debt spreads, commissioning delays, utilization, operating cash flow and incremental ROIC relative to WACC.
The hypothesis remains unvalidated. Current evidence establishes increasing constraints, not a proven systematic valuation premium.
FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS
Investors: Look beyond technological growth toward companies controlling scarce assets with sustainable cash-generation potential.
Boards: Preserve financing flexibility and treat computing and energy access as strategic capital-allocation variables.
Financial institutions: Stress-test interconnected infrastructure exposures against regulatory, utilization and refinancing shocks.
Governments: Recognize that reliable electricity and deep capital markets are increasingly essential to technological competitiveness.
European policymakers: Accelerate the conversion of strategic commitments into bankable infrastructure and productive industrial applications.
THE FINAL VELOCITY INSIGHT
CAPITAL WILL FOLLOW THE SCARCITY OF ACCESS
The next major financial rotation may occur within the AI economy rather than away from it.
From speculative infrastructure toward commercially secured capacity.
From heavily leveraged expansion toward financial resilience.
From technological ambition toward measurable industrial productivity.
Three financial dynamics will shape that transition.
First, the scarcity of critical infrastructure can generate an economic premium. Electricity access, advanced computing and regulatory certainty may increasingly influence valuations.
Second, capital productivity will separate sustainable growth from financial vulnerability. Companies that convert investment into operating cash flow while maintaining financial flexibility may command more durable premiums.
Third, Europe has an opportunity to compete through the combination of patient capital, industrial specialization and reliable infrastructure. But that opportunity will depend on execution rather than declarations.
For investors, the priority is risk-adjusted returns.
For boards, it is disciplined capital allocation.
For policymakers, it is building the conditions in which intelligence becomes economically productive.
The next financial advantage will not belong to those who commit the most capital to the AI Supercycle, but to those who secure the scarce capabilities required to generate the greatest sustainable returns from it.
THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
In the AI-native economy, scarcity creates the premium. Capital determines who can acquire it. Productivity determines who can sustain it.
