THE PRICE OF A FRACTURED WORLD
How the AI Supercycle, Restrictive Monetary Policy and Economic Nationalism Are Transforming the Rules of Global Capital Allocation
NOVEMBER 25, 2026 | FDK
Where Financial Markets, Industrial AI and Geopolitics Converge
THE BIG TAKE
The world economy is showing renewed strength, but the cost of financing its next phase of expansion is becoming a formidable obstacle.
November 25 brought that contradiction into focus. American third-quarter GDP growth was revised upward to 4.3%, while core PCE inflation reached 3.3%. At the same time, Washington proposed new tariffs affecting 14 major trading economies.
Investors responded to this increasingly difficult environment by selling technology and semiconductor shares and turning toward sovereign bonds.
The underlying collision involves three powerful developments: unprecedented investment in artificial intelligence, persistent inflationary pressures and a global trading system becoming more fragmented.
Together, they may reshape financial-market performance and investment decisions well beyond the current trading cycle.
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
Could the real obstacle to the AI Supercycle be an economic and geopolitical environment that makes deploying intelligence increasingly costly, rather than any limitation in the technology itself?
PART I — THE FIVE SIGNALS
The session's five defining signals point toward an increasingly complex relationship between growth, inflation and corporate valuations.
1. Stronger growth, tighter policy expectations. U.S. GDP growth was revised to 4.3%, reflecting strength in business investment. That resilience gives monetary authorities less reason to relax restrictive financial conditions.
2. Inflation remains difficult to contain. Core PCE inflation reached 3.3%. The persistence of price pressures increases the returns required to justify long-duration projects in technology, energy and infrastructure.
3. Trade policy adds another layer of cost. Proposed U.S. tariffs affecting 14 major economies threaten established supply chains and could encourage a further regionalization of industrial production.
4. Semiconductor valuations remain vulnerable. Nvidia declined approximately 8.7% intraday, while the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 7.3%. Investors are reassessing how much future profitability is already reflected in AI-related equity prices.
5. Energy security complicates the outlook. Brent moved back above $93 per barrel, reinforcing uncertainty about industrial operating costs and the path of inflation.
THE HIDDEN PATTERN
The most important connection is the relationship between AI investment and the resources required to support it.
Greater computing capacity demands more energy and infrastructure. Building that infrastructure requires capital. When inflation, tariffs and geopolitical uncertainty raise costs, financing becomes more demanding.

This difference in timing is becoming an important source of financial risk.

Three consequences beyond the immediate selloff
A more regional investment structure. Trade restrictions and geopolitical risks could push companies to establish manufacturing facilities, computing capacity and energy infrastructure across multiple jurisdictions. This may increase total capital expenditure even where it reduces global efficiency.
Greater emphasis on balance-sheet strength. Investors will increasingly examine companies' borrowing requirements, liquidity, customer commitments and refinancing exposure before rewarding their growth plans.
A stronger investment case for productive infrastructure. Electricity grids, industrial automation, defense production and intelligent mobility could benefit where investment leads to demonstrable productivity improvements and reliable earnings.
These possibilities do not establish that the rotation has already occurred across every sector. They identify where capital may increasingly seek a more favorable balance of growth, risk and returns.
Europe's opportunity
Europe has an important alternative to competing solely through the scale of its computing investment.
Its manufacturing industries, energy networks and high-speed rail systems provide an established physical infrastructure that Industrial AI could make considerably more productive.
The opportunity includes predictive maintenance, lower energy consumption, better network utilization and more efficient asset management.
Europe's most compelling competitive advantage may be the ability to apply intelligence across its productive economy rather than attempting to match the United States dollar for dollar in frontier-computing expenditure.

THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
An increasingly fragmented global economy may produce a new dividing line between companies dependent on international integration for profitability and those capable of preserving their competitiveness despite rising barriers.
The implications for capital allocation are substantial.
Investors should place greater weight on cash-flow durability, refinancing capacity and pricing power. Corporate boards must reassess large investments against more demanding financing assumptions. Industrial executives should prioritize AI applications capable of reducing energy, labor and other input costs.
Central banks face the difficult task of distinguishing temporary inflation associated with infrastructure construction from the longer-term productivity gains technology may deliver.
For European policymakers, stronger coordination of energy, capital markets and industrial strategy remains essential.
THE CLOSING VIEW
The technological potential of artificial intelligence remains formidable. What has changed is the economic environment in which that potential must be realized.
Financing is more demanding. Trade relationships are less predictable. Energy remains exposed to geopolitical disruption. And investors are increasingly unwilling to value technological growth independently of its financial costs.
The next market leaders will be those capable of combining advanced intelligence with industrial competitiveness, efficient energy use and durable financial returns.
In a world becoming more fragmented and expensive, the decisive advantage will belong not simply to the fastest innovators, but to those able to turn innovation into sustainable profitability.
In the Age of Velocity, intelligence drives acceleration. Capital imposes discipline. Productivity creates the ultimate advantage.
