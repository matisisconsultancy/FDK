10 OCTOBER 2026 | WEEKLY GLOBAL EDITION
THE GREAT REPRICING OF CITIES
AI, the Reinvention of the City and the New Geography of Prosperity
IN FOCUS — THE LICENCE TO SCALE
The AI revolution has entered a new phase: cities are no longer competing simply to attract intelligence. They are competing to finance, power, accommodate and legitimize its industrial expansion.
Five developments this week reveal the magnitude of that transition.
On October 5, Morgan Stanley warned that US data-centre developers face a potential 32 GW net power shortfall through 2028, equivalent to 34% of projected requirements.
On October 6, Google announced agreements covering 3,590 MW of electricity supply, including 890 MW from nuclear facilities to be upgraded by Constellation Energy.
The same day, new evidence showed that Spain’s Aragón region could attract more than €60 billion in digital-infrastructure investment over the next decade, largely because qualifying projects can receive planning decisions in less than twelve months.
On October 8, France’s Sesterce announced plans for an additional €10 billion AI-infrastructure investment in Finland.
On October 9, Australia’s Firmus abandoned a proposed $5 billion IPO, after investors questioned the financing requirements, valuation and execution risks associated with its expansion strategy. 
These developments are not independent.
They expose a new causal relationship:
Intelligence creates demand for infrastructure. Infrastructure requires capital. Capital requires execution certainty. Execution increasingly depends on energy availability, public consent and institutional capacity.
The city is where those constraints converge.
And financial markets are beginning to price the consequences.
THIS WEEK’S GVI VERDICT
Singapore retains first place. New York and London remain second and third. Seoul consolidates fourth, while San Francisco holds fifth.
Madrid advances to seventeenth as new evidence reinforces the Iberian Peninsula’s prospective role in Europe’s AI-infrastructure geography.
Helsinki, Milan, Warsaw and Kuala Lumpur gain forward momentum, although the index applies discounts to investments that have not yet become operational.
Australia experiences greater financing pressure following the Firmus IPO withdrawal.
The overriding conclusion is that the next geography of prosperity will be determined less by announced AI investment than by the capacity to convert investment into productive assets without transferring unacceptable costs to citizens.

The industrialization premium
Seoul remains the Velocity leader because its advantage extends beyond software and research into semiconductor manufacturing and industrial applications.
WIPO’s 2026 innovation-cluster rankings place Seoul fourth globally, behind Shenzhen–Hong Kong–Guangzhou, Tokyo–Yokohama and San Jose–San Francisco.
The important distinction is between producing intelligence and industrializing intelligence.
Seoul possesses capabilities across both activities. 
Madrid’s emerging infrastructure premium
Madrid’s position strengthens following the October 6 assessment of Iberian data-centre development.
Rystad Energy forecasts that Iberia’s share of European data-centre capacity will increase from 5.8% in 2025 to 11.5% in 2030.
Spain’s installed capacity could reach 3.9 GW, with Madrid, Aragón and Navarra hosting much of the announced development pipeline.
However, approximately 70% of Spain’s transmission-grid nodes reportedly have no spare capacity. That is a material execution constraint, not a minor qualification. 
The GVI therefore rewards Madrid’s improving strategic position while applying a discount for network-capacity limitations and distinguishing the metropolitan economy from investments located elsewhere in Spain.
The capital-conversion advantage
Hong Kong’s strength reflects its role in connecting Chinese innovation and manufacturing to global capital markets.
Singapore continues to benefit from a different advantage: its capacity to coordinate finance, infrastructure development, regulatory institutions and international business activity.
These are different models of urban competitiveness.
Both underline the same principle: capital formation becomes more valuable when the surrounding urban system can absorb and deploy it efficiently.

Milan: from financial centre to industrial-intelligence platform
On October 7, Core Stack and Green Arrow Capital announced plans to invest more than €1 billion over four years in thirteen medium-sized Italian data centres.
The first two facilities are planned for the provinces of Cremona and Verona, with permitting targeted by the end of 2027 and operations expected in early 2028.
These are not operational assets, and the investments are not located in Milan itself. Their potential significance for Milan comes from the surrounding Northern Italian industrial, financial and technology ecosystem. 
The investment thesis is consequently broader than data-centre construction.
It concerns whether Northern Italy can connect industrial automation, energy, engineering capabilities, financial capital and digital infrastructure into a larger productive system.
Warsaw: the productive value of digital infrastructure
New evidence from Poland’s data-centre industry suggests that recovered heat from future facilities could supply more than 7% of the heat currently delivered by the country’s district-heating networks by 2030, subject to infrastructure development.
That would create value beyond computing services, potentially lowering energy-system waste and expanding the social benefits of digital infrastructure.
This is a forecast, not currently delivered capacity. 
Kuala Lumpur: a new electricity-intensive growth phase
On October 9, Malaysian utility Tenaga Nasional reported an expectation that electricity consumption in Peninsular Malaysia would reach 148.66 TWh in 2027, driven partly by data-centre expansion.
For Kuala Lumpur, the development offers considerable economic opportunity.
But the same demand raises questions about network investment, electricity pricing and the distribution of infrastructure costs.

Austin: when intelligence outgrows infrastructure
Austin remains one of North America’s most important technology ecosystems.
But the broader US power constraint is becoming more serious.
Morgan Stanley’s October 5 assessment estimated a potential 34% net shortfall in power required by US data-centre developers through 2028, equivalent to approximately 32 GW.
The consequences may extend beyond data-centre operators to suppliers of memory, optical equipment and supporting components if deployments are postponed.The broader conclusion is that abundant venture capital does not guarantee abundant productive capacity.
Helsinki: the two sides of accelerated development
Finland illustrates the necessity of separating investment commitments from completed infrastructure.
On October 8, Sesterce announced a project in Jämsä with an initial planned capacity of 200 MW, subsequently expandable to 600 MW.
However, on October 6, Finnish authorities ordered a halt to certain preparatory work at two Google data-centre sites because required environmental assessments had not been completed.
The projects are in different locations, but together they demonstrate both Finland’s growing infrastructure opportunity and the importance of execution governance. 
The GVI gives Finland’s development pipeline forward-capacity credit while discounting environmental and approval risk.
Melbourne: capital markets impose greater discipline
The withdrawal of Firmus’s proposed $5 billion IPO is a significant signal for Australian AI-infrastructure finance.
The proposed transaction had implied an equity valuation of approximately $30.6 billion. Investors subsequently questioned the company’s capital requirements, projected execution and commercial assumptions.
This is principally an issuer-specific event, not evidence that Melbourne’s overall innovation economy is deteriorating.
Nevertheless, it strengthens the case for assigning lower probability-adjusted value to ambitious infrastructure projects without demonstrated cash-flow conversion.

VI. FIVE KEY SIGNALS
SIGNAL 1 — POWER IS BECOMING THE FIRST FINANCIAL GATE
Google’s October 6 agreement with Constellation covers 3,590 MW of electricity supply, including a 20-year agreement associated with 890 MW of additional output from upgraded nuclear facilities.
Constellation expects to invest more than $4.3 billion in its generation fleet, with the first upgraded plant expected to supply electricity in 2028.
The significance is financial as well as industrial.
Long-duration energy arrangements are becoming essential components of the creditworthiness and operating resilience of AI infrastructure. 
GVI implication: Cities with credible access to reliable power gain a financing advantage over equally innovative cities facing uncertain connections.
SIGNAL 2 — FAST PERMITTING CREATES VALUE, BUT NOT WITHOUT SOCIAL LEGITIMACY
Aragón’s development model has attracted substantial planned investment through faster administration and coordinated approvals.
Its qualifying projects can receive planning decisions in under twelve months.
Yet critics have challenged the adequacy of public consultation, while environmental litigation and proposed national resource-use rules create uncertainty.
The region’s ability to convert its pipeline into operational capacity will depend partly on reconciling those concerns. 
GVI implication: Execution speed is valuable only when it is institutionally sustainable.SIGNAL 3 — CAPITAL IS MOVING FROM ANNOUNCEMENT VALUE TO CASH-FLOW VALIDATION
The Firmus IPO withdrawal illustrates increasing investor differentiation between the scale of proposed AI infrastructure and its demonstrated economic viability.
The market is demanding clearer evidence of construction capability, customer commitments, capital requirements and operating returns. 
GVI implication: Forward-capacity scores must discount ambitious plans when financing and commercialization remain uncertain.
SIGNAL 4 — CHINA IS MOVING TOWARD INDUSTRIAL-AI DISCIPLINE
On October 9, China issued new guidelines emphasizing technological breakthroughs, industrial applications and stronger controls against speculative investment.
The policy calls for industrial-AI pilot bases while warning officials against excessive expansion and poorly justified projects. 
GVI implication: Beijing, Shanghai and Shenzhen retain strong industrialization potential, but capital efficiency and execution discipline become increasingly important differentiators.
SIGNAL 5 — THE SOCIAL LICENCE IS ENTERING THE INVESTMENT MODEL
The debate surrounding AI infrastructure is broadening from technical capacity to the allocation of costs and benefits.
Grid upgrades, land use, environmental approvals and electricity prices increasingly determine whether communities support new facilities.
The same project can create substantial private economic value while imposing local costs that are not reflected in its initial financial projections.
Recent disputes in Spain and Finland demonstrate how these pressures can affect execution schedules. 
GVI implication: Social acceptance must increasingly be assessed as an input into execution probability and project valuation.

VIII. THE THREE CONSEQUENCES
FOR CITIZENS — THE AI DIVIDEND MUST BECOME VISIBLE
The growth of AI infrastructure will increasingly create competing claims on public resources.
Electricity.
Land.
Water.
Housing.
Transport capacity.
Public investment.
The economic success of a city cannot therefore be judged solely by the value of investment it attracts.
The relevant test is whether new infrastructure contributes to higher real disposable incomes, reliable energy, affordable housing, accessible employment and better public services.
A city that attracts enormous AI capital while worsening everyday affordability risks weakening the very social legitimacy required for continued investment.
The citizen’s question is no longer how much AI investment is arriving, but how much of its productivity becomes a better urban life.
FOR INVESTORS — UNDERWRITE THE CITY, NOT JUST THE ASSET
AI investment is increasingly exposed to risks traditionally associated with infrastructure and real estate.
Project delays.
Grid connections.
Customer concentration.
Water availability.
Political intervention.
Financing costs.
The profitability of the asset cannot be separated from the capabilities of the surrounding urban system.
This changes investment underwriting.
A high-growth metropolitan economy with inadequate grid capacity may offer lower risk-adjusted returns than a smaller market where permits, power, construction and customers are aligned.
The investment opportunity extends beyond data centres to generation, grid equipment, storage, fibre, industrial property, transport and enabling infrastructure.
But scarcity alone does not guarantee attractive returns.The critical distinction is between scarcity that can be resolved profitably and scarcity that prevents capital from becoming productive.
FOR POLICYMAKERS — THE NEW OBJECTIVE IS PRODUCTIVE CONVERSION
Cities increasingly need integrated strategies connecting technology, energy, housing, mobility, skills and financing.
A standalone AI strategy is insufficient.
The policy objective should be to reduce the time and economic cost required to move from investment decisions to operational capacity.
That includes resolving bottlenecks before they become politically destabilizing.
It also requires mechanisms ensuring that residents receive tangible benefits from infrastructure investment.
The broader ambition is not simply faster development.
It is a more productive and socially sustainable metropolitan economy.

IX. FDK VELOCITY INSIGHT
THE CITY ENTERS THE CAPITAL STRUCTURE
The most consequential development of the week is not a change in the identity of the world’s leading cities.
It is a change in the financial mechanism determining their future.
The AI revolution is becoming increasingly dependent on physical assets with long construction periods, heavy financing requirements and complex local externalities.
That creates a new transmission chain:
INTELLIGENCE
↓
INFRASTRUCTURE DEMAND
↓
POWER + LAND + CAPITAL
↓
SOCIAL ACCEPTANCE + PERMISSION
↓
EXECUTION CERTAINTY
↓
COST OF CAPITAL
↓
PRODUCTIVE CAPACITY
↓
URBAN PRODUCTIVITY
↓
HUMAN DIVIDEND
The critical variable is the conversion mechanism between capital and productive capacity.
Two cities with similar AI capabilities can produce dramatically different outcomes.
One can convert investment into electricity, computing, housing, mobility and employment while maintaining public support.
The other can attract the same investment but experience grid congestion, housing inflation, political opposition and rising financing costs.
The difference is not technological.
It is institutional, social and financial.
That produces three general propositions.
First, the value of intelligence increasingly depends on the physical system capable of deploying it.
Second, the cost of capital increasingly reflects the execution capabilities of the geography in which that system operates.
Third, social cohesion can reinforce or undermine those execution capabilities.
These are causal hypotheses to be tested against project performance, borrowing costs, infrastructure delivery and urban outcomes—not assumptions that every city already demonstrates the same relationship.
Their combined implication is nevertheless powerful.
THE NEXT GREAT INVESTMENT MAP WILL BE A MAP OF URBAN PRODUCTIVE CAPACITY.
Singapore’s leadership illustrates the advantages of coordination.
Seoul demonstrates the benefits of connecting technological discovery with industrial production.
Madrid’s strengthening position reveals the potential of energy-connected regional infrastructure.
Finland illustrates both the opportunity created by energy-compute integration and the continuing importance of environmental governance.
Australia’s financing difficulties demonstrate that investors are becoming more demanding about the relationship between ambition and returns.
The great cities of the AI age will increasingly compete on the efficiency with which they convert global capital into local productivity—and local productivity into broadly shared prosperity.
This is where the financial and social dimensions of the Next Economy converge.
Capital needs the city to become productive.
The city needs capital to expand its productive frontier.
Both need public legitimacy for the process to remain sustainable.
The defining competition is therefore shifting from the concentration of intelligence toward the conversion of intelligence.
And that leads to the central GVI conclusion:
THE FUTURE BELONGS TO THE CITIES THAT CAN TURN INTELLIGENCE INTO ABUNDANCE WITHOUT TURNING PROSPERITY INTO SCARCITY.
