Beijing’s call to build one-person AI companies opens a new front in the U.S.–China AI supercycle
The Big Take
China is asking individuals to do what once took a founding team, an engineering budget and a first round of capital. Build a company. Use AI agents to write the code, create the marketing, answer customers and automate the back office. Start with a problem small enough for one person to understand and important enough for someone else to pay to solve.
That is the call to arms behind China’s one-person company movement. In Christopher Beam’s Bloomberg Businessweek reporting, municipal incubators offer workspace, subsidized AI tools and access to investors. Founder communities help people build and test products. The invitation reaches people who may never have considered starting a technology company, including workers between jobs. And in Hangzhou, one incubator judge estimated that only about 0.5% of admitted ventures became credible investment prospects. That is one judge’s estimate at one program. Its significance lies in the question it raises. Why encourage so many people to start companies when so few may ever be worth funding?
Because the strategic return can accrue to China even when the financial return eludes most founders. Every serious attempt can train an operator, expose a bottleneck in a real workflow or reveal where customers will pay for automation. Failed founders can take those skills into factories, exporters and service businesses. Platforms can learn which tools get used repeatedly. The state can gain a wider base of people capable of deploying AI. For that return to materialize, people have to test their products against paying demand, and the lessons have to travel beyond the incubator.
The United States has built a formidable engine for financing and developing frontier AI. China is trying to build an equally formidable engine for putting it to work. The next phase of the supercycle will be shaped by how fast each economy turns model capability into durable productivity and who captures the cash flows along the way.
1. The new front in the U.S.–China AI supercycle
Stanford’s 2026 AI Index puts the contrast in perspective. The United States recorded roughly 23 times China’s private AI investment in 2025, on Stanford’s measure, while leading on notable frontier models. Yet leading U.S. and Chinese models traded places on some benchmark comparisons after early 2025. The investment figures measure private capital, and model rankings measure performance on selected tasks. Their divergence shows why capital raised and benchmarks won are incomplete measures of competitive advantage. 
The race is now being fought on three fronts. Frontier capability determines which tasks become technically possible. Deployment determines how quickly those tasks become economical and reliable in real businesses. Value capture determines whether the returns accrue to model providers, cloud platforms, application companies or the customers using them. A country can lead on one front and find its companies surrendering margins on another.
China’s AI Plus plan makes diffusion an explicit policy objective. The State Council set targets for penetration of next-generation intelligent terminals and AI agents above 70% by 2027 and 90% by 2030. These are policy targets. The one-person company push gives that ambition a practical route to market. Instead of waiting for large enterprises to redesign their operations, municipalities can help thousands of individuals test narrower uses in parallel. 
China’s strongest opportunity may be where software meets its existing industrial base. The International Federation of Robotics reported 354,000 industrial robot installations in China in 2025. Consider the small team in Beam’s reporting adapting a robot dog to detect gas leaks.The commercial opportunity sits in the inspection workflow, where somebody has to integrate the hardware, understand the operating environment, verify the readings and earn the customer’s trust. Multiply that opportunity across machine maintenance, quality control and export operations, and the strategic value of mobilizing small teams becomes clearer. 
2. Why the call to build matters
AI reduces the minimum cost of attempting a business. A founder can prototype an application, translate the interface, draft sales material and handle early support before hiring across those functions. The biggest opportunity may lie in narrow markets that could sustain a profitable small business but would struggle to justify a conventional software team.
The movement also changes where competition takes place. As more people can produce competent software, video or marketing copy, the scarce resources become customer access, trust and the ability to deliver a dependable outcome. Beam’s microdrama reporting shows both sides of the shift. One writer said her per-script fee had fallen by almost half as more writers adopted AI. She also said her total income rose because she secured more work. Consumers and buyers can benefit from lower prices, output can rise, and pricing power can weaken at the same time.
This is the economic tension inside the mobilization. Beijing can spread the ability to produce while the returns concentrate in the platforms that supply compute, bring customers or handle transactions. It can also give displaced workers a route back into productive activity while transferring the volatility of self-employment onto households. The serious labor-market test is whether founders and displaced workers achieve sustained earnings, including after subsidies end.
3. The historical comparables
Precedent What it helps explain today
China’s early reform-era getihu China began issuing permits to individual businesses in the late 1970s, creating room for households and individuals to enter commerce. The one-person AI company revives that political logic with a different productive asset. A founder can now deploy software and rented compute alongside personal labor and local knowledge. 
China’s WTO accession and the manufacturing shock China joined the WTO in 2001. Research on the subsequent import shock found persistent employment and income effects in more exposed U.S. labor markets. The parallel is that a new source of low-cost supply can benefit buyers while imposing durable adjustment costs on particular workers and places. AI makes parts of services and digital production more exposed to that mechanism. 
China’s electric-vehicle expansion China produced about 16 million electric cars in 2025 and exported more than 2.5 million, according to the IEA. Intense domestic competition can yield internationally competitive products and put pressure on supplier and producer margins. In AI, that pressure could travel through model pricing, embedded software and digitally delivered services. 
Electrification and factory redesign Economic historian Paul David’s account of electrification shows why adoption can run ahead of productivity. Firms had to redesign factories and processes around electric power. AI faces a similar organizational test. Giving workers agents is the first step. Reworking decisions, approvals and accountability determines whether the gains persist. 
4. Why this time is different
A microenterprise can be global from inception. A founder can reach an overseas customer with translated software or a digital service before building an overseas sales operation. The competitive pressure can travel faster than it did in physical manufacturing. It will still encounter local rules on data, procurement and liability, as well as the practical demands of language and customer support. The WTO identifies digitally deliverable services as a major channel through which AI could reshape trade. 
The supplier can become the competitor in a single release.An application can be viable on Monday and face a model provider bundling its core feature on Friday. That exposure is greatest when the product depends on one model and offers a feature customers can easily obtain elsewhere. A founder with proprietary workflow data, a trusted customer relationship or integration into an operational system has more room to benefit from improvements across competing models.
Capital requirements diverge at opposite ends of the same industry. Frontier models draw on concentrated compute and investment. The application layer lets ever smaller teams rent that capability. A rise in the number of founders can therefore coincide with greater concentration of infrastructure and economic rents. For investors, the growth of an ecosystem and the returns of a particular layer are separate questions. 
The relevant unit of productivity is the completed, verified task. A published customer-support study found a 15% average increase in issues resolved per hour with AI assistance. A separate early-2025 experiment found that experienced developers working in familiar repositories took 19% longer when allowed to use the tools available at the time. Different tasks, users and systems produced different outcomes. For a one-person company, the cost of checking, correcting and supporting an agent’s work can decide whether an apparently automated business makes money. 
5. What comes next
First, a commercial shakeout. Generic content, basic marketing and thin applications built around a single model are likely to see prices fall fastest. A credible survivor will be able to point to repeat customers, a distribution channel it can defend and a contribution margin that covers inference, customer acquisition and human review. Some of the best businesses may look like specialist service firms with excellent software economics.
Second, a move into industrial and export workflows. Chinese manufacturers can use AI to quote faster, produce multilingual documentation, coordinate suppliers and support equipment abroad. In that scenario, China exports cheaper or better goods and improves the services attached to them. A factory that uses AI to shorten the time from inquiry to shipment may gain more economically than the small vendor that sold it the tool.
Third, stronger competition at the platform layer. U.S. cloud and model providers can benefit from higher usage even as competition compresses revenue per task. Chinese providers can gain adoption through inexpensive models and locally deployable systems while finding international monetization harder. The decisive financial question is whether incremental demand translates into cash returns after compute investment, or whether customers capture most of the savings.
Fourth, trade friction moves into the workflow. As agents handle sensitive tasks, procurement rules, data residency, auditability and liability can shape market access. Some countries may use one ecosystem for ordinary business software and another for sensitive industrial or government workloads. Cross-border adoption will depend as much on who can inspect and govern the system as on the price of a model call.
6. Key takeaways for investors and governments
Audience What to look for
Investors in AI infrastructure Track cost and revenue per successfully completed task, paid utilization and cash returns on incremental capex. Model price cuts can expand demand and still compress returns if utilization or pricing disappoints.
Investors in applications Underwrite customer retention, distribution, workflow depth and gross margin after inference and human review. Ask what customers would still pay for if a major platform bundled the core feature.
Investors in established industries Look for companies that can keep part of the productivity gain through faster delivery, lower defect rates, improved service or lower working capital. A low-cost model can create more value for an industrial adopter than for its provider.
U.S. government Pair frontier investment with adoption.Help smaller firms and industrial suppliers test AI through extension programs, shared evaluation facilities and procurement pilots with measurable operating outcomes. Preserve opportunities for junior workers to build the experience that future teams will need.
Chinese government Judge the one-person company campaign by unsubsidized revenue, repeat customers, founder earnings and skills that carry over into other jobs. Tie continued support to those outcomes so local programs have an incentive to identify commercially viable uses.
European governments Use industrial buyers and public procurement to give smaller AI suppliers a first credible customer. Make switching between models practical and build demand for systems that can be audited and deployed under local requirements.
7. Conclusion
China’s call to build one-person companies puts entrepreneurial experimentation at the center of industrial policy. The wager is that a large population of founders, freelancers and small teams can discover commercially valuable uses for AI faster than any central plan can specify them. The danger is that the same mobilization produces crowded markets, unstable incomes and attractive metrics for municipal officials while leaving customers and cash flows elusive.
For the United States, frontier leadership becomes more valuable when businesses can deploy it at scale. For China, diffusion becomes more valuable when it lifts productivity and produces customers who return after the subsidy expires. For investors, the opportunity may sit with the business that uses cheaper intelligence to improve an expensive workflow, even when another company supplies the model.
The next AI advantage will belong to the economy that gets from one founder’s prototype to a thousand paying customers fastest.
