There is a meaningful change. Over the past week, the Federal Reserve, ECB and Bank of England have moved beyond treating AI primarily as a future productivity story. They are now incorporating the AI Supercycle into the present policy problem — through inflation, capital formation, energy demand, labour-market reallocation and financial stability.

The emerging sequence is increasingly clear: **AI capex → near-term demand + bottleneck inflation → higher-for-longer rates → debt-financed scale-up → productivity dividend later.** That is the Great Split in monetary-policy form. The inflationary investment phase is arriving before the deflationary productivity phase.

::pull THE NEW CENTRAL-BANK EQUATION
AI is not yet deflationary enough to justify lower rates. It is already capital-intensive enough to affect inflation, yields and financial stability.

::signals Five Developments That Matter | How AI entered the reaction function
# The Fed is treating AI investment as an inflation shock
On September 28, Governor Lisa Cook said AI-related prices have surged and that data-centre construction, electricity and other shared inputs are spreading inflation beyond technology itself. She expects AI productivity eventually to be disinflationary, but only modestly and not soon enough to offset current pressures. On October 1 she described the AI build-out and its inflationary effects as one of her principal risks for 2027.
signal: AI is now an inflation input, not just a future productivity bet.

# AI has entered the Fed's macro reaction function
Vice Chair Philip Jefferson said on October 1 that the U.S. economy is being hit simultaneously by an energy shock, trade-policy changes and a "massive AI infrastructure buildout." He identified AI spending as a major support for business investment and GDP but also as a contributor to core-goods inflation. With unemployment near 4.1% and inflation still above target, the Fed can tolerate less inflation from the investment boom than markets previously assumed.
signal: The build-out supports growth and feeds core-goods inflation at once.

# The Bank of England has identified AI leverage
Its September 30 Financial Policy Committee record says global AI-related debt issuance had reached about $450 billion by early September — more than twice the whole of 2025. It cited roughly $4.1 trillion of AI capex financed by debt between 2026 and 2030 and around $700 billion of data-centre investment financed through private credit between 2026 and 2028. The critical new point: the BoE explicitly links disappointment in AI productivity not only to technology valuations, but to sovereign bond markets and fiscal outlooks.
signal: If the dividend fails, the correction won't stay inside tech equities.

# The ECB is moving to systemic-dependency risk
Christine Lagarde said on October 1 that nearly nine in ten significant euro-area banks already use generative AI. Her concern is increasingly the interaction between agentic trading, common frontier models, cyber risk and geopolitical dependence. She argued that Europe needs its own AI capabilities and a position in the AI supply chain because access to models essential to financial resilience cannot ultimately depend on decisions taken abroad.
signal: AI capability is becoming part of monetary sovereignty.

# The policy problem is asymmetric across the Atlantic
The United States is confronting too much AI capital formation relative to short-run physical capacity; Europe confronts too little indigenous AI capacity combined with exposure to energy, foreign frontier models and fragmented capital markets. Singapore remains closer to an enable-and-govern model: MAS is concentrating on AI diffusion, workforce adaptation and trusted capital formation rather than signalling an AI-driven monetary-policy adjustment.
signal: Different continents face opposite versions of the same shock.

::section What Has Changed | From a one-way disinflation story to a two-phase sequence
The old central-bank assumption was broadly: *AI → productivity → higher potential growth → lower unit costs → disinflation.*

The new sequence is more complex: *AI → capital formation → energy + construction + chips + skilled-labour scarcity → inflation first* — and only later: *diffusion → productivity → lower unit costs → industrial-AI deflation.*

This changes the policy horizon enormously. The Fed is effectively saying the transition itself may require tighter monetary conditions before AI earns central banks room to ease. The BoE is saying that if the promised productivity dividend fails to arrive, the correction will not remain inside technology equities, because the build-out is increasingly financed through corporate bonds, private credit and leveraged capital structures. And the ECB is recognising a distinctly European problem: AI capability itself is becoming part of financial resilience and monetary sovereignty.

::section The Neutral-Rate Implication | A time-varying r*
No major central bank formally changed its estimate of r* this week. But the direction of travel is important. During the infrastructure phase, AI can plausibly push the equilibrium real rate upward through extraordinary investment demand, greater expected returns on capital and stronger productivity expectations. Later, broad productivity diffusion could expand supply and reduce inflation pressure.

The consequence is a time-varying neutral rate: in the **capex phase, r* up**; in the **productivity-diffusion phase, inflation down**. Central banks therefore face an identification problem — easing too early because they anticipate future productivity could accommodate today's investment inflation.

::section The Labour-Market Paradox | When unemployment no longer means slack
Cook also sharpened an issue with major implications for the dual mandate. AI unemployment may not always represent deficient aggregate demand. It could reflect a skills mismatch produced by technological reallocation. In that case, cutting rates to reduce unemployment risks stimulating demand without solving the underlying mismatch — and could therefore increase inflation.

That is a profound change: ==higher unemployment may no longer automatically mean more slack.==

::patterns The Cross-Asset Transmission | Where the Great Split widens
# Capacity vs capture
The first phase rewarded capacity creation. The next phase will increasingly test economic capture — distinguishing companies that convert AI capex into productivity and cash flow from those whose valuations require indefinitely accelerating investment.
# Cash-flow vs debt-funded AI
The split widens between AI financed from cash flow and AI financed through corporate debt, private credit and leveraged structures exposed to higher long-end yields.
# Energy producers vs importers
It widens between economies able to produce energy and those forced to import it to power the build-out.
# Owners vs consumers
And between countries that own critical AI infrastructure and those that merely consume it.

::pull THE TRANSMISSION MECHANISM
AI capex → corporate debt → energy + real-rate pressure → higher long-end yields → higher hurdle rates → productivity test → valuation dispersion.

::section The EU Policy Implication | One coherent message
For Europe, the central-bank message is becoming unusually coherent. AI policy can no longer be separated into industrial policy, energy policy, financial policy and monetary policy. Europe needs compute, grids, storage, capital markets and domestic AI capabilities simultaneously.

Otherwise the ECB faces the worst version of the Supercycle: the inflationary costs of AI investment without the full productivity dividend of AI ownership. That is the emerging European policy risk.

::takeaways Key takeaways | The monetary regime meets the Supercycle
- AI has entered the reaction functions of the Fed, ECB and BoE — not just their research agendas.
- The inflationary investment phase arrives before the deflationary productivity phase.
- AI implies a time-varying r*: up during capex, disinflationary only after diffusion.
- Debt-financed AI threatens to spill from tech equities into bond and fiscal markets.
- Europe risks the costs of AI investment without the dividend of AI ownership.

::close The decisive question is no longer whether AI raises productivity. It is whether ==productivity arrives before the cost of financing the AI Supercycle forces the monetary regime to change.==
