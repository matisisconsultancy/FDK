THE GREAT SPLIT HAS ENTERED THE REACTION FUNCTION

The Big Take

There is a meaningful change. Over the past week, the Federal Reserve, ECB and Bank of England have moved beyond treating AI primarily as a future productivity story. They are now incorporating the AI Supercycle into the present policy problem — through inflation, capital formation, energy demand, labour-market reallocation and financial stability.

The emerging sequence is increasingly clear:

AI CAPEX → NEAR-TERM DEMAND + BOTTLENECK INFLATION → HIGHER-FOR-LONGER RATES → DEBT-FINANCED SCALE-UP → PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND LATER

That is the Great Split in monetary-policy form. The inflationary investment phase is arriving before the deflationary productivity phase.

Five developments matter most:

• 1. The Fed is explicitly treating AI investment as an inflation shock. On September 28, Governor Lisa Cook said AI-related prices have surged and that data-centre construction, electricity and other shared inputs are spreading inflation beyond technology itself. She expects AI productivity eventually to be disinflationary, but only modestly and not soon enough to offset current pressures. On October 1 she went further, describing the AI build-out and its inflationary effects as one of her principal risks for 2027.  Lisa Cook — An Update on AI and the Economy, September 28, 2026⁠
• 2. AI has entered the Fed’s macro reaction function, not merely its research agenda. Vice Chair Philip Jefferson said on October 1 that the U.S. economy is being hit simultaneously by an energy shock, trade-policy changes and a “massive AI infrastructure buildout.” He identified AI spending as a major support for business investment and GDP but also as a contributor to core-goods inflation. With unemployment near 4.1% and inflation still above target, the Fed can tolerate less inflation from the investment boom than markets previously assumed.  Philip Jefferson — The U.S. Economy and Monetary Policy, October 1, 2026⁠
• 3. The Bank of England has identified the second-order risk: AI leverage. Its September 30 Financial Policy Committee record says global AI-related debt issuance had reached about $450 billion by early September — more than twice the whole of 2025. It cited estimates of roughly $4.1 trillion of AI capex financed by debt between 2026 and 2030 and around $700 billion of data-centre investment financed through private credit between 2026 and 2028. The critical new point is that the BoE explicitly links disappointment in AI productivity not only to technology valuations, but to sovereign bond markets and fiscal outlooks.  Bank of England — September 2026 FPC Record⁠
• 4. The ECB is moving from AI adoption risk to systemic-dependency risk. Christine Lagarde said on October 1 that nearly nine in ten significant euro-area banks already use generative AI. Her concern is increasingly the interaction between agentic trading, common frontier models, cyber risk and geopolitical dependence. Crucially, she argued that Europe needs its own AI capabilities and a position in the AI supply chain because access to models essential to financial resilience cannot ultimately depend on decisions taken abroad.  Christine Lagarde — Where AI Risks Meet, October 1, 2026⁠
• 5. The policy problem is becoming asymmetric across the Atlantic. The United States is confronting too much AI capital formation relative to short-run physical capacity; Europe confronts too little indigenous AI capacity combined with exposure to energy, foreign frontier models and fragmented capital markets. Singapore remains closer to an enable-and-govern model: MAS is concentrating on AI diffusion, workforce adaptation and trusted capital formation rather than signalling an AI-driven monetary-policy adjustment. 

What has changed

The old central-bank assumption was broadly:

AI → PRODUCTIVITY → HIGHER POTENTIAL GROWTH → LOWER UNIT COSTS → DISINFLATION

The new sequence is more complex:

AI → CAPITAL FORMATION → ENERGY + CONSTRUCTION + CHIPS + SKILLED LABOUR SCARCITY → INFLATION FIRST

and only later:DIFFUSION → PRODUCTIVITY → LOWER UNIT COSTS → INDUSTRIAL-AI DEFLATION

This changes the policy horizon enormously.

The Fed is effectively saying that the transition itself may require tighter monetary conditions before AI earns central banks room to ease.

The BoE is saying that if the promised productivity dividend fails to arrive, the correction will not remain inside technology equities because the AI build-out is increasingly financed through corporate bonds, private credit and leveraged capital structures.

And the ECB is recognizing a distinctly European problem: AI capability itself is becoming part of financial resilience and monetary sovereignty.

The neutral-rate implication

No major central bank formally changed its estimate of r* this week. But the direction of travel is important.

During the infrastructure phase, AI can plausibly push the equilibrium real rate upward through extraordinary investment demand, greater expected returns on capital and stronger productivity expectations.

Later, broad productivity diffusion could expand supply and reduce inflation pressure.

The consequence is that AI may produce a time-varying neutral rate:

CAPEX PHASE → r* UP

PRODUCTIVITY-DIFFUSION PHASE → INFLATION DOWN

Central banks therefore face an identification problem: easing too early because they anticipate future productivity could accommodate today’s investment inflation.

The labour-market paradox

Cook also sharpened another issue with major implications for the dual mandate.

AI unemployment may not always represent deficient aggregate demand. It could reflect a skills mismatch produced by technological reallocation. In that case, cutting rates to reduce unemployment risks stimulating demand without solving the underlying mismatch — and could therefore increase inflation. 

That is a profound change in the monetary-policy problem:

HIGHER UNEMPLOYMENT MAY NO LONGER AUTOMATICALLY MEAN MORE SLACK.

Investor implication

The AI trade is therefore entering a harder phase.

The first phase rewarded capacity creation.

The next phase will increasingly test economic capture.

Markets will need to distinguish between companies capable of converting AI capital expenditure into productivity and cash flow, and those whose valuations require indefinitely accelerating investment.

The most important cross-asset transmission mechanism is becoming:

AI CAPEX
→ CORPORATE DEBT
→ ENERGY + REAL-RATE PRESSURE
→ HIGHER LONG-END YIELDS
→ HIGHER HURDLE RATES
→ PRODUCTIVITY TEST
→ VALUATION DISPERSION

The Great Split should therefore widen — not only between AI winners and losers, but between cash-flow-funded and debt-funded AI, between economies able to produce energy and those forced to import it, and between countries that own critical AI infrastructure and those that merely consume it.

EU policy implication

For Europe, the central-bank message is becoming unusually coherent.

AI policy can no longer be separated into industrial policy, energy policy, financial policy and monetary policy.

Europe needs compute, grids, storage, capital markets and domestic AI capabilities simultaneously.

Otherwise the ECB faces the worst version of the Supercycle:

the inflationary costs of AI investment without the full productivity dividend of AI ownership.

That is the emerging European policy risk.

THE NEW CENTRAL-BANK EQUATION

AI IS NOT YET DEFLATIONARY ENOUGH TO JUSTIFY LOWER RATES.

IT IS ALREADY CAPITAL-INTENSIVE ENOUGH TO AFFECT INFLATION, YIELDS AND FINANCIAL STABILITY.

The decisive question for the next phase of the cycle is therefore no longer whether AI raises productivity.

It is whether productivity arrives before the cost of financing the AI Supercycle forces the monetary regime to change.
