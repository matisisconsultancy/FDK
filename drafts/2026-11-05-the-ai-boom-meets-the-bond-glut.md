How Rising Neutral Rates, Non-Bank Leverage and Autonomous Finance Are Redefining Monetary Policy—and the Next Rotation of Global Capital

THE BIG TAKE — The AI Supercycle is beginning to reshape not only the demand for capital, but also the mechanisms through which central banks control its price and financial markets distribute its risks.

Three consequential developments between November 4 and November 5 reveal a new phase in the Great Split.

First, ECB Executive Board member Isabel Schnabel has explicitly linked the expansion of AI-related investment, rising public borrowing requirements and the transition from a global savings glut to a bond glut. She argues that these structural forces are placing upward pressure on the natural rate of interest.

Second, the US Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee has challenged an increasingly popular market assumption: despite extraordinary AI-related corporate borrowing, there is currently no evidence that technology issuance is directly crowding out demand for long-term US government bonds. The more important transmission may be through stronger growth expectations and higher equilibrium interest rates.

Third, the institutional response to autonomous finance is accelerating. The ECB is exploring AI-agent payments within its digital euro innovation programme, while the US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency has issued a new supervisory framework addressing AI systems capable of acting with greater autonomy.

The central question is changing. It is no longer simply how AI affects inflation and productivity. It is whether monetary authorities can preserve control over financial conditions as capital formation becomes more concentrated, financial intermediation migrates outside banks, and increasingly autonomous systems participate in financial transactions.

This is a material development for monetary policy, financial stability and European capital-market integration.

1. THE ECB’S MOST CONSEQUENTIAL SIGNAL: THE SAVINGS GLUT HAS BECOME A BOND GLUT

Schnabel’s November 5 speech, Navigating Stormy Waters: Reflections on Monetary Policy, provides the strongest new monetary-policy signal.

She identifies a structural change in the global balance between savings and investment.

For years, abundant savings and relatively weak investment contributed to downward pressure on equilibrium interest rates.

That environment is changing.

AI infrastructure, broader digital transformation, energy investment, defence expenditure and government financing requirements are increasing demand for capital.

The resulting expansion in public and private debt issuance is contributing to what Schnabel describes as a bond glut.

This shift creates upward pressure on the natural rate of interest, r, even before considering temporary inflation shocks.*

Importantly, Schnabel does not claim that AI alone has permanently raised the neutral rate, nor does she announce a new numerical estimate. Her argument concerns the combined effect of structural investment and financing requirements. 

THE HIDDEN PATTERN: HIGHER PRODUCTIVITY MAY COEXIST WITH HIGHER INTEREST RATES

The conventional assumption that technological progress inevitably produces lower inflation and cheaper money is incomplete.

The AI Supercycle creates competing monetary forces.

AI CAPITAL FORMATION → HIGHER INVESTMENT DEMAND → GREATER FINANCING REQUIREMENTS → POTENTIALLY HIGHER NEUTRAL RATES.

Over a longer horizon:

INDUSTRIAL AI ADOPTION → PRODUCTIVITY GROWTH → LOWER UNIT PRODUCTION COSTS → POTENTIAL DISINFLATION.

These processes operate on different timescales.

The first can raise financing costs before the second generates measurable productivity improvements.

The central risk for Europe is that the global cost of capital increases faster than European businesses can implement technologies capable of improving their returns.The Great Split may therefore emerge through the discount rate before becoming fully visible in productivity statistics.

2. THE US TREASURY CHALLENGES THE CROWDING-OUT THESIS

The US Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee’s November 4 report adds an important qualification.

Despite heavy technology-related debt issuance, the committee finds no evidence that borrowing to finance AI infrastructure has systematically weakened demand at long-term Treasury auctions.

Its conclusion is particularly revealing.

To the extent that technology investment is influencing Treasury yields, the committee considers stronger economic growth a more convincing explanation than direct displacement of government-bond demand.

It also identifies three emerging financial mechanisms.

Technology issuance is widening borrowing spreads within parts of the sector.

Lenders are extending financing through GPU-backed loans, equipment leases and data-centre project structures.

International investment flows into American technology are contributing to dollar strength, creating an additional channel through which global financial conditions can tighten.

These conclusions are based on the committee’s November 3 meeting and November 4 report. They are not a Federal Reserve policy decision. 

THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT: THE CAPITAL SQUEEZE IS NOT UNIFORM

This distinction is critical for investors.

The AI Supercycle may not yet be displacing demand for sovereign debt.

But competition for capital is already becoming more consequential among corporate borrowers.

The largest technology companies can finance investment through substantial operating cash flows, equity issuance and investment-grade debt.

Less established infrastructure companies may depend more heavily on secured lending, project finance and refinancing markets.

The resulting asymmetry can widen as interest rates remain elevated.

The next financial differentiation may occur within the AI ecosystem itself: between companies generating sufficient cash flow to finance expansion and companies whose valuations depend on continuous access to increasingly selective capital markets.

The Treasury report also observes that strong investment in computing equipment, intellectual property and data centres has supported economic activity without yet producing a comparable broad acceleration in employment.

This reinforces the Great Split thesis: capital formation is advancing faster than the diffusion of its labour-market benefits. 

3. THE ECB’S WARNING: MONETARY POLICY COULD BECOME HOSTAGE TO NON-BANK FINANCE

Schnabel’s second major argument is potentially even more consequential.

The expansion of non-bank financial intermediation, combined with higher sovereign debt, could undermine central banks’ ability to conduct monetary policy independently.

Non-bank institutions now hold financial assets comparable in scale to those held by banks.

At the same time, euro-area public debt has increased by approximately 20 percentage points of GDP since the early 2000s.

A growing proportion of sovereign debt is intermediated by investment funds and leveraged institutions that depend on short-term market financing.

This changes monetary transmission.

Higher policy rates can increase financing costs rapidly.

Falling government-bond prices can trigger margin calls and forced asset sales.

Financial-market instability can then oblige central banks to intervene to preserve liquidity, potentially complicating their inflation-control responsibilities. 

THE FINANCIAL-STABILITY IMPLICATION

The potential transmission sequence is:

MONETARY TIGHTENING → HIGHER FUNDING COSTS → LEVERAGED NON-BANK DELEVERAGING → SOVEREIGN-BOND VOLATILITY → LIQUIDITY PRESSURE → TIGHTER CORPORATE FINANCING.

AI introduces an additional, indirect vulnerability through the increasing financing requirements and market valuations associated with technological investment.

A disorderly repricing of AI-related credit could coincide with stress in sovereign funding markets.Such a combination would make it more difficult for monetary authorities to distinguish the appropriate interest-rate stance from the need to stabilise financial markets.

This is a risk scenario, not evidence that such a crisis is currently unfolding.

Schnabel advocates stronger oversight of non-bank institutions rather than weakening the regulatory safeguards applying to commercial banks.

She also raises the possibility that more robust non-bank regulation could support consideration of access to central-bank liquidity facilities.

The implication is consequential: the institutional perimeter of monetary and financial stability may need to evolve alongside the structure of financial intermediation.

4. AUTONOMOUS FINANCE: CENTRAL BANKS BEGIN PREPARING FOR AI-AGENT PAYMENTS

A separate November 5 ECB speech identifies another emerging frontier.

Speaking in Tirana, Executive Board member Piero Cipollone outlined the Eurosystem’s strategy for digital payments and tokenised finance.

The ECB’s digital euro innovation programme is examining potential future applications involving AI-agent payments and micropayments.

These are exploratory activities, not approved autonomous payment services or an announcement that the digital euro has been issued.

The ECB also reports that its digital euro pilot is planned for the second half of 2027, with 36 payment service providers selected.

Meanwhile, its Pontes infrastructure is already facilitating settlement of tokenised financial instruments in central-bank money. Cipollone reported that the European Stability Mechanism recently completed a €1 billion tokenised benchmark-bond transaction using the system. 

The significance is structural.

As AI agents become capable of initiating purchases, executing transactions and managing financial processes, payment systems will require stronger mechanisms for authorisation, accountability and operational resilience.

Central-bank money may become an important settlement foundation for this increasingly automated financial architecture.

THE US SUPERVISORY RESPONSE

On November 4, the OCC issued its Super Intelligence: Supervisory Framework.

Despite the terminology, the document covers technologies and systems previously encompassed by the established definition of artificial intelligence, including agentic applications.

Its central principle is that supervisory attention should reflect the consequences of an AI system’s actions rather than technological sophistication alone.

An apparently simple agent capable of initiating consequential transactions may warrant stronger controls than a sophisticated model restricted to internal analysis.

The framework is guidance for examiners. It does not establish new binding requirements for banks or treat AI as a separate category of financial risk. 

The emerging supervisory principle is that autonomy, operational authority and potential financial consequences increasingly determine the intensity of oversight.

For European banks, this introduces an additional dimension of competitive differentiation.

Institutions that can deploy AI agents safely while maintaining human accountability, transaction controls and operational resilience may capture efficiency improvements without assuming disproportionate operational risks.

5. THE GREAT SPLIT — THREE FINANCIAL SCENARIOS

The latest developments suggest three possible paths.

SCENARIO I — PRODUCTIVITY-LED CAPITAL EXPANSION

AI investment remains strong, but productivity improvements spread progressively across manufacturing and services.

Corporate profitability strengthens, financing remains accessible, and higher equilibrium real interest rates are supported by sustainable growth.

This is the most constructive scenario for long-term capital formation.

SCENARIO II — HIGHER-FOR-LONGER CAPITAL COSTS

Energy constraints, public borrowing and AI infrastructure expenditure sustain elevated financing demand.

Productivity improvements remain concentrated.Corporate spreads widen, and businesses with weaker balance sheets postpone technological investment.

This scenario could deepen the Great Split between capital-rich companies and the broader productive economy.

SCENARIO III — FINANCIAL REPRICING AND MONETARY CONSTRAINT

Disappointing AI investment returns trigger a correction across technology equities and credit.

Leveraged non-bank institutions experience funding pressures, while sovereign markets remain vulnerable to higher financing costs.

Central banks confront a difficult combination of persistent inflation and financial instability.

The decisive distinction between these scenarios is whether AI investment generates sufficient sustainable cash flow and productivity to support its financing requirements.

6. FIVE TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS AND EUROPEAN POLICYMAKERS

1. Reassess the neutral-rate assumption. The transition from savings abundance to stronger investment and borrowing demand could support structurally higher equilibrium interest rates. Long-duration valuations should be evaluated under multiple real-rate scenarios rather than assuming an eventual return to exceptionally cheap capital.

2. Distinguish sovereign crowding-out from corporate capital competition. The Treasury’s latest findings do not support claims that AI issuance has already displaced demand for US government bonds. However, increasingly differentiated credit conditions within technology and infrastructure financing deserve attention.

3. Treat non-bank leverage as a critical transmission channel. Sovereign-bond financing, repo markets, private credit and leveraged investment funds may increasingly determine how monetary tightening affects financial stability.

4. Position for the transition from AI infrastructure to Industrial AI productivity. European investors should prioritise evidence of improved operating margins, unit-cost reductions, capital efficiency and durable cash-flow generation rather than relying exclusively on AI capital-expenditure announcements.

5. Accelerate Europe’s financial integration. Schnabel explicitly links stronger productivity to innovation, technological diffusion and the ability of European firms to scale. Her support for a common European corporate framework reinforces the need to remove barriers to cross-border investment and growth financing.

The policy objective should be to strengthen Europe’s capacity to finance productive transformation without transferring excessive financial risk to banks, public finances or central-bank balance sheets.

WHAT COMES NEXT — THE MONETARY TEST

The Bank of England’s November 5 monetary-policy decision remains the most immediate event to monitor.

At the time of this assessment, the decision has not yet been published. The official release is scheduled for noon London time.

The existing Bank Rate is 3.75%. Markets widely expect an increase to 4.00%, but that remains a forecast rather than an announced decision. 

Investors should focus on the committee’s voting distribution, updated inflation projections, treatment of energy-related second-round effects and its assessment of business investment and employment.

A separate development arrived from Norges Bank this morning: Norway maintained its policy rate at 4.50%, while preserving the possibility of additional tightening should inflation remain persistent. The decision reinforces the divergence in monetary responses to inflation pressures across European economies. 

No new formal AI-specific monetary-policy framework or neutral-rate estimate has been announced by the ECB, Federal Reserve, Bank of England, MAS or SNB in the developments reviewed.

THE FINAL TAKE — THE NEXT MONETARY REGIME WILL BE DECIDED IN THE CAPITAL MARKETS

November 5 establishes an important shift in the central-bank debate.

The ECB is increasingly recognising that rising investment requirements, public borrowing and structural financial changes are transforming the environment in which monetary policy operates.The US Treasury is providing evidence that AI-driven capital formation can influence interest rates through stronger growth expectations without necessarily displacing government-bond demand.

And central banks and supervisors are beginning to prepare for financial systems in which increasingly autonomous technologies may influence transaction execution and operational risk.

These developments point toward a more complex monetary environment.

The decisive variable will be the relationship between the marginal cost of capital and the marginal productivity generated by that capital.

For investors, that relationship will increasingly determine valuations, credit differentiation and market rotation.

For central banks, it will influence the difficult balance between inflation control, financial stability and the functioning of increasingly complex capital markets.

For European policymakers, it reinforces the urgency of building financial institutions and markets capable of financing the industrial transformation ahead.

VELOCITY INSIGHT

The next Great Split will not simply separate those who possess intelligence from those who do not. It will separate economies capable of financing intelligence into productivity from those whose financial structures turn technological opportunity into a higher cost of capital.
