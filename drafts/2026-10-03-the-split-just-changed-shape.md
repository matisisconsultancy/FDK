Washington still controls the frontier. Beijing is learning to route around it.
There is a consequential inflection.
The core thesis is strengthening, but in a more important way than a simple benchmark race. The United States still holds the superior frontier stack. China is increasingly attacking the economics, software dependency and geographic enforceability of that advantage.
The Big Take
Three developments now fit together.
First, Tencent has reportedly agreed to a five-year, roughly $7 billion lease for access to about 100,000 advanced AI chips in Oracle data centers across Southeast Asia—hardware unavailable for direct purchase in China. Reuters reported the FT disclosure on October 1; Oracle and Tencent had not publicly confirmed the deal. 
Second, DeepSeek and Huawei have opened a second front: software sovereignty. DeepSeek has open-sourced compute and communications tools optimized for Huawei Ascend chips, and the two companies jointly advanced a 128-chip Ascend 950 “supernode.” DeepSeek explicitly framed TileLang as part of an independent GPU software ecosystem and as a simpler alternative to CUDA-style programming. 
Third, the U.S. frontier lead is becoming dramatically more capital intensive. Reuters reported today, October 3, that U.S. AI investment could reach roughly $9 trillion between 2025 and 2032 under one estimate, while the U.S. sector would need about $3.55 trillion of annual revenue by 2032 to earn a 10% return on that capital. Bain estimates hyperscalers and other AI infrastructure builders need more than $4.2 trillion in new revenue over five years to justify the buildout. 
That changes the strategic equation.
THE U.S. LEADS IN FRONTIER CAPABILITY.
CHINA IS ATTACKING THE COST OF ACCESS, DEPLOYMENT AND DIFFUSION.
The Tencent structure matters because it suggests export controls may constrain ownership of advanced chips inside China more effectively than they constrain access to frontier compute abroad. If that model scales, Chinese firms can rent U.S.-class compute offshore for training while deploying domestically on increasingly capable Huawei infrastructure.
The DeepSeek–Huawei collaboration attacks the second moat: CUDA lock-in. The hardware gap remains meaningful, but China is trying to reduce the software penalty that makes non-Nvidia accelerators difficult to use at scale. 
Meanwhile, Google’s Gemini 4 “Argon,” unveiled September 30, reinforces that the American frontier remains very strong: Google described it as comparable with OpenAI’s Astra and Anthropic’s Opus on key coding and cyber benchmarks, even though it still trails them on some measures. 
The hidden pattern
China does not necessarily need to beat the U.S. chip-for-chip.
Its emerging strategy can be:
Offshore frontier compute for training
→ domestic accelerators for deployment
→ open software to weaken CUDA dependence
→ low-cost models
→ massive distribution through Chinese platforms and industry.
That is potentially more dangerous to the U.S. economic moat than a headline benchmark victory.
Because it changes the contest from:
Who has the best model?
to:
WHO CAN DELIVER THE MOST INTELLIGENCE PER DOLLAR OF CAPITAL?
And that takes us directly back to the capital-efficiency thesis.
The United States is scaling the most powerful AI infrastructure system ever built. But its hurdle rate is rising with every incremental gigawatt, GPU cluster and financing structure.
China is pursuing a different optimization function:
**less dependence
lower inference cost
higher utilization
wider industrial diffusion.**
What this means for the AI Supercycle
The first-order conclusion remains bullish for accelerated computing: both systems are still demanding more compute.
But the second-order investment map is changing.
The premium should increasingly migrate from pure capacity toward capital efficiency, utilization, networking, power efficiency, inference economics and software abstraction.
The U.S. advantage remains frontier capability.China’s emerging advantage is becoming economic diffusion.
And the most important contest may now sit between them:
CAPABILITY vs. UTILIZATION.
Five signals to watch next
1. Whether Washington closes the offshore-cloud route. A regulatory move against remote access to advanced accelerators would directly attack the Tencent model.
2. Adoption of DeepSeek/Huawei tooling. If other Chinese labs move onto Ascend through the new open software stack, CUDA dependence begins to weaken structurally.
3. Tencent model progress. If Hunyuan improves materially after access to offshore frontier compute, this becomes evidence that cloud geography can partially bypass hardware geography.
4. China’s inference pricing. If model prices continue falling faster than U.S. prices, China keeps pressure on industry-wide AI rents.
5. U.S. AI revenue versus AI capital employed. Today’s Reuters numbers make this the decisive financial metric of the next phase. 
THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
The U.S.–China AI race is no longer simply about denying China the best chips.
China is building a three-part workaround:
RENT THE FRONTIER.
REBUILD THE STACK.
UNDERCUT THE ECONOMICS.
The United States still leads the frontier.
But China increasingly does not need to own the frontier to challenge its economics.
That makes the next phase of the AI Supercycle much more consequential:
AMERICA IS BUILDING THE MOST POWERFUL AI SYSTEM.
CHINA IS TRYING TO MAKE AI CHEAPER, MORE PORTABLE AND HARDER TO CONTAIN.
