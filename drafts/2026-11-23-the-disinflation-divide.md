Singapore’s latest inflation figures reveal why central banks must distinguish easing energy costs from the productivity dividend of AI.
The Big Take
A consequential new monetary signal has emerged from Singapore.
On November 23, the Monetary Authority of Singapore reported that October core inflation fell to 1.5% from 2.0%, while headline inflation eased to 1.9% from 2.2%. Electricity and gas inflation declined particularly sharply, from 8.7% to 1.0%. MAS +1

The distinction is critical. Falling inflation is not necessarily evidence that AI productivity has arrived. Singapore’s improvement was largely driven by electricity tariffs and slower services price increases, rather than demonstrated economy-wide AI-induced unit-cost reductions.
Yet MAS continues to identify both directions of the AI shock: stronger investment may sustain domestic inflation, while a sharp reversal in AI capital expenditure could weaken growth and inflation. Its existing October monetary framework therefore remains relevant; today’s release does not establish a new policy decision. October 2026 +1
A second development: Asian financing architecture
Also on November 23, MAS and the People’s Bank of China announced expanded renminbi financing arrangements, including corporate facilities that can support capital expenditure, alongside broader liquidity and collateral mechanisms.
These measures are not specifically targeted at AI, but they expand the regional infrastructure through which capital-intensive investment can be financed. MAS
For investors, the implication is a potential divergence between easier local inflation conditions and continued access to investment financing.
The implications for investors and EU policymakers
For equity markets, softer energy inflation improves operating-cost conditions. The decisive test remains whether AI adopters produce measurable productivity and free-cash-flow gains rather than simply benefiting from temporarily lower input costs.
For credit markets, lower inflation may ease risk-free-yield pressure, but it does not automatically reduce credit spreads or default risk. If the AI investment cycle weakens, borrowers exposed to speculative capacity and refinancing needs could deteriorate even as government yields decline.
For the ECB and European policymakers, Singapore illustrates the need to distinguish three variables: imported energy disinflation, domestically generated productivity, and AI-related investment demand. Treating all three as one inflation signal could lead to monetary-policy miscalibration.

Velocity Insight
THE FIRST DECLINE IN INFLATION DOES NOT PROVE THAT THE PRODUCTIVITY REVOLUTION HAS ARRIVED.
The decisive monetary transition occurs when sustained productivity gains—not temporary reductions in energy costs—begin to lower unit production costs.
That is when the AI Supercycle can move from an inflationary investment boom toward a durable expansion of productive capacity.
