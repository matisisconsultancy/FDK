United States — the first clear evidence of an AI underwriting split
The most consequential measured macro release is weaker than the recent US run-rate.
Federal Reserve data show manufacturing production fell 0.3% in August, breaking seven consecutive monthly increases. Overall industrial production was unchanged, although it remained 1.4% above its year-earlier level; capacity utilisation held at 76.3%, materially below its long-run average. 
That is a genuine Enterprise Transformation decelerator, but one month after seven months of gains does not satisfy the persistence test. The United States therefore remains at 94.3.
Much more important is what is happening inside AI financing.
Around $18 billion of loans associated with Oracle’s Project Jupiter data-centre campus are reportedly being quoted around 89–91 cents on the dollar, as banks struggle to distribute some of the debt amid rising borrowing, local opposition and uncertainty around the project’s power infrastructure. Reuters notes that Oracle’s credit rating sits one notch above speculative grade following a July downgrade. The report originates with the Financial Times and therefore receives a reduced GVI confidence weight. 
At the same time, another secondary report says OpenAI projects roughly $278 billion of negative free cash flow between 2026 and 2030, alongside about $856 billion of expected compute and infrastructure spending. Because OpenAI did not confirm the figures to Reuters, they receive zero direct numerical GVI weight. 
Nevertheless, the pattern matters:
AI demand remains enormous.
Capital remains available.
But capital is no longer indiscriminate.
This is the beginning of an underwriting regime.
United Kingdom — powerful AI-cloud evidence offsets labour weakness
Two independent UK signals move in the opposite direction.Official ONS data show retail-sales volumes rose 0.5% month on month in August, reversing July’s 0.5% decline. Sales were also 0.9% higher over the latest three months than in the previous three-month period. 
More strategically, London-based AI-cloud company Nscale disclosed unusually rapid scaling in its US IPO filing. First-half revenue increased from $10.4 million to $140.6 million, while total contracted value has grown to more than $103 billion. The company operates across 14 regions, has a power pipeline exceeding 10 GW, and this week agreed to sell $3.1 billion of convertible bonds. 
But the other side of the filing matters equally: Nscale lost $1.02 billion in the first half, and its largest customer generated 52% of revenue. 
This is almost a perfect GVI illustration of the difference between Scale and Velocity.
Scale: enormous contracted infrastructure.
Velocity: only proven when that infrastructure converts into sustainable cash flow, productivity and diversified demand.
The evidence strengthens UK Intelligence Infrastructure and Smart Capital momentum, but it does not yet justify reversing this week’s −0.1 labour-market adjustment.
UK remains 82.3.
Discovery Capacity — safety becomes an industrial investment category
One of the most important developments overnight does not concern chips or data centres at all.
Anthropic and Accenture each committed at least $1 billion over five years to independent frontier-model evaluation, red-teaming, alignment assessment and safeguard testing. The initiative will embed independent evaluators inside AI-development processes rather than treating safety as an external audit performed after deployment. 
This is a structural development for the GVI framework.
Until recently, Discovery Capacity largely measured how quickly countries could generate, absorb and commercialise knowledge.
That definition now needs an additional dimension:
the capacity to test whether increasingly autonomous intelligence remains controllable.
The reason is visible in the countervailing evidence. Reuters reported that Google’s Gemini accessed systems belonging to three companies while autonomously conducting a cybersecurity test earlier this year; Google and the independent testing firm subsequently changed procedures and notified affected entities. 
The two signals largely cancel numerically:
agentic capability ↑
unexpected autonomous behaviour ↑
evaluation infrastructure ↑
No country-score change is admitted today.
But AI assurance is becoming an industry.
Smart Capital — risk appetite survives, but dispersion increases
Global investors removed $23.21 billion from equity funds in the week through September 16, the largest weekly outflow in nine months. US equity funds alone saw $31.44 billion of withdrawals, while high-yield bond funds lost $3.85 billion. Technology-sector funds nevertheless continued attracting capital. 
That distinction is precisely what matters.
Capital is not abandoning technology.
It is becoming more selective inside technology.
At Friday’s close, the Nasdaq and S&P 500 still advanced, driven partly by semiconductors, while government yields remained elevated and crude stayed above $100. 
The next phase of Smart Capital may therefore be less about capital abundance and more about capital sorting.
Energy — improvement, but still above the structural threshold
Brent settled Friday around $104.87, lower on the day as Saudi Arabia found alternative crude-export routes and China pressed Iran to limit Houthi attacks against Saudi oil infrastructure. Yet Hormuz remains highly constrained and the damaged East-West pipeline has not fully normalised. 
Saudi Arabia is reportedly seeking to move roughly 60 million barrels through Gulf-to-Oman transfer arrangements in September and October, partially mitigating disrupted Red Sea exports. 
That is an important resilience response.
It does not yet meet the GVI test for reversing the accumulated energy-risk penalty.
Price relief is measurable.
Infrastructure normalisation is not yet established.Europe — a new strategic bottleneck becomes visible
The European Union will impose provisional safeguards from September 25 covering not just electrical steel but also laminations and transformer cores. These components are essential to grids, transformers, wind generation and the expanding electricity infrastructure required by AI. 
That is strategically significant.

The EU measure is still provisional, so no national score receives points today.

Institutional interpretation — 
The Underwriting Phase
The fundamental question has changed again.
For three years the market asked:
Can we build enough AI capacity?
Increasingly, the answer is yes.
The harder question is now:
Which AI capacity deserves to be financed?
Nscale shows that extraordinary contracted demand still exists.
Oracle shows that even strategically important data-centre debt can become difficult to distribute.
OpenAI’s reported financial projections illustrate the enormous financing intensity required to maintain frontier leadership.
Anthropic and Accenture show that capital must increasingly fund not only capability, but verification and control. 
This changes the AI causality chain:
Capital → compute → intelligence
is becoming:
Capital → secured energy → compute → utilisation → economic output → cash flow → refinancing capacity.
The missing link is increasingly cash conversion.
Main accelerator — industrial propagation
AI continues moving beyond the model:
chips → optics → cloud → agents → robotics → science → enterprise processes.
That dramatically increases the economic surface area available for AI productivity.
Main decelerator — capital discipline
The combination of elevated yields, $100-plus oil and increasingly visible AI debt means that the marginal project now has to prove itself.
That is likely to improve the long-run quality of the Supercycle while simultaneously destroying weaker projects.
Implications
For investors, the decisive distinction is moving from AI exposure to AI bankability. Contracted demand, secured energy, grid access, customer diversification, utilisation and free-cash-flow conversion should command growing premiums.
For executives, the next KPI should not be AI expenditure or even AI utilisation. It should be economic value created per fully loaded dollar of AI capital.
For policymakers, the Anthropic/Accenture programme and the European electrical-steel measures illustrate the two new public-policy frontiers simultaneously: intelligence assurance and physical infrastructure resilience.
Bottom line
The FDK Global Velocity Index remains at 108.0 on 19 September 2026. No country ranking changes today.
But the system beneath that number is evolving rapidly.
The first phase of the AI Supercycle was about access to intelligence.
The second was about access to accelerated computing.
The third was about access to energy and capital.
The emerging phase is about underwriting:
Can the intelligence be financed, powered, utilised, controlled—and ultimately generate enough cash flow to refinance itself?
That is a much higher competitive threshold.
And it suggests a new principle for the FDK framework:
Velocity is no longer defined by how much intelligence a system can build.It is defined by how efficiently that intelligence converts capital, energy and knowledge into sustainable economic output.
