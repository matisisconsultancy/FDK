A Franco-German Alliance, a £20 Billion British Infrastructure Partnership, and Germany’s Energy Investment Decisions Reveal the Next Phase of Europe’s AI Supercycle
THE BIG TAKE — Four consequential developments on November 3 suggest that Europe’s AI infrastructure competition is entering a new phase: building integrated industrial platforms capable of turning electricity, computing equipment and private capital into commercially sustainable AI services.
The most strategically significant announcement comes from France and Germany.
Bull and Schwarz Digits have established a new Franco-German alliance to develop sovereign AI infrastructure, combining European supercomputer manufacturing with the STACKIT cloud platform.
In Britain, Brookfield has joined Drax and Radiant to develop a potentially £20 billion AI infrastructure complex at an existing power station.
Germany has awarded contracts supporting 5.16 GW of additional dispatchable electricity capacity.
Meanwhile, a new Bitkom study estimates German data-center investment will reach €23.5 billion in 2026, while warning that electricity constraints could limit further expansion.
The emerging distinction is between countries that attract AI infrastructure investment and industrial ecosystems that retain the value generated by that investment.

1 | FRANCE–GERMANY — BULL AND SCHWARZ DIGITS FORM A SOVEREIGN AI ALLIANCE
November 3 — New strategic partnership confirmed.
French supercomputer manufacturer Bull and Germany’s Schwarz Digits announced a memorandum of understanding to develop an integrated European AI and sovereign-cloud ecosystem.
The proposed architecture combines:
Bull’s European manufacturing capabilities, including its Angers facility.
Schwarz Digits’ STACKIT sovereign-cloud infrastructure.
BullSequana AI software and computing services.
Joint development of servers using Open Compute Project standards.
Potential integration with SAP RISE and advanced enterprise computing.
The partners intend to provide infrastructure and services governed under European jurisdiction, particularly for regulated industries and public institutions.
No binding infrastructure investment amount, new Gigafactory site, financing close or EuroHPC consortium membership was disclosed. 
Why this matters
This is potentially more consequential than another standalone data-center announcement.
Europe’s AI sovereignty problem is not simply insufficient computing capacity.
It is the fragmentation of its industrial capabilities across hardware manufacturing, cloud services, enterprise software, infrastructure ownership and customer demand.
The Bull–Schwarz initiative seeks to integrate several of these capabilities.
EUROPEAN HARDWARE → SOVEREIGN CLOUD → ENTERPRISE APPLICATIONS → RECURRING AI SERVICES
The second-order implications are significant.
First, European computing equipment manufacturers could gain access to a larger commercial distribution platform.
Second, Schwarz Digits could strengthen its ability to serve regulated industries seeking alternatives to non-European cloud infrastructure.
Third, integration with enterprise software could support recurring commercial demand.
For investors, the principal question is whether the alliance will move from technological cooperation to binding hardware orders, customer contracts and committed capital expenditure.
For CEOs and boards, the strategic opportunity is greater control over sensitive data, computing infrastructure and business-critical applications.
For European policymakers, this represents a promising industrial model: strengthening sovereignty through commercially integrated capabilities rather than infrastructure subsidies alone.
Strategic verdict: A meaningful Franco-German industrial alliance, but not yet a committed Gigafactory investment.

2 | UNITED KINGDOM — BROOKFIELD ENTERS A POTENTIAL £20 BILLION AI INFRASTRUCTURE DEVELOPMENT
November 3 — New strategic development agreement.Drax, Brookfield and Radiant announced a partnership to develop AI data-center infrastructure at Drax Power Station in North Yorkshire.
Brookfield’s investment involvement will be structured through its existing AI infrastructure joint venture with Qatar’s Qai.
The proposed development could exceed £20 billion in total investment, including computing equipment.
The initial phase could involve approximately £2.5 billion.
Development stage Capacity ambition Target
Phase I Up to 100 MW First quarter 2028
Phase II Approximately 500 MW additional capacity 2028–2031
Phase III Further expansion toward more than 1 GW overall After 2031
The proposed campus would benefit from the power station’s existing 4 GW grid connection and 2.6 GW of dispatchable generation capacity.
However, the first phase is expected to draw electricity from the grid.
Construction remains conditional on planning and regulatory approvals, customer commitments, commercial agreements and final investment decisions.
Neither £20 billion nor £2.5 billion should be interpreted as fully committed construction expenditure or completed financing. 
The emerging industrial architecture
The partnership illustrates a potentially powerful model for AI capital formation.
ENERGY ASSETS + GRID INFRASTRUCTURE + INSTITUTIONAL CAPITAL + COMPUTE OPERATIONS
Drax contributes industrial land, electricity infrastructure and energy expertise.
Brookfield contributes infrastructure investment capabilities and access to financing.
Radiant contributes computing-infrastructure development and commercialization capabilities.
The economic significance is substantial.
Existing power-generation sites may become increasingly attractive locations for AI infrastructure because of established grid connections, industrial land and electricity expertise.
This could accelerate a broader process of industrial asset conversion across Europe.
But the model involves material uncertainty concerning electricity costs, environmental compliance, planning, future technology requirements and customer demand. Drax’s biomass operations also face sustainability scrutiny.
The second-order consequence
European energy companies could increasingly become strategic partners in AI infrastructure rather than merely suppliers of electricity.
For investors, this creates potential opportunities across utilities, infrastructure funds, power-generation assets and data-center platforms.
For policymakers, it raises an important question: whether Europe can redevelop existing industrial energy assets into computing infrastructure without undermining electricity affordability or decarbonization.
Strategic verdict: A major new partnership with institutional capital backing, but substantial development and financial-close conditions remain outstanding.

3 | GERMANY — 5.16 GW OF ELECTRICITY CAPACITY RECEIVES CONTRACT AWARDS
November 3 — Confirmed national energy procurement decision.
Germany’s Federal Network Agency awarded contracts covering approximately 5.16 GW of dispatchable electricity capacity, primarily involving new or converted gas-fired power stations.
The awards form part of Germany’s broader strategy to maintain electricity reliability as coal generation declines.
The principal recipients are:
Company Awarded capacity
LEAG and subsidiaries Approximately 3 GW
RWE Approximately 2.12 GW
Other successful bidders Remaining capacity
RWE separately confirmed awards for three hydrogen-ready projects in Voerde, Werne and Weisweiler.
The procurement creates an estimated €12 billion support framework over the relevant contract period.
This is not €12 billion of AI-specific investment or a confirmed equivalent amount of immediate plant-construction spending.
The facilities are intended to provide reliable capacity by 2031, supported through long-term availability payments funded by electricity consumers. 
Why this matters for AI Gigafactories
Germany is confronting two infrastructure transformations simultaneously.
Its electricity system is becoming increasingly dependent on variable renewable generation.Its computing infrastructure is becoming increasingly electricity-intensive.
These developments require a coordinated investment strategy.
AI DEMAND GROWTH → GREATER RELIABILITY REQUIREMENTS → GENERATION AND GRID INVESTMENT → ENERGY-SYSTEM COSTS → COMPUTE ECONOMICS
The new awards could strengthen long-term electricity-system reliability.
They do not establish dedicated power availability for individual Gigafactories, nor do they resolve near-term grid-connection constraints.
The timing is also important.
AI infrastructure projects targeting commercial operation in 2028 cannot assume that additional capacity expected by 2031 will solve their immediate electricity requirements.
Strategic verdict: A consequential energy-system funding decision, with indirect but potentially important implications for German Industrial AI competitiveness.

4 | GERMANY — €23.5 BILLION IN DATA-CENTER INVESTMENT EXPOSES EUROPE’S SCALE DEFICIT
November 3 — New Bitkom study published.
German digital-industry association Bitkom estimates that data-center investment will reach a record €23.5 billion in 2026, an increase of approximately 57% over 2025.
Of this total, approximately €18 billion relates to IT hardware and €5.5 billion to buildings and technical infrastructure.
The association expects Germany’s AI and high-performance computing capacity to expand significantly.
Indicator 2026 2035 forecast
Total data-center IT connection capacity 3.3 GW 9.7 GW
AI/HPC capacity 670 MW 5.3 GW
AI/HPC share 20% 55%
Annual electricity demand* 23 TWh 56–70 TWh
Electricity-demand figures include data centers and smaller IT installations.
These are industry estimates and forecasts, not announced investments in a single project or binding Gigafactory financing commitments. 
The fundamental competitive question
Germany is already Europe’s largest data-center market.
But its industrial ambitions face a substantial scale challenge.
Bitkom projects that European data-center capacity could reach approximately 37 GW by 2035, against 160 GW in the United States and 90 GW in China.
These projections are uncertain, but the relative magnitudes are revealing.
Europe could expand computing infrastructure rapidly and still lose relative competitive ground.
This creates a strategic imperative.
Europe cannot rely exclusively on matching American investment volumes.
It needs greater economic productivity from its computing infrastructure.
Germany has a potential advantage in Industrial AI, where advanced manufacturing, robotics, automotive engineering, chemicals and industrial software create specialized demand.
The critical investment question is whether increased computing capacity will translate into European productivity growth, higher industrial margins and durable returns.
Strategic verdict: Germany’s capital formation is accelerating, but translating infrastructure investment into industrial competitive advantage remains the decisive test.

EUROHPC — FORMAL PROCUREMENT STATUS

THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS — THREE FINANCIAL DYNAMICS TO WATCH
1 | From infrastructure ownership to vertical integration
The Bull–Schwarz alliance illustrates the potential integration of computing hardware, cloud infrastructure and enterprise services.
This could improve the ability of European providers to retain a larger share of AI expenditure.
The relevant financial measure will be recurring revenue generated per euro of computing infrastructure investment.
2 | From energy utilities to AI infrastructure investment platforms
The Brookfield–Drax agreement illustrates how institutional capital could combine with existing energy assets to create new computing infrastructure.
For European utilities, this may open additional opportunities to monetize grid connections and industrial sites.
But converting physical infrastructure into AI capacity also transfers new technological, commercial and regulatory risks onto investment structures.
3 | From public subsidies to measurable industrial returns
Germany’s latest electricity awards and Bitkom’s investment estimates expose the growing interaction between energy policy, digital infrastructure and industrial competitiveness.
Europe’s public investment challenge is no longer simply encouraging capacity expansion.
It is ensuring that infrastructure investment produces measurable productivity gains.

FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS, CEOs AND POLICYMAKERS
1 | INVESTORS — Vertical integration deserves increasing attention.
The most attractive AI infrastructure propositions may combine manufacturing capabilities, cloud services, contracted customer demand and reliable power access. Announced investment size alone is an inadequate valuation metric.
2 | EUROPEAN BANKS — Separate development capital from operational computing risk.
Grid infrastructure, data-center buildings, advanced hardware and AI services have different economic lives, depreciation profiles and cash-flow characteristics. Financing structures should reflect those distinctions.
3 | CEOs AND BOARDS — Sovereign computing is becoming an industrial procurement decision.
For regulated European companies, cloud jurisdiction, infrastructure control, interoperability and service reliability may increasingly influence technology investment decisions.
4 | INDUSTRIAL AI — Computing investment must translate into productivity.
Germany’s industrial ecosystem demonstrates an opportunity to differentiate European AI infrastructure through specialized manufacturing applications rather than relying solely on general-purpose model development.
5 | POLICYMAKERS — Europe’s objective must be capital productivity, not simply capital mobilization.
A successful Gigafactory programme should help retain industrial value in Europe through manufacturing, software, infrastructure services and productivity-enhancing applications.

THE BIG TAKE — THE EUROPEAN AI CAPITAL FORMATION CHALLENGE
November 3 reveals an increasingly important transformation.
Europe’s AI infrastructure investment model is evolving beyond isolated data-center projects.
A Franco-German industrial alliance is seeking to integrate hardware manufacturing and sovereign cloud services.
A major institutional investor is partnering with an energy producer to explore a gigawatt-scale computing development.Germany is allocating financial support to electricity-system reliability while private investment in computing infrastructure accelerates.
The developments are different, but their underlying economic direction is increasingly similar.
Europe is beginning to connect the institutions that control energy, industrial technology, capital and computing demand.
The fundamental challenge is converting those connections into profitable, operational infrastructure.
For investors, the next decisive evidence will be binding customer contracts, financial closes, enforceable power arrangements and operational milestones.
For European industrial policy, the strategic ambition must go further.
Europe cannot win the AI Supercycle merely by financing more data centers. It must capture a larger share of the industrial value created by the intelligence those facilities produce.
VELOCITY INSIGHT
The next European AI champions will not simply own computing capacity. They will control the industrial ecosystems that convert computing capacity into productivity, competitive advantage and sustainable returns.
