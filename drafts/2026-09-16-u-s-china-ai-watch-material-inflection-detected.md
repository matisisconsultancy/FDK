The United States is beginning to contest China on the diffusion layer itself. The strategic unit of competition is shifting from the model to the entire frontier-to-inference system.

The consequential development on September 16, 2026 is not a new DeepSeek release. It is the combination of two American moves occurring at opposite ends of the AI infrastructure chain: Washington is trying to tighten the physical supply-chain perimeter around AI hardware, while Anthropic is simultaneously building one of the world’s largest dedicated inference footprints outside the United States.

Together, they materially refine our Velocity Edge thesis. Until now, the clean formulation has been that America dominates frontier creation while China increasingly dominates the economics and diffusion of intelligence. Today suggests that the United States may no longer be willing to concede that second battlefield.

1. The U.S. is trying to turn North America into an AI-hardware bloc

The Wall Street Journal reports today that Washington is pressing Mexico to accept tougher rules of origin for AI chips, servers and related hardware as part of the USMCA negotiations, specifically to prevent Chinese or other non-North-American components from entering the U.S. market through Mexican assembly operations. The proposal is still under negotiation and has not been adopted, but its scope is strategically important. 

The numbers explain why.

Mexico exported approximately $82.9 billion of computer servers during the first half of 2026, up 172.1% year over year, according to S&P Global Market Intelligence. Roughly 93.9% went to the United States. AI servers have become important enough to challenge automobiles as Mexico’s defining export category. 

Only a decade ago, China dominated much of this equipment supply chain. By the first half of 2026, China’s share of U.S. computer-equipment imports had fallen dramatically while Taiwan and Mexico captured most of the market. 

Washington is therefore moving beyond:

GPU export controls.

The perimeter increasingly becomes:

semiconductors → HBM → servers → system assembly → rules of origin → regional AI infrastructure.

That is a significant policy evolution.

America appears to be trying to create something analogous to the automotive regional-value architecture—but for the AI production system.

The strategic logic is powerful:

U.S. frontier models
→ U.S./allied chips
→ Taiwanese manufacturing
→ North American server assembly
→ U.S. AI factories
→ controlled supply chain
→ greater infrastructure resilience.

If implemented, this would make it harder for Chinese hardware manufacturers to use third-country production as a route into the enormous North American AI-infrastructure market.

2. Anthropic makes an even more important move: 2.16 GW dedicated largely to inference

At the same time, Reuters reports today that Anthropic has signed its first Australian data-centre agreement, securing capacity at a planned 2.16-gigawatt campus roughly 250 km from Brisbane. The facility is expected to begin coming online in 2027 and remains subject to Australian regulatory approval. 

The striking point is not merely the size.

It is the workload.

The facility is intended primarily for inference rather than frontier-model training. 

That distinction matters enormously for our framework.

A large share of the first phase of the AI Supercycle was driven by:

training capacity.

The next phase increasingly appears to be:

INFERENCE CAPACITY.

Training creates intelligence once.

Inference monetizes it billions or trillions of times.

And 2.16 GW devoted primarily to inference means frontier-model companies are beginning to build physical infrastructure around the expectation of enormous recurring consumption of intelligence.

Australia also illustrates why geography is changing. The country offers abundant renewable-energy potential, available land and proximity to Asian markets at a moment when U.S. and Asian power grids are increasingly constrained.Reuters notes that Australian AI infrastructure investment could reach around A$150 billion, or roughly US$107 billion, by 2030. 

OpenAI already secured Australian capacity last year. Anthropic is now following.

The American AI ecosystem is therefore moving from:

global model distribution

toward:

global physical distribution of inference capacity.

That is a much deeper competitive architecture.

This does not invalidate the thesis that China is the primary challenger on AI economics.

Chinese labs continue narrowing the capability gap while operating with substantially greater hardware constraints; AP’s assessment today similarly describes Chinese groups including DeepSeek, Moonshot, Z.ai and Alibaba as increasingly competitive with leading U.S. models even while the U.S. retains leadership across key frontier capabilities. 

But something important has changed.

America is beginning to build a distribution architecture of its own.

Not primarily through cheaper open models.

Through infrastructure.

The emerging Great Split

The competition is becoming:

China: make each unit of accelerated computing produce more intelligence.

America: make accelerated computing available everywhere economically valuable intelligence will be consumed.

China optimizes:

Intelligence / Hardware

America increasingly optimizes:

Hardware × Energy × Capital × Geographic Reach

These are different mechanisms for reducing the effective cost of AI deployment.

And they can collide directly.

⸻

Why this matters for the AI Supercycle

The Anthropic announcement is especially important because it strengthens one of our core propositions:

The AI Supercycle is migrating from training to inference.

The first capital wave built:

GPUs → training clusters → frontier models.

The next wave increasingly requires:

regional AI factories
→ sovereign inference
→ enterprise agents
→ local data residency
→ low latency
→ Physical AI
→ continuous token production.

That potentially creates a much larger infrastructure market than training alone.

Training workloads are episodic.

Inference workloads scale with economic activity.

Every additional AI employee, coding agent, autonomous vehicle, robot, financial agent, logistics system or scientific model generates persistent inference demand.

The causality becomes:

Better models
→ more economically useful tasks
→ more inference
→ larger regional AI factories
→ lower latency and lower unit cost
→ more applications
→ still more inference.

That is the Supercycle.

The competitive equation should now evolve to:

AI Power = Frontier Capability × Inference Economics × Infrastructure Scale × Supply-Chain Control × Diffusion Velocity

The United States remains strongest in:

**Frontier Capability

• Infrastructure Scale
• Supply-Chain Control.**

China remains increasingly powerful in:

**Inference Economics

• Diffusion Velocity.**

But the overlap is growing.

And that is exactly what makes the competition more consequential.

The Velocity Insight

The September 16 inflection is not that America suddenly defeated China on low-cost inference.

It has not.

The more important development is that the United States is beginning to attack the structural foundations of diffusion:

secure the hardware supply chain at home,
then place enormous inference capacity closer to users abroad.

The two developments today therefore form one coherent causality chain:SUPPLY-CHAIN CONTROL → AI FACTORIES → REGIONAL INFERENCE → LOWER DEPLOYMENT FRICTION → GREATER AI DIFFUSION

China’s response remains:

MODEL EFFICIENCY → LOWER HARDWARE REQUIREMENT → LOWER COST → MASS DEPLOYMENT → GREATER AI DIFFUSION

This gives The Velocity Edge a sharper formulation.

America is trying to industrialize the global production and distribution of inference.

China is trying to industrialize the efficiency with which that inference is produced and consumed.

The United States still leads the frontier.

China remains the most powerful challenge to the economics of that frontier.

But September 16 adds an important new dimension:

the U.S. is no longer fighting only to own the frontier—it is beginning to fight to own the infrastructure through which the frontier reaches the world.

That makes the AI Supercycle broader, more physical and considerably more capital intensive.
