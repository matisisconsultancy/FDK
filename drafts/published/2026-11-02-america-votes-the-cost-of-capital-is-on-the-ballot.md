On the eve of the US midterm elections, The Velocity Edge launches a dedicated watch on the political forces reshaping the American economy—and their consequences for global markets. The central investment question is whether the election changes the conditions under which capital earns its returns.

Our thesis: **affordability is becoming the political constraint** on America's investment boom. The election could accelerate the passage from a market that rewards announced investment to one that demands evidence of sustainable returns, credible financing and public consent. That transition connects household electricity bills to data-centre economics, congressional scrutiny to private-credit underwriting, and the Treasury market to equity valuations worldwide.

::section The electorate is testing the growth model | Aggregate versus household
Investment can expand while voters feel poorer. Technology valuations can rise while housing remains inaccessible. Data-centre construction can accelerate while communities question the effect on their electricity bills. When those experiences diverge, the political response can change project economics.

Capital markets price future growth; elections determine how much of its cost society is willing to carry. This tension could outlast whichever party wins Congress—affordability offers both parties a powerful organising principle, and exposes businesses whose returns depend on costs being absorbed elsewhere.

::section AI's political exposure is becoming financial exposure | Through the cash flows
The transmission runs through project cash flows: greater scrutiny → changed obligations or slower delivery → later revenues → higher financing needs → lower equity returns. This is a conditional mechanism, not a forecast of obstruction—but it explains why strong demand for computing does not guarantee strong returns for every owner of it.

A project delayed while interest accrues can lose value before its first customer arrives. The decisive distinction is becoming clearer: which businesses can finance the full cost of their expansion—and still generate attractive cash flows?

::pull Political access can help secure a meeting. It cannot service debt.

::section Private capital faces an accountability test | Oversight before law
Bloomberg describes preparations for increased scrutiny of private equity's involvement in housing, healthcare and data centres. The more directly those investments touch living costs, the harder it becomes to keep their economics outside political debate.

The first consequence may arrive through disclosure rather than legislation. Congressional investigations can expose contractual terms, leverage, valuation assumptions and risk allocation—and oversight can change underwriting before Congress changes the law. This establishes a differentiation process: transparent structures with resilient cash flows should withstand scrutiny better than investments dependent on optimistic refinancing.

::section Congressional control changes the route—not the existence—of risk | Three outcomes
Republicans entered with a 53–47 Senate majority; Democrats need a net gain of four seats for control. Even Democratic control of both chambers would leave Trump with veto power and substantial executive authority.

The third outcome deserves particular attention: a result welcomed by deregulation-sensitive equities could still become difficult if bond investors demand higher yields. Policy support and financial conditions can move in opposite directions. Conversely, a Democratic victory could increase scrutiny without derailing the cycle. There is **no congressional outcome** that abolishes the discipline of cash flow, interest rates and delivery.

::section A relief rally could conceal the next repricing | The global channel
The vote can clear the calendar. It cannot clear the balance sheet. The initial reaction will answer only whether the result was a surprise; corporate results and financing conditions will answer what the growth is actually worth. A broad equity rebound could coexist with pressure on exposed technology companies.

For international investors, the transmission begins in the Treasury market: fiscal negotiations affect yields, which set discount rates worldwide. Delayed American AI infrastructure would reach overseas suppliers of semiconductors, power equipment and cooling systems—a delayed order book can damage returns even when long-term demand remains intact. If shares rally while financing conditions deteriorate, the market is celebrating political resolution while the investment hurdle is rising.

::section The economic contest of 2028 starts here | Distribution of gains
The most durable pressure will concern the distribution of technological gains. Can AI produce visible productivity benefits beyond the companies building it? Can infrastructure expand without undermining household affordability? Can private investment demonstrate that its returns reflect value created rather than costs transferred? The political durability of American growth will increasingly depend on whether households can recognise themselves among its beneficiaries.

::takeaways Five decisions for investors and boards | What to act on now
- Underwrite projects at their full economic cost—test who pays for power, grid connections, delays and refinancing.
- Separate demand growth from shareholder returns; capacity expansion alone does not establish value creation.
- Treat oversight as a financial variable, since greater disclosure can change counterparties' willingness to provide capital.
- Read bonds and credit alongside equities—a relief rally is less reassuring when the cost of funding rises.
- Prepare for several stages of resolution; close counts may extend beyond election night, and delays alone do not establish wrongdoing.

::close The decisive question after November 3 will be ==which companies can preserve their returns when political permission becomes harder to secure and capital becomes less forgiving==.
