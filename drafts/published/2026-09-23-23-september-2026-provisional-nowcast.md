The important change today is beneath the headline. The **Weighted Global Velocity Index** holds at 108.0 (2025=100), with a provisional unrounded daily movement of **+0.03** that rounds to unchanged. No country-level score changes clear the full GVI admission threshold, and no rankings move.

But the underlying system improves. Brent has now closed below $100 for the first time since September 8 and is trading around ==$99.18== this morning. More importantly, Saudi Arabia has restarted the East-West pipeline, restoring a physical bypass of the Strait of Hormuz — the first combination of price relief *plus* infrastructure restoration strong enough to warrant a partial reversal of the recent Smart Capital penalty.

At the same time, Alibaba has unveiled a new AI accelerator, a roadmap toward models of as much as 5–10 trillion parameters, and a target of more than 20 GW of global data-centre capacity by 2032. The dominant signal today is a shift from AI scale to ==AI system control==: silicon, memory, data, energy, infrastructure and capital converging into one competitive stack.

::signals Today's Signals | four movements beneath a flat headline
# China — the full-stack strategy becomes explicit
Alibaba's Apsara announcements are the most important technology signal overnight. The company unveiled its **Zhenwu V900** AI processor, which it says delivers three times the performance of its predecessor and can participate in clusters of up to 500,000 accelerators, with commercial mass production scheduled for Q1 2027. Earlier-generation Zhenwu chips are already deployed across hundreds of external customers spanning autos, finance, energy, manufacturing and embodied intelligence.

China's competitive architecture is becoming increasingly symmetrical with the US hyperscaler model: models → proprietary chips → supernodes → cloud → agents → enterprise distribution. But the new chip is not yet in mass production and the 20-GW target is future capacity, so the evidence raises momentum, not today's structural score. China remains **91.1**.
signal: Full-stack momentum accelerating, but the score waits for production.

# United States — enterprise AI monetises the verification layer
Palo Alto Networks launched a subscription cybersecurity product using frontier models from OpenAI and Anthropic to continuously test corporate applications, APIs and cloud infrastructure. Separately, Snorkel AI disclosed an annualised revenue run-rate above **$350 million** — up from roughly $20 million a year earlier — raised $350 million at a $3.5 billion valuation, and expects profitability this year.

The signal is clear: frontier models → specialised data → continuous evaluation → cybersecurity → recurring enterprise revenue. This improves Enterprise Transformation and Discovery Capacity momentum, but the evidence remains company-specific and does not clear the threshold for a US score revision. The United States remains **94.3**.
signal: Value migrates from raw model capability toward verification and recurring cash flow.

# Energy — the first genuine reversal of the September shock
Saudi Arabia restarted the East-West pipeline on September 22, having shut it since September 11 after drone attacks damaged three pumping stations. The route — which can bypass Hormuz and has recently carried roughly 4 million barrels per day, about 4% of global supply — is operating at reduced rates, with full capacity potentially six to eight weeks away.

This finally changes the classification from *price relief only* to *price relief plus partial infrastructure restoration*, admitting a positive Smart Capital contribution today. But the penalty is not fully reversed: the pipeline remains below normal capacity, Hormuz risks stay material and monetary policy is still restrictive.
signal: Real, measured relief — but incomplete.

# South Korea — resilience becomes industrial policy
Seoul announced a ten-year resource-security strategy to cut dependence on Middle Eastern crude from roughly 70% in 2025 to 50% by 2035, expand crude stockpiles by about 20 million barrels by 2030, and broaden its critical-minerals list from 38 to 51 materials — including germanium, a key semiconductor input.

This is precisely the policy architecture that matters in an increasingly physical AI economy: semiconductors + energy security + critical minerals + stockpiles = industrial resilience. The plan is official but its targets remain prospective, so no points are added today. South Korea remains **88.4**, with stronger resilience momentum.
signal: Semiconductor leadership increasingly depends on energy and minerals.

::section Smart Capital — relief on the long end, pressure at the short end | an asymmetric financial signal
The US 10-year Treasury yield finished Tuesday around **4.953%**, below the 5% threshold, while lower oil prices supported technology assets and helped the Nasdaq reach another record.

But the monetary constraint has not disappeared. Two-year Treasury yields reached about **4.79%**, their highest since mid-2024, and futures markets implied roughly a **54%** probability of another Fed increase in October. Energy risk is easing and long-duration yield pressure is easing modestly, but policy-rate risk remains elevated — and that prevents a larger GVI upgrade.

::section Institutional Interpretation — The Full-Stack Race | integration accelerates, financing decelerates
The first AI race was about the model. The second was about the GPU. The third became about energy and capital. The emerging race is about controlling the entire system: Silicon → Memory → Networking → Models → Data → Agents → Power → Capital → Distribution.

Velocity rises when complementary layers reinforce each other. Owning a powerful model is valuable; owning the model plus compute, memory, data, power, distribution and financing is much more valuable, because feedback loops become faster and dependency risk falls.

The main decelerator is that financing still costs too much. The oil shock is easing, but central banks have not followed it downward — two-year US yields remain close to 4.8%, and another Fed increase is still meaningfully priced. The hurdle: AI productivity must compound faster than the combined cost of capital, energy and infrastructure.

For investors, the opportunity is moving from generic "AI exposure" toward companies that control scarce bottlenecks or convert them into recurring cash flow — memory, networking, power equipment, specialised data, cybersecurity and highly utilised compute. For executives, the question is less "Which AI model do we use?" and more "Which layers of the stack must we control?" For policymakers, the Korean response is instructive: competitiveness must integrate energy security, critical minerals, grid capacity, capital markets and supply-chain redundancy.
