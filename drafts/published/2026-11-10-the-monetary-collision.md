November 10 marks a consequential development in the monetary-policy response to the AI Supercycle. Within 24 hours, new Federal Reserve research, a speech by Governor Christopher Waller, a hawkish intervention by ECB chief economist Philip Lane and fresh Bank of Japan deliberations have strengthened the case that the global economy is entering a more complicated monetary regime.

AI capital formation continues to support growth, but its inflationary consequences are becoming more visible while productivity gains remain uneven. Central banks may be required to maintain tighter conditions precisely when the Supercycle demands unprecedented productive investment. This is the monetary collision at the heart of the Great Split.

::section The Federal Reserve — AI Inflation Enters the Reaction Function
Speaking in London, Governor Waller said he would support a December rate increase even if October inflation moderates, and indicated a 50-basis-point increase should be considered if underlying inflation remains particularly strong. He identified rising prices for AI-infrastructure technology equipment as an increasingly important component of the inflation outlook, and cited new Fed research suggesting energy shocks may be more persistent than previously assumed.

This is not yet a formal change in the reaction function, but policymakers are incorporating the AI investment cycle into their assessment of inflation persistence. Waller also warned that a low-hiring, low-layoff labour market may conceal weaknesses in worker mobility — AI could improve productivity within leading firms without improving broader employment.

::section The New Fed Research — Energy Inflation May No Longer Be Temporary
A study of five advanced economies — the US, euro area, UK, Japan and Canada — finds oil-price increases have transmitted more strongly into underlying inflation since the pandemic. Before 2020, a permanent 10% oil increase raised the core price level by about 0.05% after three years; under post-pandemic dynamics that effect rises to roughly 0.35%, with substantially higher persistence. In that scenario, advanced-economy core inflation could approach 2.8% around mid-2027, about 0.6 points above the pre-pandemic trajectory.

The authors caution that the findings are conditional and not official forecasts. Yet AI infrastructure is increasing electricity demand and competition for energy-intensive inputs — if energy shocks prove more persistent, authorities have less freedom to accommodate investment-related price pressures.

::pull The investment required to create future productive capacity may face restrictive monetary conditions before its productivity benefits become visible.

::section ECB and Bank of England — Tightening Meets Employment
Philip Lane indicated a December increase would be prudent if conditions remain broadly unchanged, warning the inflation peak still lies ahead. For a euro area financing defence, digital infrastructure, energy security and industrial AI, a higher cost of capital raises hurdles everywhere. The ECB must distinguish investment that creates future capacity from demand that merely adds to inflation — higher capital expenditure is not equivalent to higher productivity.

Following last week's increase in Bank Rate to 4%, BoE Deputy Governor Clare Lombardelli did not exclude another December move. She described a low-hiring, low-firing labour market in which recruitment weakens — particularly for younger workers — while dismissals stay limited. Employment stability is not dynamism: productivity gains may coexist with deteriorating opportunities for the young, weakening household-income growth even as output per worker improves.

::section Bank of Japan — AI Valuations Become a Policy Variable
The BOJ's summary of its October 29–30 meeting recognises that AI-related demand — semiconductors, production equipment, data centres — has supported activity and prices, and explicitly identifies asset prices in its assessment of financial conditions. The recent correction in AI equities therefore complicates matters: a market decline can tighten financial conditions independently of policy. The next meeting is scheduled for December 17–18. Central banks increasingly must distinguish tightening delivered through rates from tightening generated by market corrections.

::section The Next Monetary Inflection
Three forces now conflict. The investment effect: AI capital formation supports growth while raising demand for equipment, energy and financing. The productivity effect: deployment can raise output and cut unit costs, but timing and distribution remain uncertain. The financial effect: a valuation correction can tighten credit before central banks move rates. No new AI-adjusted neutral-rate estimate was established today — but policymakers are increasingly examining how AI investment, valuations and productivity affect monetary transmission.

::takeaways The Final Take | The next great capital test
- The monetary environment remains demanding: further tightening is being considered across several major economies despite doubts about the AI investment boom.
- Industrial AI deflation remains a prospective benefit, not an established offset — distinguish the capital deployed from the cash flows it produces.
- Europe must accelerate productive investment while protecting fiscal credibility, managing energy constraints and preserving financial stability.
- For investors, the decisive variables are capital efficiency, refinancing exposure, pricing power and realised productivity.

::close
