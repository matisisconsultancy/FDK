Three consequential developments are redefining the central-bank response to the AI Supercycle. Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh has explicitly distinguished the inflationary impact of today's AI investment boom from its future productivity dividend. The G7 has agreed to conduct coordinated simulations of AI-enabled cyberattacks on financial institutions. Meanwhile, the IMF's latest European outlook reinforces the widening divergence between economies capturing AI-driven growth and those absorbing its energy and financing costs.

The Great Split is becoming a three-dimensional monetary regime: divergence in productivity, divergence in capital formation and divergence in financial resilience.

The critical implication is that central banks must manage inflationary demand and systemic risks before the productivity benefits of Industrial AI become fully measurable.

::section The Fed: The AI Demand Shock Precedes the Productivity Dividend | Signal 1

The most important monetary-policy intervention today came from Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh, speaking at the IMF–World Bank Annual Meetings in Bangkok.

Warsh identified the extraordinary investment surge associated with artificial intelligence as a contributor to current inflationary pressures.

But his distinction between the timing of AI's demand and supply effects is particularly consequential.

AI is stimulating investment and aggregate demand now. Its contribution to productive capacity, efficiency and sustainable economic growth will develop over a longer horizon.

Warsh described the transition as potentially disruptive, particularly for employment, while remaining optimistic about AI's eventual productivity benefits.

He did not signal a specific interest-rate decision for the Federal Reserve's October or December meetings.

**The monetary transmission mechanism.** The sequence can be expressed as:

AI CAPITAL FORMATION → STRONGER DEMAND → RESOURCE SCARCITY → INFLATION PRESSURE

followed, potentially, by:

INDUSTRIAL AI ADOPTION → HIGHER PRODUCTIVITY → LOWER UNIT COSTS → DISINFLATION.

The distinction is not merely academic. The first sequence can justify restrictive monetary conditions. The second could eventually support lower inflation without sacrificing economic growth. But there is no guarantee that the second will arrive quickly enough to offset the first.

This is the central timing problem of the AI Supercycle.

Warsh's announcement that the Federal Reserve will hold a public hearing on November 16 to examine AI's impact on jobs and productivity is therefore significant. It indicates that the Fed is intensifying its examination of the relationship between technological adoption, potential output and employment.

No formal revision to the neutral interest rate was announced. Nevertheless, the increasing importance of extraordinary investment demand reinforces the need to distinguish a potentially higher equilibrium real rate from temporary increases in inflation and term premiums.

**The second-order effect.** The next monetary divergence could emerge between economies where AI investment is broadening into productive industrial activity and economies where investment remains concentrated in infrastructure.

In the first case, the productivity dividend may begin supporting sustainable real-income growth. In the second, capital expenditure can continue increasing financing costs before generating sufficient economic returns. The determining variable is increasingly the conversion rate of AI capital expenditure into productive output.

::section The G7: AI Financial Risk Moves From Guidelines to Stress Testing | Signal 2

The second consequential development is institutional.

On October 16, Japanese Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama confirmed that G7 finance ministers and central-bank governors had agreed to organise coordinated exercises simulating AI-enabled cyberattacks. The initiative was approved during meetings in Bangkok.

These exercises will examine the effectiveness of existing emergency-response arrangements and identify necessary improvements. Private financial institutions may participate, depending on the scenarios selected.

This represents a concrete operational step beyond the supervisory guidance published by the Financial Stability Board on October 15.

**The emerging systemic risk.** AI-enabled cyberattacks can potentially operate at greater speed and scale than conventional attacks. At the same time, financial institutions are becoming more dependent on increasingly interconnected digital infrastructures.

The resulting transmission mechanism could become:

AI CYBERATTACK → CRITICAL-SERVICE DISRUPTION → LOSS OF CONFIDENCE → LIQUIDITY MOVEMENTS → FINANCIAL CONTAGION.

This remains a potential scenario, not evidence that such a systemic event has occurred. Its importance lies in the speed at which operational disruption could develop into a financial-stability problem.

The G7 decision therefore introduces an important supervisory principle: financial resilience must be tested against the speed of autonomous technological disruption.

**The second-order effect.** The implications extend to capital allocation. Financial institutions may need to invest more heavily in independent recovery infrastructure, operational redundancy, cybersecurity and controls over autonomous financial agents.

These investments could increase near-term operating costs while reducing exposure to potentially catastrophic disruption. They could also create new competitive advantages for technology providers capable of delivering secure, auditable and resilient systems.

For investors, operational resilience is becoming part of the assessment of sustainable financial returns. For central banks, financial supervision increasingly requires coordination across jurisdictions because technological dependencies and cyber threats do not respect national boundaries.

::section Europe: The Cost of the AI Supercycle Without Its Full Dividend | Signal 3

The IMF's latest European assessment reinforces the structural asymmetry confronting the ECB.

Euro-area growth is projected at just 1.1% in 2026, while average inflation is expected to reach 3.1%. The IMF anticipates that inflation will remain elevated during 2027, with convergence toward the ECB's target delayed until 2028.

The immediate inflationary pressures primarily reflect energy and geopolitical shocks, not AI alone. But the IMF identifies Europe's weak productivity growth, fragmented capital markets and difficulty scaling innovative businesses as structural constraints on its economic potential.

Spain remains an important exception among the major European economies, with projected growth of 2.5% in 2026.

**The European monetary paradox.** The contrast with the United States is becoming more consequential. American AI investment is supporting aggregate demand, business investment and expectations of future productivity gains. Europe is experiencing higher energy costs and tighter financing conditions while capturing a smaller share of global AI infrastructure investment.

The resulting asymmetry can be expressed as:

GLOBAL AI CAPITAL FORMATION → HIGHER DEMAND FOR CAPITAL AND ENERGY → EUROPEAN FINANCING PRESSURE

while:

LIMITED EUROPEAN AI CAPITAL FORMATION → SLOWER PRODUCTIVITY DIFFUSION → WEAKER POTENTIAL GROWTH.

The ECB cannot resolve this imbalance through interest-rate decisions alone. Indeed, higher global bond yields may already tighten European financial conditions independently of changes in ECB policy rates. That complicates the interpretation of monetary restrictiveness. A stable policy rate does not necessarily imply stable financing conditions.

**China introduces a further competitive dimension.** A separate October 16 development reinforces the international capital-market implications. People's Bank of China Governor Pan Gongsheng has pledged to expand foreign access to Chinese financial markets, improve cross-border financial infrastructure and promote wider international acceptance of yuan-denominated bonds as collateral.

Pan explicitly connected China's financial-market ambitions with technological advances, including AI. These are policy commitments rather than completed changes to the international monetary system. Nevertheless, they illustrate how technological competition and financial-market development are becoming increasingly interconnected.

The second-order implication is important. The AI Supercycle is generating competition not only for computing capacity, energy and capital, but increasingly for the financial infrastructure through which global capital is mobilised. For Europe, that makes the development of deeper capital markets and stronger cross-border financial integration a strategic necessity.

::section The Next Rotation: Five Takeaways for Investors and EU Policymakers | Signal 4

The latest developments strengthen five conclusions.

**1. Monetary policy — Watch the transition between demand and supply.** The Fed's emerging analytical distinction is fundamental. AI investment is affecting demand and inflation before its productivity effects become fully visible. Investors should distinguish temporary disinflation caused by weakening investment from sustainable disinflation generated by higher productive capacity.

**2. Capital formation — Returns matter more than expenditure.** The AI infrastructure boom is raising demand for financing and scarce productive resources. The next market rotation is likely to differentiate companies that generate measurable productivity and free-cash-flow improvements from those whose investment requirements continue increasing faster than their economic returns.

**3. Financial stability — Operational resilience becomes a valuation factor.** The G7's coordinated cyber exercises provide evidence that financial authorities are treating AI-enabled disruption as an international systemic-risk challenge. Banks, insurers, financial-market infrastructures and their technology providers will increasingly be assessed on their capacity to maintain and restore critical services.

**4. Europe — Monetary policy cannot substitute for industrial transformation.** Europe's combination of weak potential growth, expensive energy and insufficient AI capital formation is becoming increasingly costly. The policy response must connect capital-markets integration, electricity infrastructure, industrial automation, accelerated computing and workforce adaptation. The objective is not simply greater AI adoption. It is a measurable increase in European productivity and capital formation.

**5. Central-bank coordination — The new frontier is systemic velocity.** AI can accelerate investment decisions, financial transactions, cyber threats and the transmission of market stress. The interaction between these mechanisms may require faster supervisory coordination and more resilient financial infrastructure. The G7 initiative signals that international authorities are beginning to operationalise that response.

::section The Final Take — The Great Split Becomes a Monetary Regime | Conclusion

The developments of October 16 reveal a decisive evolution in the AI Supercycle.

The Federal Reserve is explicitly recognising the different time horizons of AI-driven inflation and productivity. The G7 is preparing coordinated operational tests of systemic AI risk. The IMF is documenting the structural constraints preventing Europe from capturing a larger share of the technological dividend. And China is seeking to strengthen its financial architecture as technological competition intensifies.

These developments point toward a common conclusion. The next economic regime will be shaped by three competing speeds: the speed of AI capital formation, the speed of productivity diffusion and the speed at which financial institutions can absorb technological disruption.

For investors, the central test is whether returns on productive AI capital can grow faster than financing costs and systemic risks. For European policymakers, the central test is whether Europe can mobilise sufficient domestic investment to escape a prolonged period of low productivity and high external dependence. For central banks, the task is to maintain monetary credibility and financial resilience throughout a transition whose economic benefits and costs are emerging at different speeds.

::close
The winners of the next monetary regime will be the economies that ==convert AI capital into productivity faster== than inflation, financing costs and systemic vulnerabilities accumulate.
