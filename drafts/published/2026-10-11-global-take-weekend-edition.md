The world has experienced technological revolutions before. Electrification, the internal combustion engine, telecommunications, and mass production transformed the economic landscape approximately 120 years ago. They created new industries, destroyed established advantages, and reordered the hierarchy of nations.

What is happening today is not unprecedented in its nature. It is unprecedented in its potential velocity, reach, and consequences.

The Industrial Revolution of the early twentieth century unfolded over decades. Companies, industries, and countries had time to adapt, imitate, invest, and eventually catch up. Artificial intelligence threatens to compress that adjustment period dramatically. The difference is not simply that technological change is accelerating—it is that the economic system itself is becoming increasingly dependent on interconnected infrastructures of computing, energy, data, algorithms, and capital that reinforce one another. The result may be a ==switch-on, switch-off economy==.

::signals The Mechanics of the Great Switch | How velocity rewrites the rules

# History Is Repeating—But the Clock Has Changed
Electrification did not immediately transform industrial productivity. Factories initially replaced steam engines with electric motors while preserving existing processes. The breakthrough came when manufacturers redesigned factories, workflows, and organizational structures around electricity.

The same distinction applies to AI. Deploying applications is not equivalent to becoming an AI-native enterprise. But electricity required physical networks; AI combines physical infrastructure with software that can be replicated across borders at extraordinary speed.
signal: Diffusion could be far faster—even as productive integration stays constrained by energy, investment, regulation, and organizational change.

# The New Economics of Competitive Advantage
Scale, accumulated capabilities, distribution, and access to capital remain essential. But AI introduces a more powerful mechanism: the ability to improve the productive system continuously. Leaders do not simply become larger—they may become progressively more capable of accelerating away from competitors.

Yet technological leadership is not automatically financial leadership. Competitive diffusion, commoditization, and excessive capital spending can destroy returns even as adoption accelerates.
signal: The decisive variable is the ability to convert technological acceleration into sustainable economic returns.

# The Great Repricing of Capital
The transition requires enormous upfront investment in computing, energy, networks, software, and industrial modernization—but the benefits will not arrive at the same pace as the commitments. In the first phase, infrastructure demand sustains capex and inflationary pressure. In the second, successful deployment lowers unit costs and exerts disinflationary pressure.

If financing costs remain elevated while returns are delayed, capital markets may become increasingly selective. Strong balance sheets attract favorable terms; those dependent on uncertain future cash flows face rising capital costs, falling valuations, and forced consolidation.
signal: The AI revolution may accelerate technological diffusion while simultaneously concentrating financial returns.

# The Switch-On / Switch-Off Economy
The metaphor should not be read literally—economies will not flip overnight. The more consequential possibility is that certain competitive thresholds become increasingly difficult to cross. Once an industry establishes AI-native standards for cost, quality, speed, and experience, legacy economics may struggle to compete.

The same applies to nations. Those combining abundant energy, digital infrastructure, capital, research, and institutional agility may establish compounding advantages. Others risk becoming consumers of technologies developed elsewhere.
signal: The new divide is less about who uses AI and more about who ==owns, controls, and economically captures== its productive capabilities.

Greater divergence between AI-ready and AI-constrained economies.

::takeaways Five Key Takeaways | For investors, boards, and governments
- **Investors:** Reprice execution, not technological promises. Distinguish infrastructure spending from realized productivity; prioritize cash-flow conversion and capital efficiency over AI exposure alone.
- **Boards:** Redesign the enterprise before competitors redefine the industry. Organizational redesign, talent, governance, and capital allocation must advance together.
- **Governments:** Treat AI readiness as national economic infrastructure—energy, computing capacity, capital formation, skills, and regulatory effectiveness.
- **Capital markets:** Prepare for a more selective cycle. Technological progress can coexist with financial distress, triggering consolidation and sharp valuation adjustments.
- **Europe:** The cost of delay may become nonlinear. Significant industrial and research assets risk being undone by fragmented capital markets, energy costs, and limited deployment capacity.

::close The twentieth century rewarded those who industrialized first. The twenty-first may reward those who become ==AI-native before the economics of competition are fundamentally rewritten==—because being late may no longer mean arriving second. It may mean never arriving at all.
