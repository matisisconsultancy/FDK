Jason Furman has compressed into a single experiment a challenge that will occupy universities, companies and governments for years. The former chair of Barack Obama's Council of Economic Advisers used AI-assisted coding to build Econvitals, a free platform for economic data and analysis. He told Bloomberg's Stephanomics that it could reproduce much of the analytical capacity of the 35-person operation he once led at the White House.

The experiment reveals two very different speeds. An experienced economist can acquire new analytical capacity almost as soon as the tools become available. Developing an economist who knows which questions to ask, which answers to distrust and when to act on incomplete evidence still takes years.

::signal

- **Scarcity shifts** to supervisor attention, access to consequential work and institutions willing to stand behind a learner.

::section The Big Take | Two speeds, one pipeline

The traditional career absorbed some of that difference. An employer needed junior staff to prepare forecasts, reconcile data and draft briefs. In doing that work, juniors learned how senior people reached decisions and where they could go wrong, and their salaries helped finance professional training. AI can take over part of that work, leaving employers and universities to find another way to pay for the training.

That is the challenge of education in the Velocity Age. The economy can change what it asks of people faster than its institutions can prepare them to do it.

::section What Should Education Accelerate? | Practice before consequence

AI can shorten the time between a question and an explanation, an attempt and feedback, an idea and a working model. Universities can use that speed to give students more practice before their first consequential assignment, then spend the time saved examining which evidence actually distinguishes among competing explanations.

Some capabilities develop differently. An economist who revisits a forecast after a recession, or a credit analyst who follows a loan through a downturn, learns from consequences that unfold over time. Institutions can start that learning earlier by giving people responsibility, close supervision and a chance to revisit their decisions once results are known.

This changes the design of a degree. Students should move repeatedly between foundational study, AI-assisted inquiry and supervised work. A credible qualification would show how someone's reasoning held up as the assignments grew harder and the consequences more real. The same structure can serve a professional returning mid-career to take on a decision that barely existed at graduation.

::section What Will Become Scarce When Instruction Is Abundant? | The next educational divide

As good instruction becomes easier to obtain, the scarce resources will be the attention of an experienced supervisor, access to work with real consequences and an institution willing to stand behind its assessment of a learner. A university with strong ties to hospitals, laboratories, firms and public agencies can put those resources within reach, and its competitive position will depend on how many students gain that access and how far they progress.

This is where the next educational divide could emerge. Two graduates may share the same AI tutor and submit work of similar quality. One has had to defend a recommendation to a client and revise it after seeing the consequences; the other is still trying to secure a first assignment. If that experience depends on family connections or unpaid work, cheaper instruction can coexist with a narrower route to advancement.

::section Who Will Finance the Next Generation of Expertise? | A shared pipeline problem

A company can cut its junior intake and recognise the payroll savings within a year. The cost of a thinner pipeline surfaces years later in higher recruitment costs, weaker succession and growth it lacks the people to pursue. Each firm has an incentive to hire experienced talent that someone else trained — and if enough firms follow that logic, the industry can spend years rebuilding the expertise it needs to grow.

Companies can reinvest time freed up by AI in supervision and give juniors responsibility earlier. Universities can make paid, supervised work part of a degree. Where employers recruit from the same talent pool, professional bodies can help them share training costs and recognise qualifications across firms.

::takeaways Takeaways for Investors and Policymakers | Where the gains are captured
- **Investors** should test the durability of AI-driven margin gains: alongside revenue per employee, track junior intake, internal promotion rates, the cost of hiring experienced people and succession depth — can the company sustain growth with the talent it is developing today?
- **Education investors** should favour institutions that help graduates advance; repeat employer participation, paid placements and progression into roles with greater responsibility test pricing power as instruction gets cheaper.
- **Policymakers** should widen access to the first consequential assignment: co-finance paid, supervised placements where entry routes are narrowing, give learners a portable record of competence and judge programmes by sustained progression into skilled work.

::section The Fundamental Question | The judgment gap

Can we build an education system that moves at the speed of technological change while giving people the time and responsibility it takes to develop judgment?

::close
The answer will shape who captures the gains from AI, how readily economies adapt and ==how many people have a hand in deciding what comes next==.
