The most consequential signal from global markets this weekend is a reversal in equity leadership: the companies that dominated the AI-driven expansion are losing their relative advantage while the broader economy begins to outperform.

Since October 6, the cap-weighted S&P 500 has fallen about **2.9%** while its equal-weighted counterpart has gained **3.9%** — a 6.8-point divergence within the same universe. This is not evidence that the AI Supercycle is ending, but that investors are reassessing the value of earnings predictability and cash-flow durability.

The central thesis is changing: the market is beginning to reward the capacity to generate economic value under uncertainty, rather than simply the capacity to promise extraordinary future growth.

::signal
- **Leadership is rotating**, as the cap-weighted S&P 500 fell 2.9% since October 6 while the equal-weighted index rose 3.9% — a 6.8-point divergence.
- **Bond markets impose a higher hurdle**, with the US 10-year Treasury yield ending Friday around 5.14%.
- **AI financing faces scrutiny**, as Anthropic's proposed IPO of up to $64.4bn and a $26bn placement test independent demand versus supplier support.
- **Energy risk is rising**, with Iran claiming three unverified Hormuz tanker mine incidents after Brent settled at $95.84.
- **Europe drew $35.13bn of weekly equity inflows**, yet Q3 GDP grew only 0.4% with employment unchanged.
- **The Great Split** now divides those who create, finance and retain the value of technological acceleration.

::signals Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy | what the rotation reveals
# The Great Equity Broadening
The FT's November 15 analysis identifies a rotation from technology leaders toward healthcare, staples and utilities; Walmart has risen ~20%, Coca-Cola 13% and J&J 12% since October 6. LSEG Lipper data show $50.99bn of weekly global equity-fund inflows, $35.13bn to Europe. But defensives are no longer cheap — technology trades near 19x forward earnings versus more than 20x for healthcare and staples.
signal: Favour durable cash generation and reasonable valuations; do not treat defensiveness itself as an advantage.

# Nvidia and Anthropic
Nvidia's November 12 leadership transition has focused attention on its supplier-financing and strategic equity relationships. Anthropic's proposed IPO could raise up to $64.4bn alongside a $26bn private placement, with Nvidia's planned ~$10bn participation drawing scrutiny. The decisive question is whether companies can sustain growth without continuous capital support from their own ecosystem.
signal: Monitor credit guarantees, customer concentration and free-cash-flow conversion ahead of November 17 results and the November 19 debut.

# Sovereign Capital
The US 10-year yield has climbed above 5%; BoE's Huw Pill signalled further rate rises may be needed in 2027. S&P affirmed Italy at BBB+ with a positive outlook on November 13, while the Bank of Italy warned on November 12 that frequent financial-sector tax changes distort investment — the government proposes €5.9bn of additional bank-sector revenue.
signal: The next sovereign premium will reward countries that stabilize public finances without undermining the institutions financing growth.

# Hormuz
Brent ended Friday at $95.84, down ~2.5% on the week, with US crude inventories up 4.7m barrels. Iranian authorities then reported three tanker mine incidents on Saturday; Reuters could not independently verify them. The risk is a renewed inflation shock while rates and sovereign financing costs are already elevated.
signal: Watch energy-sensitive industrials, shipping insurance, refining margins and European gas as the transmission from shipping to margins stays live.

# Industrial Reliability
The FAA's November 13 ground stop disrupted more than 20,000 flights, with a router upgrade affecting 17 of 22 en-route centres; Congress has provided $12.5bn for modernization against ~$50bn sought. China exported ~927,000 passenger cars in October, up 62.3% YoY, as domestic sales fell 27.4%.
signal: The most valuable industrial AI may improve existing physical systems rather than demand new capital-intensive infrastructure.

::pull The Great Split is no longer between technological leaders and laggards — it is between those who convert acceleration into sustainable value and those financing it without retaining a sufficient share of the rewards.

::takeaways Five Takeaways | for investors, boards and policymakers
- Equity investors: the next opportunity is broader, but not indiscriminately cheaper — seek durable earnings at reasonable valuations.
- Credit investors: distinguish contractual revenue from cash received, and strategic guarantees from completed investment.
- Corporate boards: operational resilience — reliability, supplier diversification, capital efficiency — is becoming a financial asset.
- European policymakers: turn capital inflows into productive capital formation for enterprise, energy and mobility.
- Global investors: the divide is between financial returns and financial dependence.
