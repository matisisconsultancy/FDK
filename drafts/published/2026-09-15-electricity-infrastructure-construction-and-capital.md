The decisive question is therefore not whether AI is inflationary or deflationary. It is: which effect dominates at which layer and over what period of time? That is the analytical framework central banks, investors and policymakers increasingly need.

This morning gives us a clearer view of where the AI Supercycle is going — not toward one single bottleneck, but toward a *sequence* of them. Accelerated computing. Memory. Optics. Power. Energy security. Capital. Every time the system solves one scarcity, growth pushes pressure into another.

::pull THE GREAT SPLIT
Not simply AI versus non-AI — but those who own the scarce complements of intelligence versus those who must continuously finance access to them.

::section Implications for Investors | one level deeper into the physical system
The investment strategy should move one level deeper into the physical system. The most attractive assets increasingly possess some combination of scarcity, pricing power, capacity visibility, contractual demand and short capital payback. Soitec's multi-year commitments are a good example of the kind of evidence investors should look for. Not simply **AI exposure** — but ==AI scarcity + contractual economics==.

At the same time, the denominator must remain central. Warsh has shifted rate expectations. Oil is above $90 again. Japanese yields are rising. The long-duration hurdle rate remains high. The most vulnerable assets are still those whose valuation depends on cash flows arriving far in the future, high leverage, continuous refinancing and aggressive utilization assumptions. The best AI exposure increasingly combines **scarcity rents with proximity of cash flow**.

::section Implications for Executives | design substitution, not ownership
Enterprise AI architecture should now be designed around **bottleneck independence**. It is no longer sufficient to secure a model or accelerated computing. Executives increasingly need to think across models, accelerators, memory, optical networking, cloud capacity, energy and financing.

But that does not mean owning everything. It means designing substitution. The key board-level question becomes: if one scarce layer doubles in price or becomes geopolitically unavailable, can the architecture still operate? That is resilience — and resilience is increasingly part of productivity.

::section Implications for Policymakers | three conclusions for Europe
First, strategic autonomy should begin with actual bottlenecks, not slogans. Soitec's photonics position is strategically more meaningful than announcing another generic AI fund.

Second, Europe must avoid answering China solely through tariffs. Germany's improving industrial outlook is encouraging — its export-expectations index reached a four-year high in August, helped in part by AI, digitalization and government-backed investment — but its trade deficit with China and competitive pressure remain severe.

Third, energy and capital-market integration are increasingly inseparable from technology policy. French harmonized inflation has already accelerated to 2.7%, German inflation data arrive today, and euro-area inflation this week will influence expectations for an ECB move on September 10. Europe's strategic objective should therefore be ==Productive Intelligence per Euro of Scarce Capital== — not maximum subsidy expenditure.
