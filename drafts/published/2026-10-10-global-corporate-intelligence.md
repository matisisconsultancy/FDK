The companies shaping the Next Economy are no longer distinguished by how much they invest in artificial intelligence, but by how rapidly they transform capital, technology and talent into productive capacity, earnings and strategic advantage.

The fundamental question: which corporations are converting technological acceleration into durable economic power—and which are being overtaken by a changing competitive environment? This edition establishes the reference FDK Global Velocity Index scores for subsequent comparisons. Reporting date Saturday, October 10, 2026; market observations reflect the completed session of Friday, October 9.

The central thesis is that corporate velocity is becoming a financial variable. The next market rotation will increasingly distinguish companies that monetize technological acceleration from those that must finance it without comparable improvements in productivity or cash generation.

::section The Big Picture | Velocity meets the cost of capital

Three developments define today's corporate landscape. First, the AI infrastructure supercycle continues to accelerate. NVIDIA reported quarterly revenue of $96.2 billion, up 106% year over year; TSMC's third-quarter revenue rose approximately 50%; and Broadcom's AI semiconductor revenue climbed 221%. These are realized commercial results, not investment announcements.

Second, the cost of financing expansion is rising. On October 9 the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield stood near 5.24%, while Brent crude traded around $105 per barrel. Higher financing and energy costs are tightening the conditions under which infrastructure investments must generate acceptable returns.

Third, the boundaries separating industries are becoming less stable. SpaceX's proposed $8 billion acquisition of low-band spectrum signaled an ambition to compete more directly with established mobile operators. On October 9, AT&T shares fell approximately 9.8%, Verizon declined 8.7% and T-Mobile lost 13.3%, while major telecommunications-tower companies advanced sharply.

A note on method: the FDK GVI evaluates a selected global universe across ten dimensions, weighting capital efficiency (15%), productivity acceleration (15%) and realized economic capture above technological investment alone. Scores are provisional, sector-aware research assessments—not audited measurements. No comparable October 9 dataset exists, so daily changes are designated **N/V**. A share-price movement is not equivalent to a change in GVI score.

::signals Top 10 Global Velocity Leaders | The corporations controlling the transition to the Next Economy

# NVIDIA — 97 — The architecture of accelerated computing
Fiscal Q2 2027 revenue reached $96.2 billion, including $89 billion from Data Center. Its position extends beyond GPUs into software, networking and integrated computing infrastructure. Principal risks are customer concentration, supply dependence, export restrictions and the sustainability of customers' AI expenditure.
signal: Ecosystem breadth plus extraordinary commercial momentum.

# TSMC — 95 — The manufacturing constraint on global AI
Reported approximately NT$1.49 trillion in Q3 revenue, about 50% above the corresponding quarter of 2025. Its advantage lies in advanced process technology and deeply embedded customer relationships; geographic concentration and geopolitical exposure remain major structural risks.
signal: The physical bottleneck on which the entire cycle depends.

# Microsoft — 94 — The enterprise monetization platform
Azure revenue grew 43% in fiscal Q4, while Microsoft 365 Copilot exceeded 30 million paid seats. The decisive question is whether AI revenue and productivity gains continue to outpace infrastructure depreciation and operating costs.
signal: AI integrated into existing enterprise workflows.

# Broadcom — 93 — The custom-silicon alternative
AI semiconductor revenue reached $16.7 billion in fiscal Q3 2026, up 221% year over year. Custom accelerators and networking offer hyperscalers an alternative to general-purpose GPUs; vulnerability is concentration among major customers.
signal: The counterweight to GPU-only architectures.

# Alphabet — 91 — AI across an existing global ecosystem
Google Cloud revenue grew 82% in Q2 2026 alongside substantial infrastructure investment. The financial test is whether cloud profit and AI revenue can justify rising capital intensity without weakening consolidated free cash flow.
signal: Multiple channels to commercialize AI.

# Amazon — 90 — Infrastructure scale meets operational productivity
Q2 AWS revenue grew 37%, with consolidated operating income up 43%. Cloud, custom processors, logistics automation and enterprise distribution create several independent productivity channels. The main risk is the timing of returns on very large investments.
signal: Breadth of independent productivity channels.

# ASML — 89 — The technological gatekeeper
Generated €9.3 billion in Q2 revenue and lifted its 2026 outlook to €43–45 billion. Advanced lithography remains essential to leading-edge capacity; customer cycles and export controls set the pace of conversion.
signal: Essential to every expansion of the frontier.

# CATL — 88 — The energy-storage accelerator
First-half revenue rose approximately 55%, with energy-storage revenue up 87.5%. It connects electric mobility, grid storage and resilient energy infrastructure; pricing, expansion and geopolitical restrictions remain risks.
signal: Where AI demand meets the grid.

# Samsung Electronics — 87 — The memory supercycle
Preliminary Q3 operating profit reached KRW 107.4 trillion on extraordinary semiconductor demand. Strength lies in scale and vertical integration, but memory-margin sustainability, continued foundry losses and cyclicality justify a lower ranking than headline profit suggests.
signal: Exceptional profit, but earnings quality still unproven.

# Schneider Electric — 86 — The industrial infrastructure beneficiary
First-half revenue of approximately €21.2 billion, 14% organic growth, adjusted EBITA margin of 19.3%. Exposure to power distribution, cooling, automation and electrification; risks are execution capacity, working capital and project delays.
signal: Industrial electrification as a technology-like growth asset.

::section The 10 Corporate Laggards | Where competitive transformation remains incomplete

Inclusion in the lower end of the universe does not imply financial distress, nor that shares are unattractive. These scores reflect the distance between current position and the productivity, innovation and optionality of the leaders: Nissan (49), Porsche AG (51), Stellantis (54), Intel (56), Volkswagen Group (58), Bayer (60), Boeing (62), Vodafone (64), AT&T (66), Verizon (67).

The automotive divide is structural, not technological. Porsche reported a 16% decline in deliveries over the first nine months of 2026, including a 33% fall in China; Stellantis posted a first-half adjusted operating margin of only 2.1%; Volkswagen's first-half operating profit fell about 12%. The challenge is financing new platforms while defending margins in faster-changing markets.

The capital-efficiency challenge unites different models. Intel delivered 25% revenue growth in Q2 2026 but remains highly capital-intensive. Boeing turned free cash flow positive in its latest quarter yet stayed negative for the half, carrying $45.9 billion of debt. Bayer held net financial debt of approximately €33.6 billion at end-June. Improved execution must become sustained cash generation.

The telecommunications challenge is forward-looking. Vodafone reported 5.2% organic service-revenue growth; AT&T generated $4.7 billion of quarterly free cash flow; Verizon posted a 16% rise in first-half free cash flow. Friday's declines revealed how rapidly investors can reprice the possibility that satellite-terrestrial networks alter the value of established infrastructure—though the threat remains subject to regulation and competitive response.

::patterns Five Hidden Patterns | The second-order effects

# [1] Supply chains reorganize
The first stage of the supercycle rewarded computing capacity; the next rewards the complementary assets that make it productive—advanced manufacturing, memory, electrical equipment, cooling, storage and distribution. This explains the significance of TSMC, ASML, CATL and Schneider. Revenue migrates from computing equipment into the broader industrial systems supporting deployment.

# [2] Rates reprice speed
A 10-year yield above 5% changes the economics of long-duration projects. The cycle separates corporations into those financing expansion internally, those relying on external funding, and those unable to invest at scale. The cost of capital becomes a competitive selection mechanism.

# [3] Infrastructure as an asset class
Industrial AI requires computing, electricity, grid connections, cooling, telecoms and sophisticated storage. Advantage shifts toward companies integrating digital capability with physical infrastructure—letting industrial firms acquire technology-like growth without technology-company models.

# [4] Boundaries redrawn
The October 9 selloff illustrates a wider phenomenon. SpaceX's spectrum strategy challenges assumptions about the stability of industry boundaries. Valuations may incorporate competitive threats long before those threats affect reported earnings.

# [5] A new earnings-quality test
Samsung's results show the profitability possible when demand outruns supply. But growth may reflect leadership, scarcity, pricing power or inventory effects—each deserving different multiples. A high GVI score requires resilience across the cycle, not merely a strong quarter.

::section The Capital-Efficiency Test | Investment velocity versus economic capture

The methodology separates three financial stages: capital formation (capex, financing, backlog), productivity conversion (margins, utilization, unit economics) and economic capture (free cash flow, ROIC, competitive positioning). The greatest valuation risk arises when capital formation accelerates faster than demonstrated economic capture; the difference becomes clear only through execution.

Today's FDK interpretation: NVIDIA, Microsoft, Broadcom and TSMC have demonstrated particularly strong commercial conversion. Alphabet and Amazon combine substantial revenue acceleration with significant infrastructure requirements; their future ranking will depend on incremental returns rather than expenditure growth. A high GVI score is not, by itself, a recommendation to buy. Corporate leadership and investment attractiveness are distinct judgments.

Among challengers beyond the Top 10: SK hynix remains a strong contender in high-bandwidth memory; AMD is an important potential source of CPU and AI-accelerator competition; BYD saw overseas sales reach approximately 39% of September deliveries; Tencent, Alibaba, Siemens, ABB and Hitachi all warrant assessment. SpaceX deserves separate consideration as a potential disruptor whose mobile economics are unproven.

::takeaways Investor takeaways | Five decisions

- Separate growth from value creation: favor accelerating cash returns over rising capex—watch incremental ROIC and free-cash-flow conversion.
- Identify enabling infrastructure around semiconductor manufacturing, memory, power and cooling—watch utilization, order conversion and margins.
- Reprice financing risk for businesses dependent on external funding—watch interest coverage, refinancing needs and credit spreads.
- Monitor competitive boundary shifts as emerging platforms alter incumbent economics—watch market share, customer migration and pricing power.
- Preserve valuation discipline: distinguish strategically important corporations from attractively priced securities.
