On November 9 the Reserve Bank of Australia published a major speech and four research studies on the economic consequences of artificial intelligence. Together they provide unusually concrete evidence of the divergence between AI capital formation and realised productivity.

The findings are striking: Australian data-centre investment has risen tenfold in three years, South Korean DRAM export prices are up more than 200% over twelve months, and household wealth exposed to AI equities has climbed sharply — yet the RBA still finds little evidence of a material AI-driven improvement in aggregate productivity. Most importantly, it is now explicitly examining whether the investment boom is contributing to a higher neutral interest rate.

::signal
- **AI investment is becoming measurable** faster than its productivity dividend — the monetary core of the Great Split.
- **The RBA is examining** whether the AI capital boom is lifting the neutral interest rate, with model estimates already higher.
- **A productivity revolution** need not require lower rates; during the investment phase the opposite may occur.
- **Industrial AI deflation is unproven:** 35% of surveyed firms report no productivity gains, only ~11% report significant gains.
- **Household AI-equity exposure** rose from 2.0% of financial wealth at end-2022 to 5.3% in June 2026, adding a new transmission channel.
- **The next inflection** comes when central banks can separate the inflation of building AI from the disinflation of using it.

::section The Monetary Breakthrough — AI And The Neutral Rate | Hunter's channel

In her November 9 speech, Assistant Governor Sarah Hunter identified an increasingly important channel through which AI could affect monetary policy. Large-scale AI investment increases the demand for capital; if global savings do not expand correspondingly, equilibrium real interest rates may rise.

The RBA notes tentative evidence consistent with this mechanism: its model estimates of the neutral rate have increased in recent years, although the contribution attributable specifically to AI remains uncertain. Hunter emphasised that stronger growth and higher rates can coexist, citing the internet expansion of the late 1990s and early 2000s. This is analytical recognition, not a new AI-specific estimate or a change in the policy target.

::pull AI could raise the equilibrium cost of capital before it lowers the economy's cost of production.

The implication is substantial. A productivity revolution does not necessarily require lower interest rates — during the investment phase, the opposite may occur. This reinforces the High-Rate Productivity scenario within the Secular Volatility framework.

::section Industrial AI Deflation — The Evidence Is Incomplete | Adoption is not productivity

The RBA's research distinguishes sharply between technology adoption and productivity creation. Business adoption has accelerated, but remains limited: roughly 35% of surveyed firms reported no productivity gains from AI, while only around 11% reported significant gains. At the economy-wide level, no substantial productivity improvement attributable to AI has yet been identified.

Hunter also challenged the assumption that AI will produce permanent disinflation. Initial infrastructure investment raises demand and may raise prices; productivity improvements can later expand supply, but higher productivity may itself stimulate spending and incomes. The long-run inflation effect could be broadly neutral rather than persistently negative.

For the ECB and Fed, the distinction is fundamental: industrial AI deflation should not be built into policy expectations before productivity gains become observable. For investors, the line runs between companies investing in AI infrastructure and companies converting AI into higher output, lower unit costs and stronger free cash flow.

::section The Great Split — Employment And Wealth | New transmission channels

The RBA's labour-market analysis finds little evidence of widespread AI-driven displacement, though tentative signs of weaker hiring are emerging among younger workers in more AI-exposed occupations. These findings are descriptive. The research also recognises that AI may affect the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment, complicating interpretations of labour-market slack.

The financial benefits remain concentrated. Household exposure to AI-related equities rose from 2.0% of financial wealth at end-2022 to 5.3% in June 2026, mostly through retirement savings. This creates a chain: AI valuations → household wealth → consumption → monetary conditions. An AI equity correction could simultaneously weaken wealth, confidence and investment demand, even before employment deteriorates materially.

::section What Changes For The ECB, Fed And Europe | Three sharpened concerns

**First, imported AI inflation may matter more.** The RBA identifies substantial price increases in semiconductor supply chains, particularly South Korean memory chips, now reaching some downstream products — though direct consumer-price effects remain concentrated in small expenditure categories.

**Second, energy and infrastructure create divergence.** Countries with reliable electricity, grid capacity and efficient infrastructure financing are better positioned to convert AI spending into productive capacity. For Europe, this raises the importance of integrated energy markets, faster permitting and deeper capital markets.

**Third, financial supervision must address concentration.** The findings complement the BIS's November 3 warning on overinvestment, complex financing and concentrated technology-related risks. A reassessment of AI returns could transmit through corporate credit, equity and non-bank institutions. The lesson: subsidising investment volume is not the same as raising productivity.

::takeaways The Final Take | The monetary price of the AI supercycle
- AI's investment cycle is already influencing capital demand, supply chains and financial exposures; its productivity dividend remains uncertain in magnitude and timing.
- For central banks, restrictive conditions may be necessary to contain inflation even while the economy funds future productive capacity.
- For investors, capital efficiency, balance-sheet resilience and realised returns gain importance.
- For European policymakers, Industrial AI deployment — not infrastructure expenditure alone — becomes the strategic priority.

::close The next monetary inflection will come when central banks can distinguish the inflation created by ==building AI== from the disinflation generated by ==using it==.
