The defining question of the next economic cycle is no longer how fast economies can grow. It is how much disruption they can absorb without losing their capacity to grow.

Today's award of the 2026 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences to **Robert E. Hall** and **Michael Woodford**, for their work on the microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization, offers a framework for the next phase of markets. Their core insight: shocks do not propagate mechanically — they are transmitted through expectations, consumption, investment, balance sheets and the credibility of policy.

Our thesis is that markets are entering an **Age of Shocks**, in which the capacity to anticipate, absorb, finance and exploit disruption becomes a primary determinant of valuations and capital allocation. The Nobel Committee is recognizing foundational research, not predicting a regime; the market interpretation is ours.

::pull THE ROTATION
The next major rotation may separate neither growth from value nor technology from industry. It may separate ==shock absorbers from shock amplifiers==.

::section The Nobel Insight: Expectations Are Part of the Shock | Hall & Woodford
A change in interest rates does not merely alter the cost of borrowing; it changes expectations about future demand, profitability and asset prices. An energy shock affects margins, purchasing power and central-bank reaction functions. An AI shock changes the expected profitability of entire industries.

The value of an asset depends not only on the shock itself, but on how it changes expectations about future cash flows, discount rates and risk. This is the bridge between the Nobel Prize and the emerging architecture of markets.

::patterns Five Shocks, One System | circular causality
# Geopolitical & energy
Conflict and energy-infrastructure disruption raise inflation and transport costs. Energy security is becoming a determinant of sovereign creditworthiness and corporate profitability.
# Sovereign debt
Rising yields and refinancing costs can turn the sovereign balance sheet — traditionally a stabilizer — into a transmission mechanism for instability.
# AI displacement
AI accelerates productivity but can destroy the value of existing technologies, undermine incumbents and concentrate profits.
# Capital formation
The AI Supercycle demands immediate investment in compute, data centers and power; productivity gains arrive later — a mismatch that creates financing and valuation risk.
# Competitiveness
Nations and firms diverge in their ability to secure energy, mobilize capital and capture AI returns — a self-reinforcing divergence.

These shocks are not independent. A geopolitical shock lifts energy prices; persistent inflation limits easing; higher rates raise debt service; fiscal constraints cut investment; weaker investment damages competitiveness. Markets must price not only individual shocks, but the probability that they reinforce one another.

::section The AI Supercycle: Capacity Is Not Capture | the decisive distinction
Most traditional shocks destroy capacity. AI can increase productive capacity while disrupting the structures through which profits are generated. The transition runs from **AI-infrastructure inflation** toward **industrial-AI disinflation**, and it will not be smooth: if capital formation outruns realized productivity, markets may face a valuation adjustment before the benefits materialize.

Building AI infrastructure is not equivalent to generating returns on invested capital. The next phase will be decided by the conversion of technological acceleration into capital productivity — separating durable beneficiaries from those exposed to overinvestment.

::section Central Banks and The Great Split | asymmetric resilience
Central banks must now distinguish demand, supply, AI productivity and sovereign-debt shocks — each demanding a different response. Woodford's work on expectations is pivotal: credible policy stabilizes behavior; eroded credibility magnifies the original shock. This shapes the **Fed**, the **ECB** and the **PBoC**.

The result is **The Great Split**: resilient economies attract capital, raise productivity and strengthen fiscal capacity; constrained economies face higher financing costs and the reverse cycle. The United States benefits from deep markets and energy, despite fiscal and valuation vulnerabilities; China combines industrial scale with demand and debt challenges; Europe's strengths are constrained by fragmented capital markets and uneven energy competitiveness. Economic resilience is becoming an investable dimension of national competitiveness.

::section A Shock-Adjusted Framework: SAVI | velocity meets resilience
Velocity without resilience creates fragility. We propose a complementary **Shock-Adjusted Velocity Index (SAVI)**, assessing the capacity to sustain productive acceleration under adverse conditions across five dimensions: financial resilience, energy resilience, technological adaptability, institutional credibility and capital productivity. SAVI is a proposed framework — not yet calibrated or back-tested.

::signals Five Signals to Monitor | which pathway is forming
# Sovereign term premia and refinancing costs
A persistent rise in long yields not explained by stronger growth may signal rising compensation for fiscal, inflation or duration risk.
signal: Watch yields that growth cannot explain.
# Energy and electricity-system constraints
Grid reliability, capacity and industrial power costs increasingly govern technological investment.
signal: Electricity is becoming the real energy constraint.
# Credit spreads and financing availability
Widening spreads and weaker issuance reveal whether macro disturbances are becoming financing shocks.
signal: Financing shocks appear in credit first.
# AI capex versus realized cash flow
Compare infrastructure commitments with utilization, revenue, margins and returns on invested capital.
signal: Measure monetization, not expenditure.
# Cross-asset correlations and liquidity
Simultaneous losses in assets expected to diversify may signal propagation through discount rates, leverage or forced selling.
signal: Rising correlation is the amplification signal.

::takeaways Five Takeaways for Investors and Boards | resilience as strategy
- **Reprice resilience, not simply growth** — evaluate projections against financing needs and the capacity to preserve returns under stress.
- **Distinguish productive AI investment from speculative capital formation** — the measure is free cash flow and returns on invested capital.
- **Treat sovereign risk as a cross-asset variable** — fiscal vulnerabilities influence banks, credit, currencies and equity risk premia.
- **Make second-order effects central** — the largest consequences emerge through investment, financing and positioning, not immediate impact.
- **Build strategic optionality before disruption arrives** — liquidity, financing, energy and disciplined capital allocation are expensive to acquire mid-crisis.

The 2025 prize to **Mokyr, Aghion and Howitt** explained how innovation transforms economies; the 2026 prize explains how economies absorb the resulting disturbances. Together they illuminate the defining tension of the AI era.

::close The ultimate competitive advantage will belong not to those who avoid shocks, but to ==those who can turn shocks into acceleration==.
