The next monetary-policy divergence is being shaped by a fundamental asymmetry: the inflationary costs of building the AI economy are arriving before its productivity benefits can materially influence aggregate inflation.

Three developments on November 4 reinforce this. Euro-area inflation has reached 4.0%, twice the ECB's target, with energy prices rising 20.8% annually. Sweden's Riksbank raised its policy rate 25 basis points to 2.0%. And the ECB's updated wage tracker suggests negotiated wage pressures will remain relatively persistent into 2027.

Europe may face higher interest rates because of an energy shock while simultaneously requiring more investment in electricity infrastructure, automation and Industrial AI. That is consequential for European capital formation and the next phase of the Great Split.

- **The ECB wage tracker** projects ~2.8% negotiated growth in H1 2027 — firmer, but not definitive second-round evidence.
- **Energy inflation now reprices capital**, raising financing costs for AI-intensive industry before productivity gains materialise.
- **The Bank of England decides November 5**, the immediate test of whether energy inflation turns persistent.

::section The ECB: Inflation at 4%, Productivity Unresolved | the supply-driven shock
The October euro-area estimate shows headline inflation rising to 4.0% from 3.8%, and energy inflation accelerating to 20.8% from 18.9%. Core inflation held at 2.5% and services rose to 3.3%. The ECB deposit rate remains 2.50%.

The divergence between headline and core is critical: energy is accelerating rapidly, but underlying inflation has not risen proportionately. This suggests a predominantly supply-driven shock — though firmer services inflation warrants attention.

The hidden transmission runs: energy inflation → higher industrial costs → higher financing requirements → tighter monetary conditions → weaker productive investment. This is particularly consequential for data centres, semiconductors and automation, which require electricity and heavy upfront capital. Higher electricity prices can undermine their economics before productivity benefits materialise. Europe's inflation problem risks becoming a productivity problem.

::section The Riksbank: The Next Regime Becomes Visible | AI enters the risk ledger
Sweden raised its policy rate from 1.75% to 2.00%, effective November 11, signalling further increases if the outlook holds. The decision reflects stronger domestic activity, continuing supply disruptions, and the effects of energy prices and currency depreciation.

Beneath a low headline — September CPIF at 1.5% — lies the detail: excluding temporary fiscal measures, the rate was approximately 3.0%; excluding energy, 1.6%.

Crucially, the Riksbank explicitly identifies AI-investment uncertainty as a potential source of weaker global demand. Central banks now confront two opposite risks from the same cycle: sustained AI spending feeding inflationary pressure, or a reversal weakening capital expenditure and financial conditions. The Riksbank has not attributed its hike to AI — but its acknowledgement signals that global AI capex is now consequential enough to appear in national monetary assessments.

::section The ECB Wage Tracker: The Second-Round Test | firmer, not definitive
Negotiated wage growth excluding one-off payments is projected to average ~2.8% over the first three quarters of 2027, versus 2.6% for 2026. The headline tracker shows 2.8% in H1 2027 before declining to 2.4% in Q3 — though employee coverage falls to just 23.3% by then, limiting reliability.

The ECB has identified slightly firmer underlying wage pressures, not an accelerating wage-price spiral. But AI complicates the reading: productivity gains can raise real wages without raising unit labour costs, while wage moderation from weaker labour demand signals no efficiency gain. For the Great Split, the distinction is fundamental.

::patterns Two Forces Repricing European Capital | the reinforcing loop
# Monetary channel
Persistent inflation raises the probability of higher interest rates, lifting the discount rates applied to future corporate cash flows.
# Industrial channel
Higher energy costs raise the expenditure required to build and operate advanced computing, data infrastructure and automation.
# [Great Split] Uneven impact
Cash-rich, high-margin firms may keep financing AI; smaller and capital-intensive businesses may not — accelerating the divide between firms able to sustain transformation and those constrained by its cost.

::section Five Implications for Investors and Policymakers | what it means
**European duration risk is increasing** — distinguish higher inflation expectations from higher real rates and sovereign-risk premiums. **Industrial-AI deflation remains a thesis, not an outcome** — adoption statistics alone do not prove aggregate unit costs are falling. **The AI cycle is a two-directional monetary risk** — monitor data-centre financing, semiconductor orders and technology-credit spreads. **Wage dynamics will decide persistence** — the tracker shows no destabilising second-round effects yet. **Europe's financial architecture must sustain capital formation** through the rate cycle: the Savings and Investments Union, growth capital, grid investment and Industrial-AI financing are becoming decisive for competitiveness.

::section What to Watch Next | November 5 and beyond
The immediate test comes November 5, when the Bank of England publishes its decision and Monetary Policy Report; the current Bank Rate is 3.75%. Watch the BoE's view on energy inflation, the ECB's December decision with inflation at 4%, and the durability of AI capital formation. No new formal AI-specific neutral-rate estimate or framework change at the ECB, Fed, BoE, MAS or SNB was identified in today's verified developments.

::close
The AI Supercycle is changing the economics of productivity. Energy inflation is changing the economics of capital. ==The next market rotation will be determined by which businesses and economies can reconcile the two.==
