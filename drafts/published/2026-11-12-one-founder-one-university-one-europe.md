Europe's universities can give one-person AI companies what models alone cannot provide: a real problem, a place to prove the solution and a first customer.

A doctoral researcher who has studied battery degradation for three years may understand a commercially valuable problem better than any generalist founder. A railway technician may know which recurring faults keep trains out of service. AI gives both the ability to build and test a product before they can afford a software team. The university can supply what neither can assemble alone — data under controlled conditions, engineers to challenge assumptions, independent testing and a buyer with authority to commission a paid deployment.

::signal
- **China asks individuals** to build AI companies; Europe should ask universities to make that possible for anyone who knows a real problem.
- **AI lets specialists** build and test a product before they can afford to hire a software team.
- **A founder semester** should pair students, researchers and professionals with an industrial partner's defined problem and named buyer.
- **Europe already has infrastructure**: EuroHPC's AI Factories and the EU's Testing and Experimentation Facilities for real-world validation.
- **The EIT's 2026 call** commits €40 million to innovation and entrepreneurship capacity in manufacturing and deep tech.
- **Independent proof shortens** the sales cycle and gives small suppliers access to customers that favour established vendors.

::section Redesign Entrepreneurship Around The Individual
A one-person AI-native company is built around one accountable founder using AI to perform work that once required several functions — developing software, documenting it, preparing sales materials and supporting early customers. The founder remains responsible for the product; hiring can follow demand.

That changes what universities should teach. An engineer should learn to interview a customer, identify who owns the budget, build a prototype, test it against an existing process and price it against the value it delivers. These skills belong in engineering, science, design and professional education as much as in business schools.

Universities should offer a founder semester open to students, researchers, alumni and working professionals. An industrial partner brings a defined problem, names the person who can buy a solution and specifies the evidence needed to justify a paid trial. Faculty assess the work; the buyer makes a separate commercial decision — protecting academic judgment while giving the founder a real customer to answer to.

::section Give One Founder The Capabilities Of An Institution
Universities can pool resources that would be expensive for a solo founder to buy early: a secure testing environment, specialist equipment, technical advisers and shared help with security, contracts and intellectual property. Europe already has infrastructure to draw on — EuroHPC's AI Factories connect supercomputing centres with universities, startups and industry, while the EU's Testing and Experimentation Facilities support validation in real-world environments. Universities should give founders a single point of entry.

The most valuable service may be independent proof. If an AI tool claims to reduce diagnostic time, the university can help design the test, specify the baseline and record failures, overrides and the cost of human review. That can shorten the sales cycle and give small suppliers access to customers that would otherwise favour established vendors.

Universities should also publish clear terms before projects begin — what a founder can commercialize, which data they may use, and when university-owned research requires a license. Technology transfer offices should be judged in part by how quickly they resolve those questions.

::section Organize The Universities As A European Network
A regional university can help a founder win a first customer. A network of universities can help the same founder reach a second market. A founder working with a technical university in Turin on a fault-detection system might win a first trial from a local manufacturer, have a German partner university arrange a test with a buyer using different equipment, and draw on a third institution to adapt the product to another sector.

The policy opportunity is to fund these partnerships around shared industrial problems and cross-border deployments. The European Institute of Innovation and Technology's 2026 higher education call already commits ==€40 million== to strengthening innovation and entrepreneurship capacity, with a focus on manufacturing and deep tech. An initial program could focus on machine maintenance, energy management and freight logistics, where customers can define the cost of the problem and verify the result.

Funding should reward the steps that establish viability: a paid trial, a production contract, a second customer in another member state. Universities should also track founders whose projects stop — an engineer who returns to industry able to deploy AI still carries value into the economy.

::patterns What This Means For Capital Markets | who watches what
# Venture and growth investors
The economics of the second installation. If deployment gets faster and requires less custom work, revenue can grow without a proportional increase in delivery costs.
# Banks and other lenders
Signed production orders, clear acceptance criteria and credible payment terms — supporting working-capital financing once delivery risk is understood.
# Listed industrials and their investors
Whether independently tested tools improve throughput, uptime or energy efficiency across multiple sites, after implementation and oversight costs — the evidence that can move earnings and returns on invested capital.
# University boards and policymakers
Time to the first paid trial, conversion to production and repeat sales across borders — showing whether academic support is creating companies customers will sustain.

::section The Fundamental Question
Can a European university help one person turn what they know into something industry will buy across borders? If universities connect specialists to real problems, provide evidence buyers can trust and use their networks to open a second market, a founder's prospects will depend less on where they study or whom they already know.

::close
The first customer proves that one person can build a company. The ==second, across a border==, proves that Europe can help it grow.
