The most consequential development in the Global Velocity Index is the widening divergence between measurable productivity growth and the financial capacity to sustain technological investment.

The emerging regime combines stronger evidence of productivity with a more demanding price of capital. Progress may keep accelerating even as valuations decline and financing becomes more selective. For investors, the next opportunity lies in distinguishing companies that convert productivity into internally financed growth from those whose expansion depends on increasingly expensive external capital.

::signal
- **US productivity accelerated** to 3.6% annualised in Q3, with unit labour costs down 0.7%.
- **The Nasdaq fell 5.52%** on November 5, with semiconductors far worse and stress spreading into Asian markets.
- **SoftBank's five-year CDS** rose 22 basis points to 466, its highest level since October 2022.
- **The Bank of England raised** its policy rate to 4.00% on an 8–1 vote.
- **The regime now combines** stronger productivity with a higher cost of capital for long-duration investment.
- **Strong balance sheets** and realised productivity gains become the decisive competitive advantage.

::patterns The Four Developments That Matter | Read them together
# United States — the productivity dividend
Q3 nonfarm productivity rose 3.6% annualised, up from a revised 2.1%. Output increased 3.7% while hours worked rose only 0.1%; unit labour costs fell 0.7%, against +1.9% prior. Labour's share of output reached 53.0%, the lowest since the series began in 1947. The AI contribution cannot yet be isolated.
# Global markets — into the financing channel
The Nasdaq fell 5.52% and the S&P 500 4.36% on November 5, with semiconductors far worse, on concerns about AI-infrastructure profitability, financing and regulation after the US midterms. The sequence to watch: equity repricing → credit-risk premiums → higher financing hurdles → more selective capex. A broad cut in AI investment is not yet established.
# United Kingdom — the cost of capital rises
The Bank of England raised Bank Rate from 3.75% to 4.00%, voting 8–1, with one member preferring a larger rise. It expects inflation to approach 5% by mid-2027. Higher borrowing costs lift required returns on long-dated, capital-intensive projects.
# Germany — orders recover, unevenly
September manufacturing orders rose 4.4% after a revised 10.1% August contraction, led by automotive (+10.5%) and machinery (+8.3%). But the three-month trend grew just 0.2% quarter-on-quarter — recovering demand, not yet sustained acceleration.

::section The AI Infrastructure Financing Test | Execution and capital structure now decide returns
IREN reported delays to some Microsoft-related deliveries on November 5, without financial penalties. Its timetable for more than $4 billion in annualised run-rate revenue shifted from year-end toward January, possibly February, and it outlined roughly $15 billion in additional planned capital expenditure — a forward commitment, not completed investment. Viability now depends on deployment schedules, financing costs, utilisation and the timing of cash generation.

::section Second-Order Effects — The Next Financial Regime | Potential transmissions, not confirmed reallocations
Strong balance sheets may become a more valuable advantage. Infrastructure investment may concentrate toward developers with contracted demand, available electricity and credible utilisation. Capital may rotate toward companies showing realised productivity benefits. The decisive sequence remains: productivity → earnings → free cash flow → reinvestment → compounding returns — and, for capital-intensive businesses, returns must exceed the full cost of capital.

::takeaways Five Takeaways for Investors and Boards | Separate potential from realised value
- Investors — separate the productivity dividend from the AI valuation cycle.
- Capital allocators — monitor credit, not just equities; SoftBank's widening CDS flags financing as a transmission channel.
- Boards — test large programmes against deployment schedules, funding, cash-flow milestones and incremental ROIC.
- Executives — prioritise measurable productivity: output relative to the full resources required to produce it.
- Policymakers — preserve productive investment through inflation control, reliable energy and access to long-term capital.
