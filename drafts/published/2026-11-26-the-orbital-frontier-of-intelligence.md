The AI Supercycle is beginning to challenge the geographical boundaries of its own infrastructure. On November 25, the US Federal Communications Commission authorized SpaceX to deploy up to one million orbital computing satellites under its **Starmind** initiative — the first US authorization for a constellation explicitly designed to operate as space-based data centers.

The decision is a significant regulatory development, not proof of technical feasibility, completed financing or commercial viability. Yet its economic implications could be extraordinary: the geography of intelligence may become an independent source of competitive advantage.

::section SpaceX: From Connectivity to Orbital Computing
Starmind would extend SpaceX's integrated model — reusable rockets, satellite manufacturing, communications and launch services — toward computational infrastructure. The framework requires an initial operational satellite by November 2033, 100,000 satellites by 2035, half the constellation by 2038 and full deployment by 2040.

Vertical integration could capture efficiencies unavailable to competitors buying those services separately — but it creates substantial balance-sheet exposure across hardware, launch, power, thermal technology and eventual replacement. Authorization is only the beginning: the technological breakthrough is proving orbital computing can operate reliably; the financial one is proving it can earn sustainable returns on invested capital.

::section The Great Geographical Inversion
Terrestrial AI is increasingly constrained by electricity, grid connections, land, cooling and processor geopolitics. Orbit offers direct solar generation, radiative cooling and optical networks — but transforms rather than eliminates scarcity, introducing solar-array mass, launch access, radiator mass and debris risk.

Not every workload benefits from orbit. Training frontier models needs dense interconnection and favors terrestrial clusters. Processing Earth-observation data aboard satellites avoids transmitting raw information, so space-native scientific and defense applications could become viable earlier. The opportunity is the selective allocation of workloads, not wholesale relocation.

::pull Space does not eliminate the economics of scarcity. It transforms them.

::section The Cost of Intelligence Beyond Earth
Boston Consulting Group's August 2026 analysis estimates the twenty-year total cost of ownership of orbital computing at approximately **$660–750 million per megawatt**, versus **$230–300 million** for equivalent terrestrial capacity — a premium of roughly 2.5–3 times. Computing hardware accounts for about half of modeled orbital cost; launch about one-fifth, which is why cheaper launch alone cannot deliver parity.

Lighter radiators, longer satellite lifetimes, reliable inter-satellite networks and efficient processors matter more. Under a realistic improvement scenario, BCG estimates the premium could narrow toward 1.5 times within a decade. The governing metric should be the risk-adjusted cost of commercially delivered intelligence — not satellite counts or theoretical capacity.

::section The Space–AI Convergence
SpaceX is not alone. Google's **Project Suncatcher** prototype launched on October 1, 2026, and on October 30 Google reported generating an AI response from a data-center-class TPU aboard the satellite — an initial inference capability, not hyperscale operations. **Starcloud** has applied for an 88,000-satellite constellation, while **Blue Origin** has proposed Project Sunrise, involving 51,600 satellites.

The companies building the orbital economy may not capture most of its profits. Launch providers, component suppliers and communications firms earn from deployment; orbital computing operators must demonstrate profitable utilization of completed infrastructure — a familiar pattern from earlier industrial revolutions.

::section Europe and the Next Geography of Power
Europe already holds relevant capabilities — satellite engineering, optics, precision manufacturing, Earth observation and defense — through the ESA, Airbus, Thales Alenia Space, Leonardo and Arianespace. But individual capabilities are not an integrated commercial platform. I propose a **European Orbital Intelligence Initiative** built on four pillars: space-native AI applications, critical industrial technologies, an integrated industrial ecosystem and European growth capital — potentially supported by the emerging SIX–BME–Aquis growth-market architecture. The aim is superior economic returns, not matching SpaceX's scale.
