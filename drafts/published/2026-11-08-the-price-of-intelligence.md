The Great Split is becoming a monetary-policy problem. During November 3–6, central banks and international monetary authorities increasingly treated AI as a source of current demand, inflation and financial risk — not merely future productivity growth.

The underlying contradiction is now measurable: AI investment supports growth today, while its productivity benefits remain smaller, less evenly distributed and harder to verify. Tightening can suppress the investment needed for future productivity; over-accommodation can amplify infrastructure overinvestment and instability.

::signal
- **Central banks have shifted:** AI is now a current demand, inflation and financial-stability force, not only a future productivity story.
- **The Bank of England** raised Bank Rate 25bp to 4.00%, with firms reporting modest realised AI gains but far larger expectations ahead.
- **The Fed held** at 3.75–4.00%, and the tech correction alone has not justified easing while inflation and employment stay resilient.
- **The IMF warns** an AI slowdown now enters the monetary reaction function, offsetting an energy shock while adding inflationary pressure.
- **The BIS flags** overinvestment, complex debt and circular investment as financial-stability vulnerabilities in AI financing.
- **The decisive distinction** is between financing intelligence and monetising it — which determines whether valuations and disinflation hold.

::signals Where the policy signal is forming | five institutions, five readings
# Bank of England — the most important new evidence
The Bank raised Bank Rate 25bp to **4.00%**, with eight MPC members supporting and one preferring 50bp. UK inflation reached **3.5%** in September and is projected to approach **5%** in mid-2027. The accompanying productivity analysis is the real signal: surveyed firms estimate AI raised productivity by ~0.5% cumulatively over three years (≈0.1–0.2pp annually), yet expect **~0.9pp** of annual gains over the next three years — roughly half of it from *slower employment growth* rather than faster output. These are firms' expectations, not the Bank's central forecast.
signal: The Great Split may run between economies raising output and those raising measured productivity by cutting labour demand.

# Federal Reserve — correction, not pivot
The Fed held its target range at **3.75–4.00%** on October 28, with two policymakers preferring an increase. Hammack and Barkin favoured gradual further tightening; Daly indicated further increases would probably be necessary despite the tech-equity correction. October payrolls rose **165,000** and unemployment fell to **4.1%**, stronger than expected.
signal: An AI valuation correction is not, by itself, enough to justify easing while inflation is elevated and employment resilient.

# IMF — AI enters the reaction function
Georgieva warned a slowdown in AI-driven growth would become a key consideration for central banks addressing persistent inflation, noting AI investment is offsetting the Middle East energy shock while adding inflationary and financial-stability pressure. The IMF's adverse scenario: an AI reversal could cut global GDP **1.1%** by 2028, including **1.8%** in the US.
signal: AI capital formation is simultaneously an inflationary demand impulse and a source of future resilience.

# BIS — financing becomes a stability concern
In *Navigating Financial Stability in the Age of AI*, Tombini identified three vulnerabilities: potential overinvestment, increasingly complex debt structures and circular investment relationships, alongside cybersecurity, concentration and governance risks. With hyperscalers, utilities, banks and non-banks interconnected, a reassessment of AI returns could propagate through credit markets.
signal: The question is shifting from whether AI is valuable to whether its financing architecture can withstand a repricing.

# ECB and Singapore — two approaches
The ECB held its deposit rate at **2.50%** on October 29, its reaction function centred on inflation, not an assumed productivity dividend; Cipollone outlined AI-enabled payments in the digital euro programme. On November 3, Singapore's MAS launched its Future of Finance Institute, pairing practical AI adoption with stronger supervision. The SNB's Schlegel stressed exchange-rate flexibility within a 0–2% range.
signal: Singapore's model — productivity diffusion and supervision pursued together — is the one most relevant to Europe.

::section The next monetary inflection | three forces, one hard question
Central banks increasingly confront three distinct AI forces: a **demand effect** (capital, equipment, electricity, infrastructure and specialised labour); a **productivity effect** (higher output per worker, eventually lower unit costs); and a **financial effect** (concentration, leverage and valuations amplifying instability when expected returns change). The difficulty is judging which dominates at any moment.

This also complicates r-star. Sustained AI investment could raise equilibrium real rates; a reversal could lower them. No verified announcement this week set a new AI-adjusted neutral rate — but recognition is growing that conventional measurement may become less reliable during a major technological transition.

::section Implications for investors and Europe | what to watch
For investors, the principal distinction is now between **AI infrastructure demand and realised productivity**. Analysis should separate committed capex, realised revenues, incremental free cash flow and actual changes in output per worker. For European policymakers, the priority is to accelerate *productive deployment* rather than subsidise construction independent of demonstrated demand. Three items deserve attention: the US inflation release and subsequent Fed communications, the UK's revised productivity statistics on **November 17**, and evidence of European AI productivity beyond the digital sector.

::pull The AI Supercycle has entered a more demanding phase: its capital requirements are visible, its inflationary effects relevant, its financial vulnerabilities supervised — but its economy-wide dividend remains uncertain.

::close This creates a fundamental distinction between ==financing intelligence and monetising intelligence== — the next monetary divide will separate economies that turn AI investment into productivity from those that only finance the buildout.
