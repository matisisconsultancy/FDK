What if America continues to dominate the technological frontier of AI, while China becomes the first economy to industrialize the global export of intelligence?

Three consequential developments on November 26–27 materially strengthen our governing thesis. China is formally incorporating AI services into its national export strategy, accelerating domestic computing infrastructure and expanding industrial AI deployment across its manufacturing ecosystem. The emerging competitive challenge is no longer confined to DeepSeek or frontier-model rankings — it is becoming a coordinated industrial and commercial strategy.

::signal
- **China is industrializing intelligence exports**, incorporating AI services into its 15th Five-Year Plan for Trade in Services, published November 26.
- **Guangdong unveiled computing infrastructure targets through 2030**, with energy-efficiency, renewable-electricity and domestic semiconductor objectives.
- **Over 1,000 industrial AI applications** were developed across roughly 20 sectors in 2026, with 100+ AI agents selected for broader adoption.
- **Anthropic's November 25 disclosure** shows America also improving deployment economics: −70% CPU, −80%+ memory, −20% first-token latency.
- **The contest is between two systems** maximizing the economic productivity of intelligence, not capability versus efficiency.
- **We propose a new metric**: the Intelligence Export Intensity Ratio, tracking AI capabilities converted into monetizable services.

::section From Made In China To Intelligence From China | The big take
On November 26, China's Ministry of Commerce and seven other departments published the 15th Five-Year Development Plan for Trade in Services, with a dedicated section on AI-related service exports — emphasizing the international expansion of Chinese cloud computing, AI models and digitally delivered services.

Its priorities encompass international AI commercialization, hardware–software integration, industrial applications, cross-border data flows and greater participation in international standards. China is seeking to replicate elements of its manufacturing export model in the emerging global market for intelligence — exporting not simply physical products, but the intelligence embedded in products, services and industrial processes.

::section Building The Capacity To Export Intelligence | The infrastructure
On November 26, Guangdong unveiled its computing infrastructure development plan through 2030 — official planning targets, not installed capacity. The plan establishes ambitious energy-efficiency and renewable-electricity objectives for data centers, alongside greater domestic semiconductor procurement.

This reinforces the Token Economy thesis: computing infrastructure is increasingly developed not only to support domestic AI consumption, but to enable the international delivery of intelligence services. The unresolved financial question is whether installed capacity can generate competitive revenues and sustainable returns on invested capital.

::section China's Deployment Advantage | Industrial AI
::stats
1,000+ :: Industrial AI applications developed in 2026
20 :: Industrial sectors covered
100+ :: AI agents selected for adoption
30+ :: National AI pilot bases

Government-reported deployment figures; not independently audited measures of productivity or profitability. China's strategic opportunity lies not in making AI models cheaper, but in embedding those models into manufacturing processes, logistics, industrial automation and commercial services. The next economic frontier is the diffusion of intelligence through productive assets.

::patterns The Intelligence Export Flywheel | Conditional on adoption, infrastructure and returns
# Domestic foundation
Domestic computing and energy infrastructure yields more efficient models and lower deployment costs.
# Industrial integration
Integration and productivity gains support the global distribution of AI services and tokens.
# Reinvestment
Export revenues fund reinvestment in innovation — potentially a global intelligence export economy.
# The constraints
International regulatory barriers, semiconductor constraints, cybersecurity concerns and weak commercialization could limit the flywheel.

::section The Productivity Race Is Not One-Sided | America's response
Anthropic's November 25 engineering disclosure provides an important counterpoint: migrating its Messages API infrastructure from Python to Rust produced significant operating-efficiency improvements.

::stats
−70% :: CPU per request
−80%+ :: Memory usage
−20% :: First-token latency

These are company-reported software-infrastructure improvements, not equivalent reductions in total model inference costs. American frontier developers are also improving deployment economics. China's efficiency challenge is real, but cannot be assumed to create a permanent cost advantage. The next contest is between two competing systems attempting to maximize the economic productivity of intelligence.

::pull An increasingly important distinction will be between countries that purchase AI services and those that develop internationally competitive industries around their production and commercialization.

::section Where Capital May Rotate | Second-order effects
American frontier developers face continued advantage but rising pressure to demonstrate operating efficiency. Chinese cloud and AI platforms gain international opportunity, balanced by regulatory and computing constraints. Semiconductor demand persists but differentiates by manufacturing economics. Energy and electrical infrastructure grows in importance in determining the total cost of intelligence. European manufacturers face stronger competition from AI-enabled Chinese production. The deeper implication is a shift from valuing intelligence production toward valuing intelligence-enabled economic output.

::section The Next Velocity Metric | Intelligence Export Intensity
We propose incorporating an additional measure into the Global Velocity Index: the **Intelligence Export Intensity Ratio**, defined as identifiable cross-border AI revenues as a share of GDP, times 100 — tracking an economy's ability to convert AI capabilities into internationally monetizable services. The distinction is central: possessing advanced AI is not the same as exporting it profitably.

::takeaways Five takeaways for investors, boards and policymakers | What to watch
- The geography of AI value creation is expanding — examine enterprise distribution, recurring revenues and industrial adoption alongside frontier rankings.
- Chinese industrial AI deserves closer attention — evaluate whether AI-enabled competitors produce durable cost and productivity advantages.
- Computing infrastructure is becoming strategic export capacity — assess electricity, connectivity, semiconductors and trade policy as interconnected.
- Lower inference costs may expand demand faster than they compress revenues — outcomes depend on elasticity, differentiation and sustainable margins.
- Europe faces a strategic choice — remain a purchaser of foreign intelligence, or build competitive industrial AI, sovereign infrastructure and scalable applications.

::close The first phase of the AI Supercycle rewarded those who built intelligence. The next will reward those who ==industrialize its economic value== — the great capital rotation will follow the systems that turn intelligence into globally scalable earnings.
