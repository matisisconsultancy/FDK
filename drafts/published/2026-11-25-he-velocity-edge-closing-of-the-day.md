The world economy is showing renewed strength, but the cost of financing its next phase of expansion is becoming a formidable obstacle. November 25 brought that contradiction into focus: U.S. third-quarter GDP growth was revised up to 4.3%, while core PCE inflation reached 3.3%, even as Washington proposed new tariffs affecting 14 major trading economies.

Investors responded by selling technology and semiconductor shares and turning toward sovereign bonds. The underlying collision — unprecedented AI investment, persistent inflation and a fragmenting trading system — may reshape markets well beyond the current cycle.

::section The Hidden Pattern | AI, resources and repricing
The most important connection is between AI investment and the resources required to support it. Greater computing capacity demands more energy and infrastructure; building it requires capital. When inflation, tariffs and geopolitical uncertainty raise costs, financing becomes more demanding.

Artificial intelligence could ultimately reduce costs through higher productivity — even while constructing the systems to deploy it contributes to near-term inflation. That difference in timing is becoming an important source of financial risk.

::section The Direction Of Global Capital | who captures the value
Demand for AI remains substantial; the question is who captures its value. Dell is revealing: an AI server backlog of **$132.3B**, quarterly AI orders of **$60.2B** and AI revenue of **$22B** — yet its shares fell roughly 7%. A growing market for computing infrastructure does not automatically lift the valuation of every participant.

Investors are now attentive to financing requirements, operating margins and sustainable cash generation. Expect a more regional investment structure, a greater emphasis on balance-sheet strength, and a stronger case for productive infrastructure — grids, industrial automation, defense production and intelligent mobility — where investment yields demonstrable productivity gains.

Europe has an alternative to competing on the scale of computing investment alone. Its manufacturing, energy networks and high-speed rail provide physical infrastructure that Industrial AI could make considerably more productive. Its most compelling advantage may be applying intelligence across its productive economy rather than matching the United States dollar for dollar at the frontier.

::section The Next Inflection | catalysts for November 26–27
With U.S. markets closed Thursday and shortened Friday, international developments may have outsized influence: Asian semiconductor trading, the ECB's policy accounts, German consumer confidence, Tokyo inflation and a French sovereign-rating review — with the OAT–Bund spread near 150 basis points.

The immediate priority is whether equity weakness begins affecting corporate credit. Falling government-bond yields may support valuations, but can also signal rising concern about future activity. Credit spreads, semiconductor shares, oil and French bonds will show which reading is winning.

::takeaways The Velocity Insight | implications for capital allocation
- Place greater weight on cash-flow durability, refinancing capacity and pricing power.
- Corporate boards must reassess large investments against more demanding financing assumptions.
- Industrial executives should prioritize AI applications that reduce energy, labor and input costs.
- Central banks must separate temporary construction-driven inflation from lasting productivity gains.
- European policymakers need stronger coordination of energy, capital markets and industrial strategy.

The technological potential of AI remains formidable; what has changed is the environment in which it must be realized. In a world becoming more fragmented and expensive, the advantage will belong not simply to the fastest innovators, but to those able to turn innovation into sustainable profitability.
