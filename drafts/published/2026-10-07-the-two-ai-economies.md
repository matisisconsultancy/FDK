America is building maximum capability. China is optimizing maximum diffusion.

The significance of recent developments is not simply that both countries are investing more in AI. It is that they are investing against different economic constraints. The U.S. constraint is increasingly *physical*: frontier intelligence requires more advanced chips, larger clusters, more power and unprecedented capital. The Chinese constraint is *technological*: restricted access to the most advanced U.S. compute forces China to extract more capability from domestic hardware while driving down the cost of deployment.

::pull FROM EVIDENCE TO THESIS
The United States is scaling the frontier. China is scaling the economics beneath it.

::section The Evidence | Two buildouts, two constraints
In the United States, the AI buildout is becoming an integrated capital-and-infrastructure system. SpaceX is reportedly seeking roughly **$40 billion** of financing to acquire Nvidia AI chips. Google has secured **3.59 GW** of U.S. power capacity through long-duration agreements with Constellation Energy. Major private-capital firms are assembling increasingly large pools of financing around data centers, accelerated computing and power infrastructure — while frontier U.S. models continue to define the top end of capability.

China is moving differently. DeepSeek is reportedly preparing a major financing round while expanding around Huawei Ascend infrastructure and a more independent domestic software stack. Chinese models continue to improve rapidly while competing aggressively on price, and industrial deployment is accelerating across manufacturing, logistics, commerce, mobility and infrastructure.

::patterns Two Production Systems | Different flywheels, different constraints
# The U.S. Flywheel
Better models → more compute → larger capital commitments → frontier capability → premium economic value. The constraint is physical, and the machine is extraordinarily capital intensive.

# The Chinese Flywheel
Lower-cost models → cheaper inference → more deployment → higher utilization → faster learning and industrial adoption. The constraint is technological, met by domestic substitution.

# The U.S. Production Model
Frontier models, frontier chips, hyperscale compute, dedicated power, deep private capital — the most powerful intelligence system ever assembled.

# The Chinese Production Model
Near-frontier models, domestic accelerators, lower inference costs, open software, rapid industrial deployment — intelligence made cheap and pervasive.

::signals The Core Tensions | Where the race is actually decided
# America: The Economics of Maximum Capability
The American advantage rests on four reinforcing layers: frontier models, accelerated computing, capital formation, and power. Together these create an unprecedented industrial machine for producing intelligence — but one that is extraordinarily capital intensive.
signal: Can America preserve enough pricing power to earn attractive returns on the capital required to build the frontier?

# China: The Economics of Maximum Diffusion
China's strategy is driven by three levers: cost compression (lower inference costs expand viable workloads), domestic substitution (Huawei Ascend and local software reduce U.S. dependence), and industrial deployment. The larger prize is not model-provider margins but productivity.
signal: China's bet is that AI creates more value as an input to the economy than as a premium product in its own right.

# The Profitability Equation
Lower prices do not necessarily mean lower total economic value. Cheaper inference drives more viable workloads, higher usage, deeper integration, higher productivity and a larger surplus — but that surplus can accrue to model providers, cloud and semiconductors, platforms, industrial companies, or consumers.
signal: Diffusion creates the surplus. It does not determine who captures it.

# The Second-Order Effect
The U.S. system is absorbing extraordinary capital across chips, memory, networking, data centers, power, private credit and infrastructure funds. If intelligence becomes cheaper faster than capital intensity declines, revenue can grow while returns on capital fall.
signal: The distinction that matters is profit per token versus economic value per token — the winners combine both.

::pull THE INFLECTION
China does not need to build the world's best model. It needs to make paying a large premium for the world's best model unnecessary across an expanding share of the economy.

::section Why It Matters for the Velocity Edge | The core thesis, updated
The core Velocity thesis is increasingly: **technological velocity × capital formation × diffusion velocity × value capture**. The United States currently dominates frontier technology and capital formation; China is increasingly challenging on unit economics and diffusion. Neither system wins simply by producing more intelligence — it must convert that intelligence into sustainable economic returns.

The AI Supercycle is broadening. Its first leg centered on models, GPUs and data centers. Its next leg extends into power, nuclear, grid infrastructure, memory, networking, industrial software, robotics, mobility, private credit and project finance. Cheaper inference can accelerate this cycle because it expands the number of profitable applications. ==The Supercycle can strengthen even as parts of the stack commoditize.==

::section The Investment Implication | A new question for capital
The investment question is no longer *who has the best AI?* It is: **who converts intelligence into the most free cash flow per dollar of capital employed?** That means separating AI adoption from AI monetization, AI volume from AI margins, and AI beneficiaries from AI value capturers.

The variables that increasingly matter are cost per token, usage elasticity, utilization, power cost, capital intensity, productivity gains, pricing power and free-cash-flow conversion. The most valuable businesses may not be those charging the highest price for intelligence — they may be those using increasingly cheap intelligence to generate the highest returns elsewhere in the economy.

::takeaways Key takeaways | The two AI economies
- The U.S. is maximizing the returns on frontier intelligence; China is maximizing the economic impact of lower-cost intelligence.
- The competitive question is shifting from who owns the frontier to who owns the economics of intelligence.
- Diffusion creates the surplus but does not determine who captures it — the winners combine capability, diffusion and capital efficiency.
- Both models can create enormous value; they distribute that value differently.
- The decisive measure of the next AI era: how much economic surplus does AI create — and who captures it?

::close The AI race is no longer about who builds the smartest machine. ==It is about who builds the most profitable economy around intelligence.==
