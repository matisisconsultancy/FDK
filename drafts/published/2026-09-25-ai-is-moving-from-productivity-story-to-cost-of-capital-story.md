The evidence is converging. The ECB reports that long-term yields rose to multi-decade highs while corporate debt issuance to finance AI investment surged, particularly in the U.S. The Bank of England says the policy risk begins when external energy and technology shocks feed into wages, pricing and inflation expectations. The SNB kept rates at 0%, with inflation still contained because domestic second-round effects remain limited.

The key question is now: **where does the AI shock enter the economy — and what does it become once inside?**

::section The ECB: AI CapEx Is Adding to Financing Demand | Cost of capital

The ECB does not attribute higher long-term yields solely to AI. Its more precise finding is that global bond yields have risen sharply while AI-related corporate borrowing — especially in the U.S. — has surged alongside heavy sovereign issuance. The broader rise in long-term yields has been driven largely by higher real term premia.

The AI channel is therefore: AI CapEx → more corporate financing demand → more debt supply into already heavy bond markets → greater pressure on the cost of capital.

The conclusion is not that AI mechanically raises yields. It is that the AI build-out is becoming one additional source of financing demand in a bond market already absorbing unusually large issuance volumes.

::pull INVESTOR SIGNAL
Duration remains vulnerable when AI financing demand, sovereign issuance and elevated term premia reinforce one another.

::section BoE: The Policy Trigger Is Second-Round Inflation | Reaction function

The Bank of England has sharpened its reaction function: direct shock → indirect effects → second-round effects. Higher energy and technology costs alone do not automatically require tighter policy. The risk emerges when they feed into wages, pricing and expectations.

The UK chain runs: global AI + energy shock → import costs → wages + pricing + expectations → persistent inflation → tighter policy.

::pull INVESTOR SIGNAL
Watch second-round inflation, not just the initial shock.

::section SNB: Same Shock, Weaker Domestic Pass-Through | Switzerland

The Swiss National Bank kept rates at 0%. Swiss inflation has risen, but imported pressure has generated weaker domestic effects.

The Swiss chain runs: global shock → import prices → limited wage + price response → contained inflation → policy hold. The BoE–SNB contrast is straightforward: the external shock is similar, but the domestic response is not.

::patterns The Great Split: Three Monetary Channels | Same technology, different outcomes

# U.S. — Capital Formation
AI enters through capital formation and financing demand. Result: greater exposure to higher market rates.

# UK — Imported Costs
AI-energy pressure enters through imported costs and stronger second-round effects. Result: inflation persistence and tightening risk.

# Switzerland — Weak Pass-Through
The same external shock generates weaker domestic effects. Result: contained inflation and policy hold.

::section Europe: The Policy Test | The real risk

Europe must do three things simultaneously: finance AI, reduce energy constraints, and shorten the CapEx-to-productivity lag. The real risk is not simply underinvestment. It is financing the AI build-out without converting enough of that capital into European productivity.

::takeaways Key takeaways | The Velocity Insight

- The central-bank question is no longer "Is AI inflationary or deflationary?" but ==where does AI enter the economy — and what does it become?==
- In the U.S., it becomes financing demand; in the UK, second-round inflation; in Switzerland, contained imported pressure.
- That is the Great Split — same technology, different entry channels, different rates.
- The next monetary regime depends on how quickly AI investment becomes productivity — and how much financing and inflation pressure accumulates before it does.

::close The next regime belongs to whoever turns ==AI investment into productivity== before the pressure accumulates.
