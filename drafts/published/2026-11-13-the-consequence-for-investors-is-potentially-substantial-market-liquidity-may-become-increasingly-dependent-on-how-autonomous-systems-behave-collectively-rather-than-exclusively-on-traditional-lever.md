Two consequential central-bank interventions reveal a new dimension of the AI Supercycle: the productivity revolution may deepen the divide between capital and labour before its benefits spread across the economy.

On November 12, Federal Reserve Governor **Michael Barr** warned that AI could produce substantial employment disruption and increasingly concentrate economic returns among the owners of technology and capital. A November 11 **Bank of England** speech provided detailed evidence of how financial institutions are already adopting agentic AI, and why supervisory frameworks must evolve.

The question is no longer simply whether AI raises productivity — it is who captures the resulting income, how that changes aggregate demand, and whether increasingly autonomous financial systems remain stable.

::signal
- **Governor Barr warned** on November 12 that AI could concentrate economic returns among owners of capital and disrupt employment.
- **US labour share** fell to 52.8% in Q2 2026, below the 60–65% postwar range, though the decline predates the AI boom.
- **A Bank of England–FCA survey** found ~80% of respondents using agentic AI, but only 2% of agents with broad autonomy.
- **Correlated AI behaviour** across independent institutions could amplify selling pressure and reduce liquidity, Jonathan Hall warned.
- **Fed Chair Kevin Warsh** addresses the Productivity and Jobs Task Force on November 16, the next institutional test.
- **No new AI-specific neutral-rate estimate** or policy-rule change was confirmed in today's review.

::section The Fed — The Great Split Becomes Measurable | November 12 · Governor Michael Barr, Yale University
The Federal Reserve's latest intervention introduces an important distributional dimension to the AI Supercycle.

::stats
52.8% :: US labour share of national income, Q2 2026, reported by Governor Barr
60–65% :: Historical postwar range

The declining labour share predates the current AI boom; the data do not establish that AI caused the decline.

Barr identifies two competing possibilities. Broad, inexpensive AI access could let smaller businesses raise productivity, expand output and create employment. Alternatively, concentrated ownership of computing infrastructure, frontier models and data could direct an increasing share of economic gains toward a small group of companies and shareholders.

The latter scenario would intensify the Great Split, particularly if AI substitutes for entry-level and knowledge-intensive employment before new opportunities emerge. Barr emphasised that large-scale displacement has not yet materialised and the outcome remains uncertain.

The hidden monetary consequence is important: higher productivity does not necessarily mean proportionately higher household purchasing power. If labour's income share contracts, consumer demand may weaken even while corporate profitability improves — complicating the relationship between productivity, inflation, employment and the policy rate.

::section The Bank of England — From Agentic Adoption to Systemic Risk | November 11 · Jonathan Hall, Financial Policy Committee
Jonathan Hall reported that a recent Bank of England–FCA survey found approximately 80% of respondents using some form of agentic AI, but only 2% of agents enjoying broad autonomy.

::stats
80% :: Survey respondents using some form of agentic AI
2% :: Agents with broad autonomy

The denominators differ, and these figures must not be interpreted as equivalent adoption rates.

The emerging supervisory challenge concerns correlated behaviour. AI agents deployed by otherwise independent institutions may react similarly to adverse conditions, potentially amplifying selling pressure and reducing liquidity. Hall identified access controls, identifiable trading agents, monitoring and system-wide stress analysis as safeguards, and highlighted **BIS Project Logos**, a collaboration with the Bank of England and Bundesbank studying AI portfolio managers in simulated markets. These are emerging supervisory concerns, not evidence that an AI-driven systemic event has occurred.

::section The Fed's Reaction Function — The Policy Debate Intensifies
On November 12, Cleveland Fed President **Beth Hammack** argued for gradual rate increases rather than aggressive tightening, despite persistent inflation. The same day, New York Fed President **John Williams** reiterated that further policy action would be required to restore inflation to 2%, without specifying the pace.

The distinction matters because AI investment currently supports growth and increases demand for capital, while the timing of productivity-led disinflation remains uncertain. A more consequential test is approaching: on November 16, Fed Chair **Kevin Warsh** addresses the Productivity and Jobs Task Force conference in San Francisco, examining how AI and other general-purpose technologies may alter productivity, employment and monetary-policy assessments. No new AI-specific neutral-rate estimate or policy-rule change has been confirmed today.

::section The Next Financial-Market Divide
The combined evidence identifies three transmission channels, which could reinforce one another — though magnitude and timing remain uncertain.

::patterns Three transmission channels
# Capital Allocation
AI investment → concentrated productive assets → potentially higher corporate returns.
# Income Distribution
Automation → shifting labour demand → different wage and consumption outcomes.
# Financial Stability
Agentic investment decisions → correlated positioning → possible liquidity amplification.

For investors, company selection must increasingly distinguish between productivity improvements that expand total output and those that primarily redistribute income from labour to capital. Concentration risk, competitive access to AI and liquidity resilience deserve greater attention. For EU policymakers, the challenge is broader than financing infrastructure: Europe needs competitive AI access for smaller enterprises, workforce adaptation, reliable energy, deeper capital markets and supervisory capabilities for increasingly autonomous systems.

::section The Final Take — Who Will Own the Productivity Dividend?
AI may raise the productive capacity of entire economies without distributing the gains proportionately across workers, companies and countries — altering consumption, corporate profitability, capital demand and ultimately monetary-policy transmission.

The Federal Reserve is beginning to articulate the distributional implications; the Bank of England is examining the financial consequences of autonomous decision-making. The next test is whether these insights begin to influence formal policy frameworks. For investors, the central question is shifting from how much productivity AI creates to how much becomes sustainable, investable cash flow. For Europe, the imperative is to prevent the AI productivity dividend from becoming an exclusively foreign-owned financial return.

::close
The first phase of the AI Supercycle concentrated capital. The next will determine whether productivity ==broadens prosperity== — or deepens the Great Split.
