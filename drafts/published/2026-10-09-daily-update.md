Intelligence is becoming more accessible; the capital required to industrialise it is becoming concentrated. The next Great Split will not be between those who possess AI and those who do not — it will be between those who can finance intelligence, convert it into cash flow and compound their advantage, and those who cannot.

Three developments define the regime. The ECB confirms credit is selective: euro-area corporate lending grew 4.4% annually in July, but larger and AI-related borrowers are better positioned, with no significant economy-wide crowding out yet identified. Australia's Nvidia-backed Firmus abandoned its $5 billion IPO after investors challenged valuation and financing assumptions, turning to private capital. And scrutiny of frontier AI intensifies — reported differences between OpenAI revenue measures underline the need to distinguish recognised revenue, run rates, contractual commitments and realised cash flow.

The FDK Global Velocity Index remains at **108.0**. No new national observation clears the materiality, persistence, additionality and confidence thresholds required for a score change.

::signals Five Signals Defining Global Velocity | The financial hierarchy hardens
# Smart Capital: The financial hierarchy hardens
The ECB's account reveals a differentiated market: large companies retain better access, smaller businesses depend on banks, and AI-related borrowers attract capital while higher-risk borrowers face constraints. Access to capital is becoming a determinant of access to intelligence.
signal: Access to capital is becoming a determinant of access to intelligence.

# United States: Resilience without cheap capital
Initial jobless claims fell to approximately 197,000 and August wholesale sales rose 1.8% — resilience, but not proof of faster structural productivity. Elevated long-duration borrowing costs raise required returns on data centres, power and compute. The financial advantage now rests on balance-sheet strength.
signal: Technological advantage endures; financial advantage now rests on balance sheets.

# Germany: Recovery meets external weakness
German exports fell 0.8% in August, including a 6.3% drop in shipments to the US, complicating stronger production evidence. Germany must convert engineering, automation and AI into competitiveness — not merely investment.
signal: Convert adoption into competitiveness, not just more investment.

# AI Infrastructure: Valuation meets underwriting
Firmus's withdrawn IPO shows investors may accept AI infrastructure's importance while rejecting its price. The underwriting questions — operational versus announced capacity, contracted demand, utilisation, time to free cash flow, residual value — are now unavoidable.
signal: AI ambition no longer guarantees access to public capital.

# Europe: The capital-market gap
Europe's strengths in research, engineering, power and industrial software meet an uneven ability to finance transformation. Thousands of industrial firms may lack scalable capital to deploy AI across operations.
signal: Europe's competitiveness challenge is now financial as much as technological.

::patterns Second-Order Effects | The evolution of financial power
# Concentration compounds
Strong balance sheets keep investing while weaker rivals postpone, dilute or sell. Cheaper models need not mean democratised ownership of the infrastructure that monetises them.

# Compounders vs. capital consumers
Cash-generative leaders reinvest internally; externally financed developers carry refinancing sensitivity. The test is whether cash flows arrive fast enough to support the capital structure.

# Rotation toward capture
Capability → compute → infrastructure → deployment → productivity → cash flow. The next reward goes to businesses embedding intelligence into real economic activity.

# Credit as early warning
Credit markets scrutinise contracted revenue, coverage and refinancing. Widening AI-related spreads could signal stress earlier than equities.

# Europe needs capital-market integration
If only large firms can finance transformation, the intra-European productivity gap widens. The answer is deeper long-term capital markets, not subsidies alone.
::

::takeaways Five Key Takeaways | From promise to returns
- **Investors:** own the balance-sheet advantage — self-financing capacity, contracted demand, strong cash conversion; treat repeated external funding as a material risk.
- **Boards:** impose financial discipline on AI, using the framework applied to acquisitions and industrial expansion.
- **CEOs:** accelerate the conversion cycle — time from AI investment to productivity, margin and free cash flow is decisive.
- **Policymakers:** democratise access to productive capital; smaller firms' ability to finance adoption may matter as much as access to models and compute.
- **Capital allocators:** enforce the test — *capital → capacity → utilisation → productivity → cash flow → ROIC > WACC.*
