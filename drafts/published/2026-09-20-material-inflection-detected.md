The most consequential new development is coming from China, and it materially sharpens the framework we have been building. Until now, the dominant central-bank problem has been a single sequence: AI capital formation drives electricity, infrastructure and credit demand, which builds inflation pressure and lifts neutral rates. China is showing the mirror image.

On 19 September, Huang Yiping, a member of the People's Bank of China's Monetary Policy Committee, warned that AI could actually worsen China's structural imbalance between strong supply and weak domestic demand. AI is already strengthening Chinese exports and productive capacity, but if consumption does not keep pace, faster automation and productivity could intensify excess supply rather than generate inflation.

That gives us a more powerful formulation of the Great Split: the same AI shock can be inflationary in capital-scarce economies and deflationary in demand-scarce economies. This is important because it means there may be no single global AI monetary regime.

::section The U.S.–China Monetary Divergence Is Becoming Structural | Two sequences, two central banks
In the United States, the dominant sequence increasingly looks like: AI investment → data centres → electricity → semiconductors → debt issuance → stronger capital demand → higher yields and higher R*.

In China, Huang is identifying a different sequence: AI adoption → productivity and manufacturing capacity → exports and supply expansion → weak domestic absorption → excess capacity → downward price pressure.

That is a major conceptual advance, and central banks would logically react very differently to each.

::section Today's PBOC Decision Fits That Pattern | The inverse of the Fed's problem
China kept its benchmark Loan Prime Rates unchanged on 20 September for the sixteenth consecutive month — the 1-year LPR at 3.00% and the 5-year LPR at 3.50%.

The decision reflects competing pressures. Domestic credit demand remains weak, but policymakers have limited room to ease aggressively because the Fed has moved in the opposite direction, the U.S.–China yield gap is close to historical extremes, bank margins remain constrained, and China appears to be moving from outright deflation toward mild inflation.

The PBOC therefore faces an unusual AI-era dilemma: too much easing risks currency and capital-market pressure, while too little easing leaves domestic demand too weak to absorb the productivity and capacity AI is creating. This is essentially the inverse of the Fed's problem. The Fed worries that AI investment demand is too strong; China increasingly worries that AI-enabled supply may be too strong relative to demand.

::signals A New General Theory Of AI And Inflation | Where the bottleneck lies decides the regime
# Regime 1 — Scarcity Economy
If the constraint is capital, electricity, GPUs, grids and construction, then AI drives an investment boom into scarcity, producing inflation and higher rates. This increasingly describes parts of the United States, UK and Europe.
signal: Where capital and power are the bottleneck, AI is inflationary.

# Regime 2 — Demand-Constrained Economy
If the constraint is consumption, credit demand and household confidence, then AI drives productivity into excess supply, producing lower prices and requiring easier monetary conditions. This increasingly describes China.
signal: Where demand is the bottleneck, AI is deflationary.

::section Europe Sits Dangerously Between The Two Regimes | The worst possible combination
An IMF paper presented to EU finance ministers this weekend estimates that AI could lift European productivity by roughly 1% over five years, but warns that uneven adoption could increase divergence across European economies and that energy infrastructure is becoming a major constraint. Around 60% of workers in advanced European economies are in occupations highly exposed to AI.

The IMF is reinforcing the same policy architecture now emerging from the ECB debate: AI policy cannot be separated from energy, capital markets, labour markets and the Single Market. The danger for Europe is that it could simultaneously suffer American-style scarcity inflation without achieving enough Chinese-style manufacturing productivity or American-style AI capital formation. That would be the worst combination.

::patterns The Investor Implication | A two-dimensional framework
# Where is the bottleneck?
Capital? Power? Compute? Labour? Demand? The answer determines whether AI reads as an inflationary or deflationary shock in a given economy.

# Where does the productivity surplus go?
Into higher margins, higher wages, lower prices, more investment — or excess capacity? This second question may become decisive. AI productivity does not automatically create corporate value; if AI accelerates supply faster than demand, productivity becomes deflationary competition.

::takeaways Key takeaways | The emerging global architecture
- United States: AI → capital scarcity → higher R*.
- China: AI → supply abundance → deflation risk.
- Europe: AI → infrastructure scarcity and slow diffusion → competitiveness risk.
- The new question is no longer whether AI raises productivity, but where AI creates scarcity and where it creates surplus.
- China is the first major economy where a central-bank policymaker is explicitly articulating the supply-side deflation risk.

::close AI does not create one global business cycle — it is beginning to create ==different monetary regimes inside the same global economy==. That is today's material inflection.
