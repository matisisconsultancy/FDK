Markets are still largely priced around an old assumption: shocks happen, policy responds, volatility fades, and equilibrium returns. That framework justified long duration, rewarded leverage and treated volatility as an interruption rather than a regime.

The next twenty-five years may require the opposite. The defining risk is not that the world becomes uninvestable. It is that multiple risk premia remain structurally elevated because shocks no longer arrive sequentially — and because the intervals between crises are disappearing.

::signal
- **The defining risk is lost recovery time** — each disruption increasingly arrives before the consequences of the previous one have cleared.
- **Markets still assume shocks mean-revert**, but the emerging regime may offer no equivalent reset or return to equilibrium.
- **2008 was a crisis to solve; the new regime is an environment to manage** across geopolitics, energy, sovereign debt and technology at once.
- **AI can be inflationary before it is deflationary**, raising the price of capital, energy and equipment before productivity scales.
- **The decisive variable is the spread** between return on productive capital and cost of capital, not the level of rates.
- **Dispersion widens**: the dominant approach moves from "buy the index" toward "price the difference", rewarding velocity of adaptation.

::section The Market Frame | from mean reversion to secular volatility
War affects energy; energy affects inflation; inflation affects rates; rates affect sovereigns; sovereigns affect banks; banks affect credit; credit affects investment; and investment determines whether the debt accumulated to absorb earlier shocks can be serviced.

AI adds a second loop — investment drives capital and energy demand, which raises hurdle rates, productivity expectations, valuations and credit conditions, which feed future AI investment. The next regime may therefore carry a higher term premium, a higher geopolitical premium, a higher energy-security premium, a higher sovereign-risk premium and a higher premium on corporate resilience — not permanently, but structurally more often.

The old sequence of **crisis → response → recovery → normalisation** gives way to **crisis → partial response → new crisis → further adaptation**. That is *Secular Volatility*: not permanent recession, but an environment where the speed of adaptation relative to the speed of disruption becomes the decisive variable.

::section Why This Is Different From 2008 | a crisis to solve versus an environment to manage
2008 was a crisis inside the financial system, with an identifiable chain and relatively clear tools: recapitalise banks, provide liquidity, cut rates, support demand. Today instability can originate simultaneously in geopolitics, energy, sovereign debt, technology, trade, labour, cybersecurity and physical infrastructure.

Central banks can create liquidity. They cannot create natural gas, grids, semiconductors, engineers or geopolitical stability. In 2008 the dominant risk became deflation; today a crisis can produce **weaker growth, higher inflation and financial instability at the same time**. And where governments once rescued private balance sheets, markets may now question the balance sheets of the governments doing the rescuing.

::section The AI Supercycle | inflationary before deflationary
AI is often called inherently deflationary. That is too simple. Before the gains appear at scale, the economy must build extremely capital- and energy-intensive infrastructure, creating three inflation channels.

**Capital formation:** AI capex competes for scarce semiconductors, memory, transformers, power equipment, construction labour, engineers, electricity and land. AI can be deflationary in what it produces while inflationary in what must be built to produce it.

**Energy:** compute is energy converted into computation. If supply responds slowly, adjustment occurs through price — higher power prices feed manufacturing, transport, households and potentially wages. Energy policy becomes monetary policy by another route.

**Financial demand:** hyperscalers, utilities, developers, chipmakers, governments and defence compete for the same long-duration capital, raising real rates, term premiums and hurdle rates across the economy.

The paradox: the investment required to create future deflation can generate current inflation — and the monetary response can delay it. Phase I (build) is inflationary and can last years; Phase II (deploy) is disinflationary but arrives later. The core risk is that **markets capitalise the productivity dividend before the economy has built the capacity to deliver it**.

::section From The Great Moderation To The Great Dispersion | price the difference
The useful frame is a matrix of two variables: how fast productivity arrives, and how restrictive the cost of capital becomes. High rates are not necessarily bearish if productivity rises fast enough; low rates are not necessarily bullish if they merely preserve capital misallocation.

The decisive spread is **return on productive capital minus cost of capital** — it determines whether the AI Supercycle becomes a durable productivity regime or another capital cycle ending in destruction. The consequence is not uniformly poor returns but wider dispersion: between countries, companies, currencies, energy producers and importers, AI leaders and laggards. The dominant approach shifts from *buy the index* toward *price the difference*.

::takeaways Five lessons, five imperatives | from 150 years of crisis
- Do not confuse the destruction of capital with the destruction of technology — AI can survive an AI valuation collapse.
- Leverage usually matters more than the narrative; debt maturity, collateral and refinancing determine who survives.
- Institutions determine whether shocks become depressions — credibility, fiscal capacity and liquidity are economic assets.
- Build balance-sheet resilience as offensive optionality: the strong buy assets, hire talent and take share during stress.
- Sovereign risk returns to the centre — the question shifts from *who will the government rescue?* to *who will finance the government doing the rescuing?*

::section The European Question | from efficiency to resilience
Europe's postwar model rested on four assumptions now all under review: cheap Russian energy, American security, Chinese demand, and globally integrated trade and capital. It is particularly exposed because it combines one currency and one policy rate with multiple sovereign balance sheets. It must move toward a resilience architecture — energy security, defence, deeper capital markets, industrial-AI deployment and selective technological sovereignty — without autarky. The race that matters is the velocity of disruption versus the velocity of adaptation.

::close
The Great Moderation rewarded efficiency. The new regime will reward resilience. The ultimate competitive advantage will be ==speed of adaptation== — and the response must arrive before the next crisis does.
