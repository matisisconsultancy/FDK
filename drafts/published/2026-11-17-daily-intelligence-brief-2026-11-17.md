The AI Supercycle is entering a new phase in which the right to access and commercialize computing capacity may become as financially consequential as the ability to build it.

The developments of November 16–17 reveal a striking contradiction. The United States is extending restrictions beyond the physical shipment of semiconductors toward remote computing services. At the same time, OpenAI has signed a six-year agreement for 457 megawatts of AMD-powered capacity, demonstrating that large infrastructure commitments continue despite market volatility.

The next Great Split may emerge between companies that own computing capacity and companies that can reliably monetize it across an increasingly fragmented geopolitical, financial and regulatory landscape.

::signal
- **US export controls now extend** into remote computing, adding licensing requirements for Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, effective November 19.
- **OpenAI signed** a six-year, 457 MW AMD-powered capacity agreement with TensorWave, with deployment beginning in the first half of 2027.
- **Institutional investors turned selective**: 138 funds exited data-centre equities in Q3 while 119 initiated positions; semiconductor exposure proved more resilient.
- **Flagship Pioneering committed £100 million** to an AI-native biotechnology laboratory in London, its first company-creation unit outside the United States.
- **Harvard researchers estimated** 403 US hyperscale data centres emitted 25.7 Mt CO₂ between May 2024 and April 2025, roughly triple 2018.
- **The new binding constraint is regulatory market access**: owning computing capacity no longer guarantees the right to serve customers.

::section Geopolitics: Export Controls Reach Into Remote Computing | A new regulatory architecture
On November 16, the US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security released an interim final rule on the use of advanced American semiconductors through overseas data centres, to prevent restricted companies from obtaining sensitive capabilities indirectly through third-country facilities.

A key threshold concerns access exceeding **12 million aggregate total processing performance units**, including linked facilities. Additional licensing requirements affect five countries: Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. The rule provides exceptions and is expected to become effective **November 19, 2026**. It is not a universal ban, nor does it require existing contracts to terminate.

The consequences depend on customer concentration and project structure. A developer whose revenues depend heavily on affected customers may face greater uncertainty over utilization, financing and valuation; projects serving permitted customers may remain largely unaffected. The geography of computing demand is now also a question of regulatory jurisdiction.

::section Accelerated Computing: OpenAI Signs for 457 MW | Physical expansion continues
On November 16, TensorWave announced a six-year agreement to supply OpenAI with 457 MW of dedicated computing IT capacity across multiple US locations, using AMD Instinct MI455X processors and the AMD Helios architecture. Capacity is expected to enter service in the first half of 2027, with full deployment by early 2028. TensorWave states it has secured more than 2 GW of long-term capacity across its portfolio.

The financial value has not been disclosed. The announcement reinforces the expansion of alternative accelerator ecosystems—but performance, software compatibility and operating costs will determine the ultimate economics.

::section Capital Formation: Selectivity Enters Portfolios | The Great Split reaches institutions
Reuters' November 16 analysis of nearly 6,400 SEC filings shows institutional holders across eight major data-centre-related companies declined from 3,411 to 3,392. Semiconductors differed: 2,951 funds increased positions while 2,584 reduced them. The positions were held on September 30, so they predate Nvidia's November leadership crisis and the latest export rules.

In contrast, Bloomberg reported that BlackRock raised the equity overweight in its model portfolios—managing more than $300 billion—from **1% to 3%** while broadening AI exposure. The market is not choosing between owning AI and abandoning it; it is reconsidering which exposures justify their valuations and financing risks.

::section Europe: Scientific Capital and Industrial Demand | Two distinct routes
Flagship Pioneering announced a **£100 million** initial commitment for an AI-native biotechnology laboratory in London—its first company-creation unit outside the United States—recruiting roughly 50 scientist-entrepreneurs, with ambitions to seek £500 million or more from partners. AI is being incorporated into the creation of new enterprises, not merely deployed to improve existing ones; commercial returns remain uncertain.

Italy's Prysmian, whose North America accounts for roughly 40% of revenue, said it would reconsider a potential US dual listing as AI-related demand for power and fibre cables expands its American business. European industrial companies can capture revenues from global AI infrastructure without owning computing assets—but parts of the valuation process may migrate toward deeper American markets.

::takeaways Five takeaways for investors, boards and policymakers | What the week establishes
- Computing market access has become a financial variable: infrastructure and financing do not guarantee unrestricted commercial use.
- The underlying investment cycle remains active: OpenAI's 457 MW agreement confirms continuing commitments even as valuations are reassessed.
- Institutional investors are differentiating among AI exposures—selectivity, not a verified generalized retreat.
- Europe's industrial AI opportunity is becoming concrete, via Flagship's biotechnology investment and Prysmian's global infrastructure exposure.
- The next leaders may combine technological capability with reliable commercial access; the measure is cash flow generated, not capacity announced.

::close The next stage of the AI Supercycle will increasingly distinguish between the ownership of technological capacity and the ==economic right and ability to monetize it==.
