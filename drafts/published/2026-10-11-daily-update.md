The AI Supercycle is accelerating technological production faster than it is distributing economic prosperity. The next Great Split will be determined by who captures the value—not simply who produces it.

Three developments provide the evidence: the United Nations has quantified the concentration of AI's economic rewards, Europe is confronting a strategic budget contradiction, and the industry is moving toward deeper vertical integration. Together they expose a pattern — ==technological diffusion is accelerating while economic capture is concentrating==.

The defining question is no longer *Who can build intelligence?* It is: *Who will own the productive assets, financial returns and competitive advantages generated by intelligence?*

::signals Five Signals Defining Global Velocity | AI accelerates trade while prosperity concentrates
# Global Economy: More Trade, Less Broad-Based Prosperity
UNCTAD forecasts global GDP growth of 2.6% in 2026, down from 2.9%, against real trade growth of ~4%. Developing economies grow 4.0%, China 4.5%, India 7.3%, with Asia contributing 59% of global growth.
signal: The distinction is between countries participating in AI supply chains and those capturing their highest-value activities.

# AI Economics: The Profit Concentration Signal
In UNCTAD's server-rack case study, post-tax profits account for 68% of traced value added, labour under 15%. Developed economies took ~70% of announced greenfield value in strategic sectors during 2020–2025.
signal: Moving into the supply chain is not enough — economies must move upward within it.

# United States: Vertical Integration Becomes the Next Frontier
Nvidia is reportedly exploring an acquisition of or investment in Reflection AI, a developer of open-weight models and software automation. Discussions are preliminary, but infrastructure suppliers may increasingly seek value higher in the stack.
signal: Vertical integration raises the importance of competition policy, interoperability and open standards.

# Europe: The Budget Debate Becomes a Competitiveness Test
The Irish presidency's October 10 proposal would cut the 2028–2034 budget by ~€141 billion in constant prices — an 8% reduction, including 13% to competitiveness and security versus 3% for agriculture and regional spending. It would still be ~30% larger than the current framework. Leaders discuss it October 15–16.
signal: Europe risks preserving established priorities while moving too slowly to finance emerging capabilities.

# Europe and China: Managed Interdependence Takes Shape
Following consultations on October 8–9, the EU and China reached a preliminary understanding on export access, rare-earth cooperation and Chinese EV exports. Implementation requires further approval.
signal: Europe is reconciling two aims — reducing strategic dependencies while preserving interdependence.

::patterns Second-Order Effects — The Next Great Split | Where the value accrues
# [68%] The Dividend May Be Captured Before It Is Distributed
When IP, infrastructure, distribution and capital are concentrated, the productivity dividend accrues disproportionately to asset owners. Creation does not equal distribution.

# Value Chains May Become More Hierarchical
AI may strengthen existing hierarchies rather than accelerate convergence: access to intelligence becomes widespread while ownership of its economics stays concentrated.

# Europe Faces a Capital-Allocation Paradox
The danger is less insufficient capital than the inability to redirect it. A common market without effective allocation risks becoming a collection of unequal industrial economies.

# Vertical Integration Could Redefine Competition
Chips → compute → models → software → applications → cash flow. Multi-stage control brings advantages but higher capital intensity and scrutiny — proven only through returns on invested capital.

# The Market May Distinguish Capture From Leadership
Capability → market access → pricing power → cash generation → capital compounding. Investors must separate input producers from final-benefit capturers.

::takeaways Five Key Takeaways | From production to ownership
- **Investors:** follow ownership of the productivity dividend — IP control, distribution, pricing power, free cash flow, capital intensity. Participation is not profit.
- **Boards:** measure economic capture, not adoption — judge AI through margins, asset turnover and returns on capital.
- **CEOs:** compete for higher positions in the value chain — own differentiated processes and recurring value.
- **Policymakers:** make broad-based productivity diffusion a strategic objective — domestic value creation, competition and access to productive capital.
- **European leaders:** treat October 15–16 as a competitiveness test — not how much Europe spends, but whether resources expand future productive capacity.

The next Great Split concerns who ultimately owns the productivity, profits and financial power generated by the AI Supercycle.
