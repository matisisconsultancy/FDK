The AI Supercycle is entering a consequential new phase: the capacity to produce intelligence is no longer sufficient. The ability to protect, finance and continuously operate that capacity is becoming a source of competitive advantage.

Three developments establish the shift: AI infrastructure has become a direct target of geopolitical conflict; demand for accelerated computing continues to expand; and the financing divide is widening. Together they move the center of gravity from construction to continuity.

Intelligence infrastructure is now a strategic asset whose value depends on energy security, operational continuity, geopolitical resilience and access to long-duration capital. The next advantage will belong to those who can **keep it operating under adverse conditions**.

::pull THE BIG TAKE
A facility that cannot operate cannot generate its expected economic output, regardless of the capital invested in it. The emerging framework must distinguish nominal capacity from usable capacity.

::signals Five Signals Defining Global Velocity | What the overnight evidence establishes
# Intelligence Infrastructure: reliability becomes economic capacity
On October 11, Yandex confirmed the shutdown of its Vladimir-region data centre following a drone attack; two other facilities were damaged in preceding days, including a site hosting two of the three supercomputers used to develop its AI model. The emerging measure: **commissioned capacity × availability × security = effective operating capacity** — a conceptual framework, not yet a calculated GVI indicator.
signal: Usable capacity, not nominal capacity, becomes the measure that matters.

# South Korea and Taiwan: semiconductor demand remains exceptionally strong
TSMC reported September revenue up 54.6% year-on-year and record Q3 sales, with nine-month revenue up 41.1%; analysts surveyed by Reuters expect Q3 net profit to rise approximately 65% to NT$746.3 billion—a forecast, not a result. On October 12, Taiwan reported monitoring Chinese live-fire exercises in coastal areas, with no production disruption established. Taiwan is not in the 15-country GVI universe, making its ecosystem an external indicator worth monitoring.
signal: Measured demand is strong; geopolitical disruption stays a scenario, not a score change.

# China: long-duration financing becomes a competitive instrument
Tencent announced on October 12 that it is considering a bond offering in US dollars and offshore renminbi, with maturities to 20 years for dollar securities and 30 years for offshore-yuan securities; no size has been established. China's national score is unchanged.
signal: Access to long-duration capital is emerging as an AI differentiator.

# Global Capital: the development financing divide becomes more pronounced
In fiscal 2026 the World Bank mobilised a record $112 billion in private capital, against $35 billion in fiscal 2022—but only approximately $3 billion reached low-income countries. With energy costs elevated and debt-service rising, investment risks concentrating in countries with the strongest financial institutions.
signal: Financial resilience may increasingly determine technological participation.

# Smart Capital: energy and sovereign debt remain the principal constraints
The IMF and World Bank meetings beginning this week in Bangkok convene against elevated energy costs, public debt and geopolitical uncertainty. Per Reuters, the IMF expects global public debt to exceed 100% of GDP before 2030. The transmission mechanism—**energy costs → inflation → financing costs → investment hurdles → deployment speed**—is plausible, but varies across countries.
signal: Sovereign debt and energy costs are the gating constraints on deployment speed.

::section Second-Order Effects — What Comes Next | Where the resilience shift leads
**A resilience premium emerges.** Valuation may separate infrastructure with strong operational redundancy from that exposed to concentrated physical, energy or geopolitical risks.

**Strategic infrastructure becomes part of national security.** The Yandex disruptions show how computational infrastructure can become strategically relevant in conflict, accelerating attention to data-centre distribution, grid protection and sovereign computing.

**Financial resilience reinforces concentration.** Firms with strong balance sheets and long-duration borrowing may better withstand shocks—**higher operating risk → higher financing requirements → lower investment flexibility**.

**Europe faces a new opportunity.** Strengths in power systems, electrical engineering, automation and grid technologies could become valuable as operators seek reliability—in securing and operating intelligence infrastructure as much as producing it.

**The next rotation incorporates reliability.** The sequence is evolving: **models → compute → power → infrastructure → resilience → productivity**.

::takeaways Key Takeaways | Introducing resilience into the discipline
- **Investors:** introduce resilience into AI valuation—availability, redundancy, energy exposure, contracts and free cash flow after maintenance.
- **Boards:** measure usable capacity, not installed capacity—uptime, recovery, utilisation and resilience-adjusted returns.
- **CEOs:** make operational continuity a strategic responsibility.
- **Policymakers:** treat intelligence infrastructure as strategically important—energy security, secure communications and reliable computing access.
- **Capital allocators:** expand the discipline—capital → secure capacity → reliable utilisation → productivity → cash flow → ROIC > WACC.
