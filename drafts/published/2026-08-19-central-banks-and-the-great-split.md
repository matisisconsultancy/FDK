What happens when central banks must conduct one monetary policy across an economy increasingly separating into two different capital, productivity and inflation regimes? The answer is beginning to emerge.

Over the past several days, the Federal Reserve, the ECB and other major institutions have begun treating AI less as a distant productivity possibility and increasingly as a current macroeconomic force — one capable of simultaneously affecting investment, electricity demand, productivity, employment, asset valuations, credit formation and potentially the neutral interest rate.

This matters because the AI Supercycle may be creating something conventional monetary frameworks were not designed to manage: ==one interest rate, two capital regimes==. The underlying structure is the Great Split. Its financial-market manifestation is becoming the Great Capital Divide.

::section The AI Investment Paradox | Two phases, one economy
The dominant narrative is too simple: AI raises productivity, lowers costs, lowers inflation, and therefore permits lower rates. The direction may prove correct. The sequence is incomplete.

Before AI can generate economy-wide productivity, the infrastructure capable of producing and applying intelligence must be built — data centers, accelerated computing, semiconductors, memory, electricity, grids, cooling, optical networks, construction, robotics, automation, and increasingly debt.

**Phase I — Build Intelligence:** AI capex drives capital demand, electricity demand and infrastructure bottlenecks, pushing real yields and potentially r* higher, producing investment inflation. **Phase II — Apply Intelligence:** industrial AI drives productivity, greater supply capacity, lower unit costs and structural disinflation. The central-bank challenge is determining which phase is dominant — and recognizing that different parts of the same economy may inhabit both simultaneously.

::signals The Signals | Where AI is entering the reaction function
# The Fed Is Moving AI Inside The Policy Debate
The July 28–29 FOMC meeting shows participants expecting AI investment eventually to raise productivity and potential output, while emphasizing uncertainty over timing and magnitude. More significantly, the discussion reached financial stability: AI infrastructure financing increasingly interacts with borrowing, nonbank finance and parts of the banking system.

As long as AI investment is funded through the free cash flow of enormously profitable technology companies, systemic leverage stays contained. Once financing expands into corporate bonds, private credit, project finance and banking exposure, AI moves closer to the traditional domain of central-bank financial stability.
signal: AI is no longer Technology → Productivity, but Technology → Capex → Credit → Financial Conditions → Monetary Policy.

# Europe's 9% Number Is More Important Than It Looks
Christine Lagarde's August 19 intervention noted euro-area companies expect to direct roughly 9% of total investment toward AI. The more important question is what happens after: Europe must convert AI investment into diffusion, industrial AI and productivity. If that conversion fails, Europe absorbs the costs of Phase I without capturing the dividend of Phase II.

ECB evidence reveals a self-reinforcing mechanism: a one-point rise in perceived domestic competitors investing in AI is associated with roughly a 0.6-point rise in a company's expected AI investment. But those effects remain heavily constrained by national borders.
signal: Europe's fragmentation reduces the propagation velocity of AI.

# The Supercycle Is Migrating From Equity Into Credit
The first phase was visible through equities — semiconductors, hyperscalers, memory, networking, utilities, data centers, robotics. The next phase runs through balance sheets. Hyperscaler gross bond issuance already exceeded $100 billion in 2025, with external financing expanding across corporate debt, private markets and project finance.

Equity absorbs uncertainty through valuation; debt transforms uncertainty into obligations. Central banks could confront two opposite risks at once: too much capital chasing returns above the Investability Frontier, and too little capital available below it — ==financial excess and capital exclusion at the same time==.
signal: Equity absorbs uncertainty through valuation. Debt transforms uncertainty into obligations.

# The ECB Already Has A €440 Billion Transmission Channel
Euro-area households hold roughly €440 billion of exposure to major U.S. technology equities, much of it through funds and ETFs, with insurers and pension funds also exposed. An AI-related correction would not stay a Silicon Valley event — it could propagate from U.S. valuations through European funds to household wealth, redemptions, asset sales, financial conditions and European demand.
signal: The AI financial system increasingly runs through nonbanks — funds, ETFs, private credit, insurers, pensions.

# The Great Split Is Becoming A Monetary Phenomenon
A central bank sets one interest rate, but that rate operates across companies with radically different returns on capital. A 4% policy rate can be deeply restrictive for a legacy company earning a 6% return, yet barely constrain an AI infrastructure investment expected to generate dramatically higher returns. Monetary transmission becomes asymmetric — and, paradoxically, tightening can sometimes widen the divide.
signal: The investable world compounds capital inward; the world below the frontier bleeds it out.

::section The Number Central Banks May Be Misreading: r* | The neutral rate under an AI boom
If the AI Supercycle increases expected productivity and returns, desired investment can rise faster than available savings, pushing the equilibrium real rate higher: higher expected productivity → higher expected returns → greater investment demand → higher demand for capital → higher r*.

The eventual productivity dividend could alter that equilibrium again. The regime may evolve through an AI capex boom and higher r*, then a productivity shock and a new r* regime. This is why investors should resist automatically equating AI with permanently lower interest rates.

::section The Employment Paradox | Slack that isn't slack
Productivity and unemployment can rise simultaneously. If AI lets companies produce more with fewer workers in particular occupations, employment weakness no longer automatically implies conventional slack. Cyclical unemployment is not technological displacement: the first can justify easing; the second requires skills, mobility, education, entrepreneurship and new capital.

Attempting to solve technological unemployment primarily through lower rates risks a loop — labour displacement → monetary easing → cheaper AI capital → faster automation → greater infrastructure demand → inflation. Central banks must identify the cause of unemployment before reacting to its level.

::patterns Five Hidden Patterns | The map beneath the map
# AI Is A Monetary Variable Before A Target
Central banks do not need an AI mandate, but they cannot understand inflation, investment or financial stability without understanding AI.

# From Valuations Into Balance Sheets
The Supercycle is moving from equity into credit — which makes credit increasingly important.

# Electricity Enters The Dashboard
Not because central banks should manage energy, but because energy scarcity increasingly determines whether AI capex produces inflation or productivity.

# Employment Is Harder To Interpret
Weak hiring can coexist with strong productivity and output.

# The Divide Can Become A Sovereign Divide
Technological underperformance can become capital outflow, weaker potential growth and ultimately higher sovereign risk premia.

::section The New Central-Bank Dashboard | Signals for the Next Economy
CPI, payrolls, GDP and wages remain important. But the Next Economy requires additional signals: AI Capex / Free Cash Flow (rising means greater external-financing dependence); AI Debt / EBITDA (whether expectations are becoming leverage); Electricity Demand Growth / Generation Growth (whether Phase I turns inflationary); Grid Investment / Data-Center Investment (infrastructure synchronization); Productivity Growth / Employment Growth (technological reallocation); Corporate WACC / Productivity Growth (movement relative to the frontier); and Sovereign Yield Spread / Productivity Differential (divergence migrating into sovereign finance).

::section The Governance Question | Specialization × synchronization
Because AI connects monetary policy with energy, infrastructure, employment, capital markets and industrial transformation, governments may conclude central banks require broader mandates. That would be the wrong conclusion. Independence becomes more important, not less, when transformation produces enormous distributional consequences — but independence cannot mean isolation.

The correct architecture is policy specialization times policy synchronization. Central banks retain price stability, financial stability, transmission, liquidity, payments and prudential supervision. Governments retain infrastructure, fiscal policy, energy, education, labour transition and social protection. The mandates remain separate; the causal system does not.

::section The European Consequence | On the fault line
Europe possesses extraordinary scientific capacity, industrial capability, engineering talent and accumulated savings. Its weakness is increasingly the velocity with which those assets become productive capital. The danger is not that capital abandons Europe, but something subtler: the marginal global euro, dollar, yen, franc or dirham finds a more compelling return elsewhere. That is enough — over a decade, incremental allocation compounds into structural divergence.

Europe therefore faces a different question: can it convert intelligence into productivity fast enough to remain above the Investability Frontier? That is not merely an industrial-policy question. Eventually it becomes an ECB question.

::takeaways Five Consequential Takeaways | Who must do what
- For central banks: the competitive advantage of the next generation of institutions will be regime recognition — identifying what kind of inflation, unemployment and capital formation they are actually observing.
- For investors: follow credit, electricity and productivity dispersion as closely as AI equities; the Supercycle is broadening into the entire capital structure.
- For CEOs and boards: AI adoption is a cost-of-capital issue, not an IT project — falling behind can raise WACC and reduce strategic optionality.
- For EU policymakers: Capital Markets Union, electricity infrastructure and the Single Market are increasingly components of Europe's AI — and indirectly monetary — architecture.
- For the ECB: the greatest future risk may be operating one interest rate across increasingly divergent Investability Regimes inside the same monetary union.

::close The central question is no longer how high rates should be, but ==what kind of economy this interest rate is actually acting upon== — and the institutions that recognize it first will best preserve stability while the Investability Frontier moves beneath them.
