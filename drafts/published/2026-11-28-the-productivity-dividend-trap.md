Why Europe risks becoming a major consumer of artificial intelligence without capturing a proportionate share of its economic and financial returns.

The latest ECB research, *AI, jobs and prices: what euro area consumers expect*, published on November 27, introduces another dimension to the Great Split. Europe is adopting artificial intelligence rapidly. Yet its investment trajectory remains substantially weaker than America's.

The emerging divergence is between the ==consumption of intelligence== and the ==ownership of its productive infrastructure==.

::signal
- **Europe is adopting AI rapidly** but its investment trajectory remains substantially weaker than America's.
- **US business investment** is projected to grow +40% cumulatively over 2021–2027 versus +12% in the euro area, a 28pp gap.
- **In August 2026, 44%** report weekly workplace AI use and 18% daily, with 56% reporting increased output.
- **Adoption, productivity and return on capital** do not necessarily move together.
- **Industrial AI disinflation** does not necessarily imply lower neutral interest rates.
- **Europe's success** must be measured by the productive capital and wealth adoption creates, not adoption alone.

::pull THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
Can Europe accelerate AI adoption while falling further behind in capital formation, technological sovereignty and wealth creation?

Three distinctions are becoming fundamental. AI adoption measures technological penetration. Productivity measures economic transformation. Return on capital measures financial success. The three do not necessarily move together.

::section THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT — THE DEPENDENCE LOOP | FDK conceptual framework — potential adverse scenario

An economy can successfully import productivity-enhancing technologies while allowing a disproportionate share of the associated intellectual property rents, infrastructure revenues and financial wealth to accumulate elsewhere.

This is the geopolitical dimension of the productivity dividend.

::section THE NEW MONETARY PARADOX | Three simultaneous forces

Central banks could increasingly confront three simultaneous forces. *Productivity acceleration:* AI raises output per worker and lowers unit production costs. *Employment insecurity:* automation encourages precautionary savings and potentially weakens consumption. *Capital scarcity:* AI infrastructure, sovereign borrowing, defence and energy investment sustain demand for long-term financing.

The result could be an economy experiencing stronger technological capabilities, weaker household confidence and structurally elevated real financing costs. Industrial AI disinflation does not necessarily imply lower neutral interest rates.

::takeaways Key Takeaways | Five constituencies, one imperative
- Equity investors: prioritise incremental ROIC and free-cash-flow conversion over AI expenditure.
- Credit investors: distinguish duration, spread and default risk as the financing cycle matures.
- Boards of directors: accelerate the transition from AI experimentation to measurable industrial productivity.
- Central bankers: assess the distribution of productivity gains between labour, consumers and capital.
- EU policymakers: integrate energy competitiveness, capital markets, technological sovereignty and Industrial AI deployment.

::section THE FINAL TAKE — FROM INTELLIGENCE TO WEALTH | 

Europe cannot measure its success in the AI Supercycle simply by how many companies or individuals adopt artificial intelligence. It must measure how much productive capital, economic value and sustainable wealth that adoption creates within its own economy.

Otherwise, technological acceleration could coexist with increasing strategic dependence. The next geopolitical hierarchy will be determined not only by access to intelligence, but by the capacity to convert intelligence into capital accumulation.
