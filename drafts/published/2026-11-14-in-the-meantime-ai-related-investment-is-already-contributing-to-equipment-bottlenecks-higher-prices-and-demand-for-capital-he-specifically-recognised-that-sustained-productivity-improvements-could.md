A consequential turning point occurred on November 13. The Federal Reserve's new Financial Stability Report establishes AI as the most frequently cited risk among surveyed financial-market contacts, while revealing the extraordinary scale and complexity of financing behind the data-centre investment boom.

On the same day, Fed Vice Chair Philip Jefferson examined how AI could raise the neutral interest rate, fuel near-term inflation and displace workers before delivering broad productivity. On November 14, Bank of England Chief Economist Huw Pill reinforced the prospect of continued tightening into 2027.

The AI Supercycle is no longer simply reshaping equity valuations. It is becoming an important determinant of **credit creation, financial stability and the equilibrium cost of capital**.

::signal
- **Fed elevates AI:** 87% of surveyed market contacts now cite AI as a financial-stability risk, up from 50%.
- **Financing scale:** over $680bn of advanced-computing debt was issued through October, with ~$1.1tn outstanding.
- **Jefferson's inflection:** higher productivity need not lower rates; AI may raise the neutral rate.
- **Inflation holds:** producer prices rose 6.5% year on year, with electronic components up 31.7%.
- **BoE's Pill:** UK rates may rise further into 2027, with inflation near 5% by mid-2027.
- **The next test is financial, not technological:** whether committed capital earns returns under a tougher regime.

::section The Fed's New Warning | AI moves to the top of the risk agenda
The share of Fed-surveyed market contacts citing AI as a financial-stability risk rose from 50% to 87% — not an 87% probability of crisis, but a dramatic rise in institutional risk assessments.

::stats
$680bn+ :: Advanced-computing-related debt issuance through October 2026
$1.1tn+ :: Estimated outstanding public debt linked to advanced computing
31% :: Share of 2026 nonfinancial IG corporate debt issuance tied to advanced computing
~$1tn :: Largest US banks' combined exposures to tech, data centres, financing vehicles and utilities

The Fed also identifies more than $1 trillion in future leases not yet commenced and over $2 trillion in other contractual commitments and guarantees among major technology companies — contingent and interconnected, and not comparable to $3 trillion in additional bank debt.

The critical revelation concerns circular financing: hyperscalers, semiconductor suppliers, infrastructure developers and financing vehicles are increasingly connected through leases, guarantees, equity investments and credit agreements. These relationships may distribute losses — or obscure the full exposure of individual companies. The Fed compares the cycle with the late-1990s telecommunications buildout, while stopping short of predicting collapse. Understanding AI balance-sheet exposure increasingly requires examining the entire financing network, not merely reported corporate debt.

::section The Monetary Inflection | Jefferson connects AI to the neutral rate
About 25% of US firms report AI usage in at least one business function, yet one survey cited in Jefferson's speech estimates AI raised sales per employee by only about 0.5% cumulatively over three years.

Higher productivity growth does not necessarily imply lower equilibrium interest rates. An economy in a productivity-driven investment boom may sustain stronger growth alongside higher real rates. Jefferson announced no revised neutral-rate estimate and stressed inflation remains the more important immediate guide. For investors, this challenges the assumption that AI-driven productivity will automatically restore the cheap-capital environment of the previous decade.

::section Inflation | The deflationary dividend is still ahead
Producer-price data released November 13 reinforce the immediate problem. The PPI rose 0.3% in October and 6.5% year on year; electronic components rose 31.7% and computer equipment 21.4%. These technology-price moves are consistent with AI capital formation but do not establish that AI caused the broader rate.

San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly signalled additional tightening was likely, citing persistent services inflation and firms' willingness to pass input costs on. Central banks cannot rely on future Industrial AI deflation to offset present inflation unless the gains become measurable.

::section Bank of England | Higher-for-longer extends into 2027
Huw Pill indicated rates would probably need to rise further in 2027, beyond a potential December increase, citing persistent energy inflation feeding into wages and pricing. UK Bank Rate stands at 4%, with inflation expected to approach 5% around mid-2027. Pill considered market expectations near 5% in 2027 not unreasonable — his assessment, not an MPC commitment. Financing conditions may tighten further even as infrastructure investment needs remain large.

::patterns The Great Split | The next differentiation in capital markets
# Financing capacity
Companies able to finance AI through operating cash flow versus those dependent on increasingly complex external borrowing.
# Input vs. savings
Sectors already paying higher prices for AI inputs versus industries beginning to realise measurable productivity savings.
# Sovereign resilience
Economies able to sustain investment under higher rates versus those constrained by expensive energy, weak productivity and fiscal vulnerability.

These divisions will increasingly shape credit spreads, valuations, returns and sovereign competitiveness. The Great Split is becoming not merely technological, but **financial**.

::section Implications for Investors and EU Policymakers
For institutional investors, the priorities are interconnected exposure across lenders and borrowers, future lease and guarantee commitments, cash-flow conversion from AI expenditure, and sensitivity to persistently higher real rates.

The ECB's November 12 research identified favourable financing conditions for European AI-exposed companies; the Fed report supplies the systemic-risk perspective — advantages can reverse if productivity and cash flows disappoint. Europe should prioritise productive industrial adoption, competitive energy and transparent financing over investment volume alone. No comparably consequential AI-specific decision was verified from the ECB, MAS, SNB or BIS in the November 13–14 review.

::pull The fundamental question is no longer whether AI can transform production — but whether the capital committed can generate sufficient returns under a structurally more demanding monetary regime.

For investors, the distinction is between economic value creation and financing-dependent expectations. For Europe, it is between financing an industrial productivity revolution and depending on the financial returns generated elsewhere.

::close
The first phase of the AI supercycle was defined by the scale of capital formation. The next will be defined by the ==resilience of the capital that financed it==.
