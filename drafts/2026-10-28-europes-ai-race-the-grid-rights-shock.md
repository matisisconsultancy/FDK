Germany’s 100 MW Industrial Conversion and Denmark’s New AI Capacity Reveal Where Infrastructure Capital Is Moving
THE BIG TAKE — Europe’s AI infrastructure race is entering a new phase. The decisive competitive advantage is increasingly the ability to secure electricity connections, convert existing industrial assets into computing infrastructure, and bring capacity into commercial operation.
Two consequential developments have emerged from Germany and the Nordics.
On October 28, Germany’s Horst Brandstätter Group, owner of Playmobil, confirmed plans to transform its former manufacturing site in Dietenhofen into a 100 MW data-center campus.
The project is particularly revealing because a neighboring municipality reports that another proposed AI development, associated with Anthropic, is being delayed by competing claims on available grid capacity.
Meanwhile, CoreWeave confirmed on October 27 that most of its planned 22.5 MW AI deployment at atNorth’s Copenhagen facility is operational.
Together, these developments expose the growing economic significance of grid access, industrial land conversion and the difference between announced and operational computing capacity.
1 | GERMANY — PLAYMOBIL’S 100 MW CONVERSION EXPOSES THE GRID BOTTLENECK
October 28 — New project details and competing grid-access claims.
The Horst Brandstätter Group has announced plans to transform its former Playmobil manufacturing facility in Dietenhofen, Bavaria, into a data-center campus.
The proposed development includes approximately 100 MW of power capacity, with potential for subsequent expansion.
The company plans to invest a nine-figure euro amount over the coming years and operate the development through a new division, Playmobil Digital.
The approximately 18-hectare industrial site benefits from existing infrastructure, including a 110 kV electricity connection. The company says the regional network operator, N-ERGIE, has committed grid capacity to its project.
The project remains in development. No final operating date, binding customer contracts or financial close has been disclosed. 
The more consequential development
A neighboring municipality reports that a proposed AI data-center project near Hirschneuses, which its mayor associates with Anthropic, has been placed on hold because the required grid connection is unavailable.
The mayor attributes the problem to electricity capacity reserved for the Playmobil development.
These claims have not been independently confirmed by N-ERGIE, and Anthropic has not publicly verified a binding investment commitment for the proposed facility.
Nevertheless, the reported conflict illustrates a fundamental challenge for European AI investment. 
Capital can be available. Customers can be interested. Land can be identified. Yet investment cannot proceed without deliverable electricity.
The emerging transmission mechanism is:
GRID CAPACITY RESERVATION → SITE CONTROL → DEVELOPMENT ADVANTAGE → CAPITAL ATTRACTION → COMPUTE DEPLOYMENT
This creates a new source of economic scarcity.
Why this matters
Europe possesses substantial industrial infrastructure that may be suitable for conversion into AI computing campuses.
Former manufacturing plants, power stations and industrial estates can offer established electricity infrastructure, transportation access and appropriately zoned land.
But the industrial conversion model also introduces a competitive tension.
Electricity capacity allocated to one computing project may constrain another.
This means the economic value of a proposed data center increasingly depends on the enforceability and timing of its power connection.
Investor implication: Grid rights and credible energization schedules should receive greater weight in infrastructure valuations.
CEO and board implication: AI infrastructure development must begin with electricity availability and permitting—not merely land acquisition or equipment procurement.Policy implication: Germany’s experience reinforces the need for transparent grid-capacity allocation and faster network reinforcement.
2 | NORDICS — COREWEAVE BRINGS NEW AI CAPACITY ONLINE
October 27 — Confirmed operational milestone.
CoreWeave has brought the majority of its planned 22.5 MW of IT capacity online at atNorth’s DEN01 data center near Copenhagen.
The facility uses NVIDIA Blackwell systems connected through Quantum-X800 InfiniBand.
This is CoreWeave’s first deployment in Denmark and increases its European footprint to nine data centers across five countries.
The facility also has an agreement to supply surplus heat to the local district-heating network.
Separately, CoreWeave announced an expansion of its international AI networking infrastructure, with additional points of presence planned from late 2026 through mid-2027.
The company has not disclosed the exact amount of Danish capacity already operational, the associated GPU count or the economics of the deployment. 
The hidden pattern
The Copenhagen milestone demonstrates the importance of distinguishing three stages of capital formation:
ANNOUNCED CAPACITY → CONTRACTED CAPACITY → OPERATIONAL COMPUTE
Each stage reduces a different form of execution uncertainty.
Announced capacity demonstrates ambition.
Contracted infrastructure provides greater visibility over implementation.
Operational compute establishes that physical infrastructure can support commercial service delivery.
The ability to translate operational capacity into sustainable returns still depends on utilization, customer contracts and financing costs.
The Nordic development also illustrates the potential for integrating large computing facilities into local energy systems.
Heat recovery can create additional economic and environmental benefits, although the commercial value depends on actual delivered heat volumes and contractual arrangements.
Strategic implication: Nordic competitiveness increasingly rests on combining available power, efficient infrastructure, regional connectivity and credible operating execution.
3 | EUROPEAN INDUSTRIAL SUPPLIERS — THE POWER-DISTRIBUTION OPPORTUNITY EXPANDS
October 28 — New product launch.
French electrical-infrastructure group Legrand introduced its Starline T5 DoubleBar power-distribution system for high-density data centers.
The company reports that the system supports up to 2,000 amperes and rack loads reaching 150 kW.
The product addresses the increasing electrical demands of AI computing infrastructure.
This is a commercial product announcement, not a confirmed Gigafactory supply contract. 
The broader significance is that European companies can capture economic value from AI infrastructure investment without owning GPUs or operating AI models.
Power distribution, cooling, energy management, electrical conversion and industrial engineering are increasingly important components of the AI capital-expenditure cycle.
For investors, this expands the potential opportunity across established European industrial suppliers.
For policymakers, it reinforces the importance of developing Europe’s position in the physical infrastructure supporting the AI Supercycle.

5 | FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS
INVESTORS — Grid access is becoming an infrastructure valuation variable.
The ability to secure electricity capacity and establish an executable development schedule may be more valuable than a larger announced project without firm power arrangements.
EUROPEAN BANKS — Underwrite deliverable capacity, not development ambition.
Grid agreements, construction milestones, equipment procurement and contracted customer demand should be evaluated together.
CEOs AND BOARDS — Industrial conversion creates strategic opportunities.
Existing industrial assets may offer advantages over greenfield developments, provided their electricity infrastructure can support advanced computing requirements.
INDUSTRIAL AI — Operational capacity matters more than announced megawatts.
CoreWeave’s Danish deployment provides evidence of actual infrastructure delivery. The next test is sustainable utilization and customer economics.
EU POLICYMAKERS — Electricity allocation is becoming industrial policy.
Europe must coordinate AI infrastructure investment with grid expansion, energy availability and the competitiveness of existing industrial users.
THE CLOSING VERDICT
Europe’s AI Gigafactory race is exposing a more fundamental transformation in the economics of infrastructure.
The next scarce asset may not be capital.
It may be the right to deploy capital where sufficient electricity is available.
Germany’s latest developments reveal the competitive consequences of constrained grid capacity.
Denmark demonstrates the economic distinction between promised infrastructure and operational computing.
European industrial suppliers are positioning themselves to capture growing demand for the equipment that connects the two.
The implication is consequential for capital formation.
The market may increasingly reward infrastructure projects according to their capacity to convert secured electricity into operational compute and ultimately into durable cash flow.
That is the progression investors, CEOs, boards and policymakers should monitor.
VELOCITY INSIGHT
In Europe’s AI race, capital may determine who can build. Grid access will increasingly determine who can build first.
