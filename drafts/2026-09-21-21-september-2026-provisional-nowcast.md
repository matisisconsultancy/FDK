China — 
+0.1: memory sovereignty crosses into production
This is the strongest overnight GVI signal.
CXMT’s fifth-generation DRAM platform is no longer a research roadmap. It is in mass production. The company says its process has reduced active-area half-pitch to 11.95 nanometres, uses quadruple patterning, and yields at least 50% more gross dies per wafer than the previous generation on a normalized basis. Its newly unveiled 24Gb LPDDR5X products are also in mass production. 
The significance is greater than mobile memory alone.
Memory is becoming one of the critical bottlenecks in the AI production system:
accelerated computing → HBM/DRAM → memory bandwidth → inference throughput → system economics.
China is therefore gradually broadening its semiconductor architecture from:
logic alternatives + domestic accelerators
toward:
accelerators + DRAM + advanced packaging + indigenous equipment + system integration.
The new CXMT platform does not eliminate China’s gap in HBM or leading-edge lithography. Nor should company-reported process parity be treated as independently verified industry equivalence.
But the evidence clears the GVI threshold because it is operating capacity, not prospective capex.
China therefore rises from 91.0 to 91.1, with contributions from Intelligence Infrastructure and Innovation Velocity.
Japan — SoftBank converts AI conviction into long-duration capitalSoftBank has launched $10 billion of dollar-denominated bonds plus €1 billion of euro-denominated senior unsecured notes to finance its OpenAI investment and general corporate purposes. The proceeds are intended in part to fund the third $10 billion tranche of SoftBank’s OpenAI follow-on investment, scheduled to close on October 1. 
The dollar bonds span 3½-, 5½- and 7½-year maturities, while the euro bonds run four and six years. Pricing is scheduled for September 24. 
This is strategically important because it shows the AI Supercycle moving deeper into conventional institutional capital markets:
equity conviction → bridge finance → unsecured bonds → longer-duration capital → frontier-AI ownership.
SoftBank’s previously disclosed OpenAI follow-on investment totals $30 billion, which would take its cumulative OpenAI investment to about $64.6 billion, subject to completion. 
But Japan receives no numerical change today.
Why? Because the bonds have been launched but not yet priced or settled, and the productive assets being financed are primarily outside Japan. The transaction strengthens Japan’s Smart Capital momentum, but not yet its domestic productive-capacity score.
Japan remains 84.9.
United States–China — AI governance enters the strategic architecture
US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng concluded talks in New York with the US side proposing a bilateral AI safety and national-security notification mechanism ahead of the Trump–Xi summit. The two sides also discussed establishing a formal US–China AI dialogue. 
This receives zero numerical GVI weight today.
The proposal is not yet an agreement, the Chinese response remains unclear, and the mechanism has not been implemented.
But conceptually it is important for Discovery Capacity.
The most advanced AI systems are becoming consequential enough that the two largest AI powers are beginning to consider the equivalent of an incident-notification architecture.
That suggests a new layer is forming above the technology stack:
models → agents → infrastructure → deployment → systemic risk → bilateral governance.
If implemented, such a mechanism could eventually increase the resilience component of Discovery Capacity for both countries.
For today: watch variable only.
Energy — real relief, but the structural constraint remains
Brent crude fell roughly 2.1% to $101.63 early Monday, while US crude fell to about $98.15. Saudi exports have recovered to just over 4 million barrels per day in September, compared with only 2.4 million bpd in August, according to Kpler data cited by Reuters. 
That is a meaningful improvement.
But it does not yet meet the GVI persistence threshold for reversing the energy penalty.
The East-West pipeline remains impaired, Hormuz-related risk persists, and analysts cited by Reuters estimate global oil and refined-product inventories may now have only 5–10 weeks of cushion, versus 15–20 weeks roughly two weeks earlier. 
So the distinction remains:
energy price relief = measured
but
energy-system normalisation = not yet demonstrated.
The Smart Capital drag therefore moderates but is not removed.
South Korea — energy resilience adds a secondary positive
A separate overnight development shows South Korea functioning as an increasingly important swing supplier to Europe.
South Korean jet-fuel exports to Europe have reached around 129,000 barrels per day in September, the highest since October 2022. July jet-fuel production reached an almost seven-year high, while refinery runs rose 16% month on month. 
This is not sufficient to change South Korea’s already high 88.4 GVI score, because the evidence relates more to refining resilience than directly to AI productivity.
But it reinforces a strategic advantage already embedded in the Korean score:
South Korea combines advanced semiconductors with a large, export-capable industrial and energy-processing base.
That combination matters increasingly as the AI economy becomes more physical.

That matters because the same infrastructure conditions affecting aviation, logistics and petrochemicals ultimately feed into inflation, monetary policy and industrial operating costs.

Institutional interpretation — 
From Compute Scarcity to Full-Stack Scarcity
The most important signal today is not simply China’s +0.1.
It is what the upgrade represents.
The first stage of the AI Supercycle was dominated by one scarcity:
accelerated computing.
That bottleneck is now propagating.
The emerging chain is:
Compute
→ Memory
→ Optical Bandwidth
→ Power
→ Grid Infrastructure
→ Capital
→ Governance.
Each link can constrain the productivity of the others.
China is attacking the memory layer.
The Netherlands controls a critical part of the lithography layer.
South Korea dominates significant portions of advanced memory.
The United States dominates frontier models, accelerated computing and hyperscale deployment.
Japan is increasingly mobilising institutional capital around frontier AI.
Europe’s vulnerability remains the connection between energy, grids and capital markets.
That is the new architecture of Velocity.
Main accelerator — 
vertical integration of intelligence
Competitive advantage is moving toward countries and companies capable of controlling several complementary layers at once.
The closer the integration between:
chips + memory + networking + energy + software + capital,
the faster learning, deployment and productivity can compound.
Main decelerator — 
the cost of connecting the stack
The technology itself keeps improving.
But the cost of making the entire system operational remains high:
**Treasury yields near 5%
oil above $100
grid congestion
memory scarcity
increasingly complex AI financing.** 
That means the marginal advantage shifts away from simply possessing advanced technology.
It shifts toward system orchestration.
Implications for investors
The investment opportunity is increasingly migrating from one dominant bottleneck to a portfolio of bottlenecks.
The strategically scarce layers now include:
advanced lithography, memory, optical networking, transformers, power generation, grid equipment, cooling and long-duration financing.
The strongest assets should be those positioned where structural scarcity meets high utilisation and contracted demand.
Implications for executives
The AI question should move from:
“Which model should we use?”
toward:
“Which parts of the intelligence stack must we control in order to protect our economics?”
For some companies the answer will be data.
For others power, specialised models, distribution, networking or proprietary workflows.
The critical capability is increasingly architectural control, not simply AI adoption.
Implications for policymakers
China’s CXMT milestone illustrates an important principle.
Technological sovereignty is not achieved by producing one flagship component.
It comes from systematically closing bottlenecks across an industrial chain.
The policy objective should therefore be:research → capital → manufacturing → energy → deployment → skills → reinvestment.
Countries capable of sustaining that loop will compound Velocity faster than those relying on isolated subsidies or individual technology champions.
Bottom line
The FDK Global Velocity Index remains 108.0 on 21 September 2026. China rises from 91.0 to 91.1. Global rankings do not change.
The larger message is more consequential.
The AI race is becoming a full-stack economic race.
Compute remains essential.
But compute without memory is constrained.
Memory without bandwidth is constrained.
Bandwidth without power is constrained.
Power without capital is constrained.
And increasingly, intelligence without governance may also become constrained.
That produces the next evolution of the FDK equation:
VELOCITY = Intelligence × Infrastructure × Capital × Absorption × System Integration
The competitive frontier is no longer simply who builds the best AI.
It is:
Who can make the entire intelligence system move faster — from silicon to capital to the physical economy.
