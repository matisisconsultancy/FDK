The Big Take | October 4, 2026

Alert: material inflection. The evidence over the past two weeks materially strengthens our core thesis: the United States still controls the AI capability frontier, but China is increasingly challenging the economics, infrastructure stack and speed of diffusion of that frontier. The competition is shifting from who has the best model to who can turn intelligence into the cheapest, most scalable and most industrially embedded factor of production.

The most consequential development is the DeepSeek–Huawei convergence announced September 30. DeepSeek is open-sourcing compute and communication libraries for Huawei Ascend processors and extending TileLang to Huawei’s Ascend 950 architecture, including systems built around 128-chip supernodes. This matters because the real strategic moat around Nvidia has never been silicon alone—it has been CUDA and the surrounding developer ecosystem. China is now explicitly attacking that software layer. 

Five signals now define the changing balance:

1. China is building a vertically integrated alternative AI stack. DeepSeek models + Huawei Ascend silicon + CANN + TileLang + open-source libraries increasingly constitute an indigenous model-to-chip pathway. Huawei also says Ascend is now supported as a PyTorch accelerator backend and is opening access to 10,000-NPU-scale compute resources. This does not mean China has displaced Nvidia. It means U.S. export controls are increasingly producing a second-order effect: accelerating the construction of a parallel Chinese compute ecosystem. 
2. The U.S. is responding to Chinese cost pressure by collapsing inference prices. OpenAI’s September 22 GPT-6 Sol and Luna releases cut API prices by roughly 50% versus their GPT-5.6 equivalents. GPT-6 Sol is now $2/$10 per million input/output tokens and Luna just $0.10/$0.50, while GPT-6 Astra remains OpenAI’s highest-capability model. The U.S. therefore retains the frontier while increasingly matching China on commoditized intelligence economics. 
3. China’s competitive advantage is moving from models to deployment density. Huawei says its Industry AI Foundry now supports more than 1,000 deployed projects, its Agentic Infra serves over 3,500 customers, and its newest AI Cluster Service delivers 20% more token throughput than its prior generation. These are company-reported figures, but the direction is important: China is attempting to industrialize AI through standardized infrastructure, agents and sector-specific platforms rather than waiting for model leadership. 
4. Industrial AI is becoming China’s transmission mechanism. Official Chinese reporting says more than 30% of large manufacturing enterprises were using AI by mid-2026, while China had passed 700 million generative-AI users by June. Huawei separately says Chinese inference consumption has reached roughly 500 trillion tokens per day. These figures point to something larger than consumer adoption: an economy increasingly organized around cheap, abundant inference. 
5. Washington increasingly recognizes that the competition has moved beyond chips. The Trump administration has created a new high-level AI task force under intelligence chief Jay Clayton, while Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has proposed an emergency U.S.–China AI notification mechanism following discussions with Chinese officials. The strategic debate is therefore shifting simultaneously toward frontier capability, infrastructure security and the systemic consequences of increasingly powerful open models. 

THE INFLECTION

The previous U.S.–China AI framework was straightforward:

United States = frontier models + Nvidia + hyperscale capital.
China = lower-cost models + diffusion.

That framework is no longer sufficient.

The emerging architecture is:

United States = frontier intelligence + accelerated computing leadership + rapidly falling inference cost.

China = increasingly capable models + domestic compute stack + extreme cost discipline + industrial-scale diffusion.The gap at the absolute frontier still matters. But the economic value of AI will not be determined only by the last few percentage points of benchmark performance.

It will increasingly be determined by:

intelligence per dollar × tokens deployed × physical-world penetration.

That is precisely where China is concentrating its challenge.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE VELOCITY EDGE

This strengthens one of our central Velocity propositions: the next phase of the AI race is not a model race. It is a systems race.

DeepSeek–Huawei is significant because it connects three layers that previously looked fragmented:

models → accelerated computing → industrial deployment.

If that stack becomes credible, China does not need to beat the United States at every layer.

It needs to make the complete stack good enough, cheaper and easier to deploy at enormous scale.

The investment implication is consequential. The decisive competitive variables are shifting toward inference economics, power availability, interconnect, memory, software ecosystems, industrial data and deployment velocity.

Nvidia’s technological leadership remains formidable, but the strategic question for investors is beginning to change from “Who has the fastest chip?” to “Who controls the lowest-cost production system for intelligence?”

THE AI SUPERCYCLE — SECOND-ORDER EFFECT

Falling inference prices do not weaken the AI infrastructure thesis.

They strengthen it.

Cheaper intelligence creates demand elasticity. If the cost of inference falls by 50%, enterprises do not simply spend 50% less. They deploy more agents, run longer contexts, automate more workflows and move AI deeper into physical systems.

That means the Supercycle evolves:

Phase I — Scarcity: GPUs, datacenters, power.

Phase II — Cost compression: models and inference become dramatically cheaper.

Phase III — Volume explosion: token consumption accelerates faster than unit cost declines.

Phase IV — Industrial AI: intelligence migrates from screens into factories, grids, logistics, transportation and robotics.

China is moving aggressively toward Phase IV.

The United States remains ahead in Phase I’s most valuable technological frontier.

The strategic contest is therefore becoming asymmetric:

AMERICA IS STILL DEFINING THE FRONTIER.

CHINA IS TRYING TO INDUSTRIALIZE IT FASTER.

And that may become the defining investment tension of the next stage of the AI Supercycle.

Velocity Insight

The Great Split in AI is no longer between American models and Chinese models. It is between two production systems for intelligence.

The U.S. is optimizing for maximum capability. China is optimizing for maximum diffusion.

The winner will be determined not simply by who builds the smartest model—but by who converts intelligence into the lowest-cost, most scalable factor of production.
