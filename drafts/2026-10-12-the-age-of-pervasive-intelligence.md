The Great Intelligence Divide
Apple, Meta, Agentic AI and the New Global Contest for Technological and Economic Power
The next technological revolution will not be defined by the devices we use. It will be defined by the intelligence embedded in everything around us—and by the competing economic systems seeking to control it.
IN FOCUS — THE END OF COMPUTING AS WE KNOW IT
On October 23, Apple will begin selling its first foldable iPhone, the iPhone Duo. Its 7.6-inch internal display, advanced processor and redesigned software experience represent Apple’s most ambitious attempt to consolidate mobile computing into a single versatile device.
On October 13, Meta will expand the commercial availability of its Ray-Ban Display AI glasses into France, Italy and Germany. Its strategy moves in a different direction: away from the conventional screen and toward intelligence accessed through vision, voice, gestures and the physical environment.
These developments are not merely two product launches.
They signal the beginning of a potentially fundamental transformation in the architecture of computing, the organization of digital services and the distribution of economic power.
Apple is making the personal device more capable.
Meta is attempting to make the device less central to the user experience.
Yet the larger revolution lies beyond both.
We are not simply entering the next generation of personal computing. We are entering the Age of Pervasive Intelligence.
In this emerging paradigm, intelligence is no longer confined to a smartphone, computer, application or screen.
It becomes an increasingly distributed capability embedded in devices, vehicles, factories, infrastructure, cities and everyday human activity.
And Agentic AI provides the decisive acceleration mechanism.
Artificial intelligence moves from answering questions to executing authorized tasks, coordinating services and eventually optimizing interconnected physical and digital systems.
The result could be a radical reorganization of the global economy.
Software will increasingly become autonomous.
Hardware will become more specialized and interconnected.
Consumer interfaces will become increasingly contextual.
Industrial systems will begin coordinating intelligence across entire value chains.
And the United States and China will intensify their competition to shape the infrastructure, standards and platforms through which pervasive intelligence is deployed globally.
This is the beginning of a new technological and geopolitical divide.
But the distinction is more consequential than a simple confrontation between two countries.
It concerns two competing ways of organizing intelligence, capturing economic value and exercising technological power.
The next Great Split will separate the companies and economies that control the architecture of intelligence from those that merely consume its output.
I. THE NEW TECHNOLOGICAL PARADIGM
From Personal Computing to AI-Native Pervasive Computing
Three technological regimes have defined the evolution of modern computing.
The first was centralized computing, with processing power concentrated in mainframes and enterprise systems.
The second was personal and mobile computing, which distributed computational capacity to individuals through personal computers, smartphones and connected devices.
The third is now emerging.
AI-Native Pervasive Computing combines distributed intelligence, continuous contextual awareness, agentic software and interoperable physical devices.
Its significance lies not simply in the availability of AI.
It lies in a new relationship between people, technology and economic activity.
In the mobile era, consumers learned how to operate increasingly sophisticated devices and applications.
In the pervasive-intelligence era, technology will increasingly learn how to interpret and support human objectives.
The operating sequence begins to change.Instead of identifying an application, navigating its interface and executing a task, users express an intention.
An authorized intelligent system interprets the objective, identifies relevant services, coordinates their execution and presents an outcome.
This is a fundamental change in the location of economic value.
The application becomes less visible.
The intelligent orchestrator becomes more important.
The user’s trusted relationship with the system becomes a strategic asset.
The competitive advantage shifts toward the ability to connect intention, context, decision-making and execution.
The defining transition is therefore:
From device-centric computing to intelligence-centric economic systems.
This is the underlying architecture of the Age of Pervasive Intelligence.
II. APPLE VS. META: THE FIRST COLLISION
The Intelligent Device Versus the Intelligent Environment
Apple: Concentration
One powerful, integrated computing environment.
Meta: Diffusion
Intelligence increasingly accessible through the surrounding environment.
Apple and Meta represent two different interface strategies.
Apple’s iPhone Duo embodies the logic of consolidation.
The device combines portability with a substantially larger working surface, advanced computational capabilities, personal context and an integrated software ecosystem.
Its strategic potential extends beyond premium smartphone sales.
It could reduce the need for separate tablets or lightweight computers in some consumer and professional activities.
The economic objective is to consolidate more digital activity within Apple’s controlled hardware, software, payments and services environment.
Meta follows a contrasting trajectory.
Its smart glasses seek to make intelligence accessible during ordinary physical activity, without requiring users repeatedly to retrieve a smartphone.
The opportunity lies in contextual assistance, translation, navigation, communication, image interpretation and eventually agentic services.
Meta’s Neural Band adds another potential interface: interpreting subtle muscular signals to control digital functions without conventional touch interaction.
The contrast is striking.
Apple wants to own the intelligent device. Meta wants to own the intelligent moment.
Yet the two models may coexist.
Consumers could use glasses for brief, frequent and contextual interactions, while relying on an expandable mobile device for complex work, extended reading, professional analysis and consequential decisions.
The resulting experience would have two complementary modes.
Ambient Intelligence would provide immediate assistance throughout the day.
Immersive Intelligence would provide the extended workspace required for complex reasoning, creation and execution.
The strategic contest will concern who controls the transition between these modes.
Who owns the user’s identity?
Who remembers preferences and context?
Who determines which AI agent receives an instruction?
Who authorizes transactions?
And who receives the economic value when the task is completed?
The answers will shape the next generation of consumer platforms.
But hardware is only the beginning.
III. THE AGENTIC AI ACCELERATION
From Intelligence That Responds to Intelligence That Executes
Agentic AI is the pivotal technological force that could transform pervasive intelligence from a collection of connected devices into a new economic operating system.
The distinction between conventional AI assistance and Agentic AI is fundamental.
Conventional AI primarily generates information, recommendations or content.
Agentic AI can plan a sequence of actions, call specialized tools, coordinate services, evaluate intermediate results and execute tasks within defined permissions.
It changes the economic function of intelligence.
An assistant can explain how to organize a journey.
An agentic system can compare transport options, verify schedules, assemble an itinerary and complete authorized bookings.
A conventional industrial analytics system can identify an equipment problem.An agentic industrial system can help initiate maintenance planning, verify spare-parts availability, coordinate approved interventions and update operational schedules.
The opportunity is not merely faster access to information.
It is the reduction of the coordination costs required to transform information into action.
The evidence already points toward acceleration
McKinsey’s 2026 global AI survey reports that 40% of respondents from large enterprises are scaling AI agents, compared with 27% the previous year.
However, only 37% of surveyed organizations report a positive EBIT impact from AI, and just 6% qualify as AI high performers under McKinsey’s methodology.
This distinction is decisive.
Adoption is accelerating faster than measured enterprise financial returns.
Gartner’s analysis of 107 agentic deployments also emphasizes the importance of specialization, forecasting that 80% of tangible agentic AI returns in 2028 will come from domain-specific agents rather than general-purpose systems.
The implication is that the next acceleration will not be driven solely by more powerful general models.
It will depend on how effectively specialized intelligence is deployed across real economic processes.
The four forces of Agentic Acceleration
THE FDK AGENTIC ACCELERATION LOOP
The reinforcing cycle is conditional on reliable execution and sustainable economic returns.
First: Interface friction declines. Smart glasses, voice interfaces and wearable sensors make intelligence accessible across more circumstances, increasing the frequency of useful interaction.
Second: The economic value of each interaction increases. AI systems begin executing multistep workflows instead of merely providing information.
Third: Specialized agents extend intelligence into the physical economy. Logistics, manufacturing, energy, mobility and infrastructure offer opportunities to apply agentic coordination to systems involving substantial installed capital.
Fourth: Successful deployment funds further diffusion. Lower costs, improved software and demonstrated productivity can support reinvestment and expansion into additional applications.
The resulting mechanism could become self-reinforcing.
More pervasive interfaces increase opportunities for agentic interaction.
More capable agents increase the usefulness of each interface.
Greater usefulness supports adoption.
Broader adoption creates incentives for specialized software, more efficient computing and additional investment.
But the flywheel is not automatic.
Failures, security vulnerabilities, expensive inference, organizational resistance and unreliable execution can interrupt it.
That is why the decisive competitive metric will increasingly be cost per successfully completed economic task.
The transition from linear automation to compound productivity
Traditional automation improves the efficiency of predefined operations.
Agentic AI creates the possibility of coordinating entire sequences of activity across multiple functions.
The most consequential gains may therefore emerge not from automating existing tasks individually but from redesigning workflows so that fewer sequential handoffs are required.
This could change the structure of organizations.
Management hierarchies evolved partly to coordinate information, decisions and execution across specialized teams.
As AI reduces some coordination costs, companies may be able to reorganize activities around smaller teams overseeing more extensive intelligent systems.
Human expertise will remain essential for objectives, accountability, judgment and exceptions.
But the relationship between organizational scale and human coordination requirements may change.
The productivity discontinuity occurs when AI stops improving individual tasks and begins redesigning the system connecting those tasks.
That is the economic significance of Agentic Acceleration.
IV. THE NEXT DISCONTINUITY
From Agentic AI to the Autonomous Enterprise
The transition from pervasive intelligence to increasingly autonomous execution could fundamentally change how corporations operate.Consider a multinational industrial organization.
Today, procurement, engineering, finance, maintenance, scheduling and logistics may function through separate software environments and management structures.
Each function optimizes its own objectives.
Coordination occurs through meetings, approvals, reports, forecasts and enterprise software workflows.
An agentic architecture can potentially connect these functions more directly.
A change in demand could trigger adjustments in production planning.
Those adjustments could inform procurement requirements.
Procurement changes could be evaluated against supplier availability, financing and logistics constraints.
Maintenance schedules could be coordinated with production requirements.
Managers would retain responsibility for objectives, risk limits, exceptions and authorization.
The potential advantage comes from reducing the time required for information to move across functions and become an operational decision.
This creates a new form of enterprise velocity.
The relevant metric is not simply employee productivity.
It is the rate at which an organization converts information into coordinated economic action.
Three financial consequences follow.
First, working-capital requirements may decline if companies improve inventory, scheduling and procurement coordination.
Second, asset utilization may rise as downtime and operational fragmentation are reduced.
Third, incremental growth may require less additional organizational complexity.
These effects could improve return on invested capital.
However, enterprise autonomy introduces significant new risks.
Agent errors can propagate across connected systems.
Poorly defined permissions can create operational and financial exposure.
Software failures in physical infrastructure can have safety consequences.
The winning organizations will therefore combine agentic capability with rigorous oversight, verification and system resilience.
The future autonomous enterprise will not be one without human responsibility.
It will be one in which human responsibility is supported by a substantially more capable execution architecture.
V. THE GREAT INTELLIGENCE DIVIDE
Two Global Architectures Compete to Control the Next Economy
The Apple–Meta confrontation is the consumer-facing expression of a much larger struggle.
The emerging geopolitical contest concerns who will control the technological foundations through which pervasive intelligence operates.
The United States and China are the principal centers of that competition.
Their rivalry increasingly extends across semiconductors, AI models, computing infrastructure, connected devices, industrial automation, energy systems and digital standards.
But the most consequential difference may concern how intelligence is organized and deployed.
Two strategic architectures are emerging.
Architecture One — Platform-Centric Intelligence
The American technology ecosystem possesses exceptional strengths in advanced computing, frontier AI research, operating systems, cloud infrastructure, enterprise software and global consumer platforms.
Companies including Nvidia, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Google and Amazon control strategically important layers of the technology stack.
Their business models differ, and they compete intensely.
Nevertheless, the broader ecosystem is particularly strong in concentrating economic value around high-performance computing, proprietary technologies, software distribution and integrated platforms.
Its ability to attract extraordinary quantities of capital reinforces that advantage.
The strategic logic is to control critical components of the intelligence infrastructure and monetize their use across a global customer base.
Architecture Two — Industrially Embedded Intelligence
China possesses different, increasingly complementary strengths.
Its manufacturing scale, electronics supply chains, electric-vehicle industry, robotics sector and connected-device production create opportunities for embedding AI into physical products and industrial systems.Its AI developers are also advancing open-weight models and alternative computing architectures, supporting deployment across domestic and international ecosystems.
The strategic logic is to distribute intelligent capabilities throughout manufactured products, infrastructure and industrial processes.
Competitive advantage can arise from manufacturing efficiency, affordable hardware, integrated supply chains and large-scale commercialization.
This produces a fundamental distinction.
The United States possesses a powerful advantage in controlling the upper layers of intelligence. China is particularly well positioned to accelerate its diffusion through the physical economy.
Neither distinction is absolute.
American companies are major investors in robotics, industrial AI and automation.
Chinese companies also develop proprietary platforms and sophisticated models.
Moreover, the ecosystems remain commercially interconnected despite technological restrictions.
But their comparative strengths create different pathways toward the same strategic objective: pervasive intelligence at global scale.

VI. THE GEOPOLITICAL CONSEQUENCES
Technological Interdependence Becomes Strategic Competition
The Age of Pervasive Intelligence could accelerate the formation of competing technological spheres.
Governments increasingly recognize that control over AI infrastructure has implications for military capabilities, industrial competitiveness, communications, critical services and economic sovereignty.
Advanced semiconductor restrictions represent one manifestation of this competition.
AI models, operating systems, identity technologies, cloud infrastructure and connected-device standards could become additional areas of strategic differentiation.
But the world will not necessarily divide into two closed coalitions.
A more plausible outcome is asymmetric interdependence.
Countries and companies may cooperate with one technological system in certain sectors while competing with or relying on the alternative system in others.
India may pursue domestic digital sovereignty while purchasing American computing equipment and expanding its own manufacturing base.
Gulf economies may invest in U.S.-aligned AI infrastructure while maintaining extensive commercial relationships with Asian manufacturers.
South Korea and Japan remain strategically important semiconductor and industrial partners while competing across technologies and supply chains.
Southeast Asian economies may attract manufacturing investment from several competing ecosystems.
Europe will face its own strategic choices.
The resulting global architecture may resemble a network of overlapping technological dependencies rather than two perfectly separated blocs.
The important economic variable will be the degree of dependence at each layer of the intelligence stack.
A country may control its industrial data but depend on foreign computing infrastructure.It may manufacture connected devices but depend on external AI models.
It may possess advanced research capabilities but lack competitive financing or electrical infrastructure.
These differences will influence national bargaining power.
The second-order geopolitical effect
The next generation of strategic alliances could increasingly form around access to computing capacity, semiconductors, power systems, trusted data infrastructure and technical standards.
Industrial policy will consequently move beyond subsidizing technology development.
It will increasingly involve securing the entire operating environment through which intelligence becomes economically productive.
The next balance of global power will depend not only on who invents intelligence, but on who controls its deployment.
VII. EUROPE’S THIRD ARCHITECTURE
Trusted Industrial Intelligence
Europe faces a defining strategic choice.
It can continue depending predominantly on foreign technological platforms while attempting to preserve its established industrial strengths.
Or it can develop a distinctive position within the emerging global architecture.
Attempting to duplicate every layer of the American or Chinese ecosystems would require enormous capital and could distract from areas in which Europe possesses genuine competitive advantages.
Europe’s strengths are different.
Industrial engineering.
Precision manufacturing.
Transportation and logistics.
Electrical equipment.
Advanced machinery.
Aerospace.
Automotive systems.
Regulated infrastructure.
These industries contain extensive installed capital and sophisticated operating systems.
They provide a natural environment for the deployment of specialized Agentic AI.
Europe could therefore pursue a third strategic architecture:
Trusted Industrial Intelligence — embedding secure, interoperable and financially productive AI across the continent’s physical economy.
This strategy would not mean abandoning frontier AI research, computing infrastructure or digital sovereignty.
Those capabilities remain essential.
It would mean connecting technology investment more directly to Europe’s established economic strengths.
Factories could deploy specialized agents to improve scheduling, maintenance and production quality.
Railways could integrate intelligent traffic management, predictive maintenance, energy optimization and passenger services.
Energy networks could coordinate storage, generation and demand more efficiently.
Ports and logistics operators could improve asset utilization and multimodal coordination.
Public services could use secure agentic systems to reduce administrative complexity and accelerate delivery.
The strategic advantage would come from combining industrial expertise, trusted data, safety standards and measurable productivity improvements.
Yet Europe faces substantial obstacles.
Fragmented capital markets.
Uneven digital adoption.
Expensive or unreliable energy in certain locations.
Complex permitting.
Limited scale in some technology sectors.
Insufficient integration between research and commercial deployment.
Overcoming these constraints will require substantial institutional and financial reform.
Europe’s opportunity is not simply to consume AI more efficiently.
It is to become a world leader in the industrial architecture through which AI creates economic value.
America can lead in platform intelligence. China can lead in industrial diffusion. Europe must compete to lead in trusted industrial intelligence.
This is an industrial strategy, not a predetermined outcome.
Its success will depend on investment, technological execution and the creation of internationally competitive companies.

Consumers may purchase fewer separate general-purpose computing devices.

Smart glasses could reduce reliance on the screen for navigation, communication and contextual information.
Earbuds could evolve into discreet conversational interfaces.
Smart rings and watches could provide identity verification and biometric signals.
Neural wristbands could become additional input devices.
Spatial displays could extend workspaces into physical environments.
Personal edge-computing systems could provide additional processing capacity.
The resulting architecture would be distributed but coordinated.

IX. THE APPLICATION ECONOMY IS REBUILT
From Apps to Agents, From Attention to Execution
The smartphone era created an economy organized around applications.
App stores controlled distribution.
Search engines facilitated discovery.
Social platforms monetized attention.
Developers competed for screen time, downloads and direct customer relationships.
Agentic AI challenges that architecture.
An intelligent orchestrator may become the principal point through which consumers access commercial services.
The user expresses an intention.
The agent identifies potential providers.
It retrieves information, compares alternatives and executes permitted actions.
The underlying applications may remain necessary, but become less visible.
This could weaken the advantages associated with traditional application discovery and consumer engagement.
The economic contest would shift toward control over agent access, identity, permissions, data quality and transaction execution.
Three consequences follow.
First: Distribution power moves toward orchestration
Companies whose products are accessed through agents may become dependent on the rules governing agent discovery and selection.
The next platform gatekeeper could control the interface through which services are recommended and executed.
Second: Software development becomes increasingly agent-assisted
The supply of software may expand as AI reduces some development costs.
McKinsey’s 2026 survey reports that 32% of respondents say their organizations have decided against purchasing at least one software product or feature because they could build it internally using agentic coding tools.
This puts pressure on undifferentiated application vendors.
Companies offering proprietary data, trusted execution or specialized capabilities may prove more resilient.
Third: Monetization shifts toward completed outcomesA successful agent may provide more economic value by completing a task than by maximizing user engagement.
Commercial models could evolve toward transaction fees, subscriptions, enterprise licensing and charges linked to verified execution.
This represents a potential change in the organization of digital markets.
The Attention Economy monetized engagement. The emerging Execution Economy will increasingly monetize results.
That is the commercial expression of pervasive intelligence.
X. THE NEW CAPITAL FORMATION CYCLE
From Accelerated Computing to Accelerated Economic Returns
The transition to pervasive intelligence carries major consequences for global capital markets.
The first phase of the AI Supercycle has been dominated by investment in computing infrastructure.
Data centers.
Advanced semiconductors.
Accelerated computing systems.
Energy infrastructure.
Networking equipment.
The investment cycle is extraordinary, but the ultimate economic returns remain uneven.
Pervasive intelligence creates a second phase in which intelligence is deployed across consumer activities, enterprise workflows and physical infrastructure.
The financial challenge changes.
Investors must distinguish between the capital required to produce intelligence and the economic value generated when that intelligence is deployed.
Agentic AI becomes especially important because it provides a pathway from computational expenditure toward measurable productivity.
Consider a railway network.
AI infrastructure may require sophisticated computing, communications and software integration.
But economic value emerges only when those capabilities improve traffic management, reduce maintenance costs, increase asset availability, optimize energy use or raise service quality.
The same principle applies to manufacturing, logistics, utilities and connected vehicles.
Technology does not create sustainable value merely by being deployed.
It creates value when deployment improves economic performance sufficiently to justify the capital committed.

If agents reduce coordination costs and improve asset utilization, they can increase the productivity of existing capital rather than merely requiring new infrastructure.
Successful deployment could therefore help bridge the gap between the enormous capital requirements of the AI buildout and the need to demonstrate sustainable returns.
But that process is not automatic.

Execution must be secure.

Capital markets will increasingly reward the conversion of intelligence into productive cash flow, rather than the accumulation of computing capacity alone.

XII. THE NEXT FIVE YEARS
Three Scenarios for 2030
Scenario One — The Integrated Intelligence Platform
A small number of large technology companies retain control over operating systems, AI orchestration, identity and consumer distribution.
Foldable phones, glasses and other wearables operate primarily within integrated proprietary ecosystems.
Platform economics remain powerful.
The competitive advantage lies in scale, consumer trust, software integration and network effects.
The principal risk is concentration of economic and technological power.
Scenario Two — The Fragmented Intelligence World
The United States and China increasingly develop separate technological architectures.
Differences in semiconductors, operating systems, cloud infrastructure, cybersecurity standards and AI governance create barriers to interoperability.
Third countries face more difficult technological choices.
Investment flows increasingly reflect geopolitical alignment.
Industrial efficiency suffers where duplication, incompatible standards and restrictions increase deployment costs.
Scenario Three — The Pervasive Intelligence Economy
Intelligence becomes embedded across devices, industrial systems and physical infrastructure.
Specialized agents operate through interoperable services subject to defined permissions and oversight.
Consumer interfaces become increasingly contextual.
Enterprises reorganize around intelligent execution.
The largest economic returns arise from the productive deployment of AI across existing and new capital assets.
The geopolitical rivalry persists, but multiple technological ecosystems coexist and compete.
The third scenario offers the greatest potential for broad-based economic productivity. It also requires the most demanding combination of technological interoperability, financial discipline and institutional trust.
These scenarios are analytical possibilities, not quantified forecasts.
XIII. FIVE STRATEGIC TAKEAWAYS
For Investors, Boards of Directors and Policymakers
Redefine the technological investment thesis.
The relevant investment frontier is no longer personal computing alone. It is Pervasive Intelligence: the distributed integration of AI into consumer behavior, enterprise operations and physical infrastructure.
Recognize Agentic AI as the decisive acceleration mechanism.
The greatest productivity opportunity lies in the transition from AI-generated information to coordinated economic execution. Investors should measure completed tasks, cost efficiency, cash conversion and incremental return on invested capital.
Position for the next architecture of competitive advantage.Control over models alone will be insufficient. Identity, trusted orchestration, semiconductors, interoperable services, energy infrastructure and industrial deployment will determine the distribution of economic value.
Prepare for a more complex U.S.–China technological divide.
American strengths in frontier platforms and Chinese strengths in industrial diffusion will intensify competition across the intelligence stack. Governments and corporations must manage technological dependencies without assuming that the world will separate into two perfectly closed blocs.
Make Industrial AI the foundation of Europe’s competitive renewal.
Europe’s most credible opportunity lies in combining technological sovereignty with its established strengths in manufacturing, mobility, engineering and critical infrastructure. The objective must be measurable productivity, not technological expenditure for its own sake.
VELOCITY INSIGHT
THE LAW OF PERVASIVE INTELLIGENCE
The evolution of computing has historically followed a recognizable sequence.
Computing power became more abundant.
Its cost declined.
Access expanded.
New applications emerged.
Economic activity reorganized around the resulting capabilities.
Pervasive Intelligence introduces an additional mechanism.
Intelligence is no longer merely made accessible.
It becomes capable of executing an expanding range of economic activities.
That produces the possibility of a reinforcing cycle:
More pervasive intelligence → More agentic execution → Lower coordination costs → Higher capital productivity → Greater economic returns → Further intelligent deployment.
The speed of this cycle will depend on reliability, energy efficiency, interoperability and capital discipline.
Its economic significance could be enormous.
For the first time, computing may become not merely an instrument supporting economic activity but an increasingly active participant in coordinating that activity.
The central measure of success will therefore change.
Not how many intelligent devices are sold.
Not how many models are trained.
Not how many tokens are generated.
But how much additional economic value intelligent systems can produce relative to the capital required to deploy them.
This is the fundamental law of the emerging Pervasive Intelligence Economy.

They will influence the competitive structure of technology platforms, the productivity of industrial systems, the allocation of capital and the geopolitical balance between the United States, China and Europe.

Some countries and companies will control critical intelligence infrastructure.

Still others risk becoming dependent consumers of systems developed elsewhere.

For the United States, the challenge is converting platform leadership into widespread productive deployment.
For China, it is combining industrial scale with technological autonomy and sustainable economic returns.
For Europe, it is building a distinctive architecture of trusted industrial intelligence before external technological dependence becomes structurally entrenched.

The second placed it in the hands of individuals.
The third is beginning to embed it throughout the physical economy.
And Agentic AI will accelerate the transition from intelligence as information to intelligence as execution.
