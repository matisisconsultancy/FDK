THE VELOCITY EDGE — IN FOCUS | NOVEMBER 7, 2026 | FDK
THE AGE OF SECULAR VOLATILITY
Why the World Survived 150 Years of Crises — and Why the Next Regime May Be Different
The central risk is not bigger crises. It is less time to recover between them.
THE MARKET FRAME
Markets are still largely priced around an old assumption:
shocks happen, policy responds, volatility fades, and equilibrium returns.
That framework justified long duration, rewarded leverage, supported just-in-time capital structures and encouraged investors to treat volatility as an interruption rather than a regime.
The next twenty-five years may require the opposite framework.
The defining risk is not that the world suddenly becomes uninvestable.
It is that multiple risk premia remain structurally elevated because shocks no longer arrive sequentially.
War affects energy.
Energy affects inflation.
Inflation affects rates.
Rates affect sovereigns.
Sovereigns affect banks.
Banks affect corporate credit.
Credit affects investment.
Investment determines productivity.
And productivity ultimately determines whether the debt accumulated to absorb the previous shocks can be serviced.
At the same time, AI introduces a second loop:
AI INVESTMENT
→ CAPITAL + ENERGY DEMAND
→ HIGHER HURDLE RATES
→ PRODUCTIVITY EXPECTATIONS
→ ASSET VALUATIONS
→ CREDIT CONDITIONS
→ FUTURE AI INVESTMENT.
The market implication is profound.
The next regime may carry:
a higher term premium,
a higher geopolitical premium,
a higher energy-security premium,
a higher sovereign-risk premium,
and a higher premium on corporate resilience.
Not permanently.
Not everywhere.
But structurally more often.
The central investment question is therefore changing.
It is no longer simply:
When will rates fall?
Or:
When will volatility normalise?
It is:
WHICH COMPANIES, COUNTRIES AND ASSETS CAN COMPOUND WHEN THE RISK-FREE WORLD IS NO LONGER RISK-FREE?


⸻


THE BIG TAKE
The world has survived depressions, wars, energy shocks, financial collapses, pandemics and technological bubbles.
And yet the system kept recovering.
That history has created a powerful assumption: crises are temporary, shocks mean-revert and normalisation eventually returns.
That assumption may now be wrong.
The defining feature of the next twenty-five years may not be one extraordinary crisis.
It may be the disappearance of the intervals between crises.
War is becoming persistent.
Geopolitical competition is structural.
Public debt is already elevated before the next emergency arrives.
Energy systems are under pressure from security, electrification and AI.
Technology is moving faster than institutions.
Financial shocks propagate almost instantly.
And each new disruption increasingly arrives before the consequences of the previous one have cleared.
The central risk is therefore not crisis itself.
IT IS THE LOSS OF RECOVERY TIME.
For most of modern economic history, the world survived because disruption eventually slowed long enough for adaptation to catch up.
The emerging regime threatens that rhythm.
We are moving from:
CRISIS → RESPONSE → RECOVERY → NORMALISATION
to:
CRISIS → PARTIAL RESPONSE → NEW CRISIS → FURTHER ADAPTATION.
That is Secular Volatility.
It is not permanent recession or permanent market decline.
It is an environment in which overlapping structural shocks make the speed of adaptation relative to the speed of disruption the decisive economic variable.

I. WHY THE WORLD KEPT SURVIVING
The historical record reveals a remarkably consistent pattern.
The Long Depression destroyed speculative railway capital but not railways.
World War I shattered the first era of globalisation but not industrial capability.
The Great Depression broke banks and political systems but produced new financial and social institutions.World War II destroyed physical capital on an extraordinary scale, yet reconstruction produced Bretton Woods, European integration and decades of growth.
The 1970s energy crisis produced stagflation, but also energy efficiency, new supply and a new monetary regime.
The dot-com crash destroyed valuations without destroying the Internet.
The Global Financial Crisis exposed leverage, but produced stronger banks and better macroprudential supervision.
The pandemic shut down the global economy, yet fiscal support, central-bank intervention, vaccines and digital infrastructure prevented depression.
Across these episodes, five forces repeatedly drove recovery:
technology, institutional learning, capital mobility, entrepreneurial adaptation and time.
The central lesson is simple:
THE WORLD SURVIVED BECAUSE SYSTEMS ADAPTED BEFORE DAMAGE BECAME PERMANENT.
The question now is whether they will still have enough time.


⸻


II. THE CORE HISTORICAL LESSON
Modern crises often followed a recognisable sequence:
SHOCK → DAMAGE → POLICY RESPONSE → ADAPTATION → NEW EQUILIBRIUM.
The world usually had an “after.”
After 1945 came reconstruction.
After the 1970s came disinflation.
After the Cold War came globalisation.
After 2008 came financial repair and monetary accommodation.
After COVID came reopening.
The emerging regime may offer no equivalent reset.
Geopolitical rivalry can persist after wars end.
Defence spending can remain structurally higher.
Supply chains can remain strategic.
Energy security can remain expensive.
AI investment can continue through valuation corrections.
Climate adaptation can continue through every phase of the business cycle.
The old equilibrium may not return because the old equilibrium itself is disappearing.

III. WHY THIS IS DIFFERENT FROM 2008
The Global Financial Crisis is the natural modern comparison.
But it can mislead.
2008 was fundamentally a crisis inside the financial system. Today’s instability increasingly originates outside finance and then propagates through it.
The 2008 chain was complicated but identifiable:
REAL ESTATE
→ MORTGAGE CREDIT
→ SECURITISATION
→ BANK LEVERAGE
→ COLLATERAL COLLAPSE
→ LIQUIDITY CRISIS
→ CREDIT CONTRACTION.
Once policymakers identified the banking system as the epicentre, the tools were relatively clear:
recapitalise banks,
guarantee liabilities,
provide liquidity,
cut rates,
expand central-bank balance sheets,
support demand.
Today there may be no single balance sheet to repair.
Instability can originate simultaneously in geopolitics, energy, sovereign debt, technology, trade, labour markets, cybersecurity and physical infrastructure.
Central banks can create liquidity. They cannot create natural gas, electricity grids, semiconductors, engineers or geopolitical stability.
In 2008, the dominant macro risk eventually became deflation.
Today a crisis can produce:
**WEAKER GROWTH
HIGHER INFLATION
FINANCIAL INSTABILITY**
at the same time.
Fiscal capacity is also different.
In 2008, governments largely rescued private balance sheets.
In the next major crisis, markets may simultaneously question the balance sheets of the governments doing the rescuing.
And the system now moves faster.
Algorithms react instantly.
Deposits move digitally.
AI agents may increasingly automate financial decisions.
Risk is also more dispersed outside banks.
The essential distinction is simple:
2008 WAS A CRISIS TO BE SOLVED.
THE NEW REGIME IS AN ENVIRONMENT TO BE MANAGED.

Who controls the semiconductor?

Resilience.

VI. THE END OF THE CHEAP-BUFFER WORLD
The next shock arrives with many policy buffers already partially consumed.
Governments face high public debt, higher interest costs, aging populations, defence spending, energy-security investment, climate adaptation, industrial policy and AI infrastructure requirements.
The state remains powerful.
But intervention is becoming more expensive.
The sovereign is no longer simply the ultimate stabiliser.
It is increasingly another balance sheet markets must price.

VII. THE AI SUPERCYCLE — WHY AI CAN BE INFLATIONARY BEFORE IT BECOMES DEFLATIONARY
Artificial intelligence is often described as inherently deflationary.
That is too simple.
AI can eventually reduce the cost of producing knowledge, software, designs, services and physical goods.
But before those gains appear at scale, the economy must build the infrastructure required to produce and deploy intelligence.
That infrastructure is extremely capital- and energy-intensive.
This creates three separate AI inflation channels.
CHANNEL 1 — THE CAPITAL-FORMATION SHOCK
The first is the most immediate.
AI CAPEX
→ DATA CENTRES + CHIPS + NETWORK EQUIPMENT + POWER GENERATION + GRID CONNECTIONS + CONSTRUCTION
→ HIGHER DEMAND FOR SCARCE PHYSICAL INPUTS
→ HIGHER PRICES.
AI does not initially create unlimited productive capacity.
It competes for existing capacity.
That means higher demand for:
advanced semiconductors,
memory,
transformers,
power equipment,
construction labour,
engineers,
electricity,
land,
cooling infrastructure,
and capital.
If those inputs cannot expand as quickly as investment demand, prices rise.
This is investment inflation.
It is analogous to previous infrastructure booms, but potentially much faster because the demand is being generated simultaneously by a small number of extraordinarily well-capitalised global companies.
The key point is:
AI CAN BE DEFLATIONARY IN WHAT IT PRODUCES WHILE BEING INFLATIONARY IN WHAT MUST BE BUILT TO PRODUCE IT.


⸻


CHANNEL 2 — THE ENERGY AND BOTTLENECK SHOCK
The second channel operates through electricity.
Compute is not virtual.
It is energy converted into computation.
As AI adoption expands:
MORE COMPUTE
→ MORE ELECTRICITY DEMAND
→ MORE GRID CAPACITY
→ MORE GENERATION + STORAGE + TRANSMISSION INVESTMENT.
If electricity supply responds slowly, the immediate adjustment occurs through price.
Higher power prices then affect not only data centres.
They affect:
manufacturing,
transport,
households,
industrial margins,
and potentially wage demands.
The transmission becomes:
AI ELECTRICITY DEMAND
→ HIGHER POWER PRICES
→ HIGHER INDUSTRIAL COSTS
→ HIGHER CONSUMER PRICES / LOWER MARGINS
→ POTENTIAL SECOND-ROUND WAGE EFFECTS.
This is why energy policy increasingly becomes monetary policy by another route.
An economy with abundant electricity can absorb the AI investment boom more easily.
An economy with constrained generation and grids experiences more of the transition through inflation.


⸻


CHANNEL 3 — THE FINANCIAL-DEMAND SHOCK
AI also increases the demand for capital itself.
Hyperscalers, utilities, data-centre developers, semiconductor manufacturers, governments and defence industries increasingly compete for the same pool of long-duration financing.
The chain becomes:
AI + DEFENCE + ENERGY CAPEX
→ HIGHER DEMAND FOR CAPITAL
→ HIGHER REAL RATES / TERM PREMIUMS / CORPORATE SPREADS
→ HIGHER HURDLE RATES ACROSS THE ECONOMY.
This is not CPI inflation in the conventional sense.
But it raises the price of capital, which determines the economics of investment.Projects outside the AI ecosystem can therefore be crowded out even without a literal shortage of bank credit.
A traditional industrial company may delay automation because the required return on investment has risen.
A government may postpone infrastructure because debt service absorbs more fiscal capacity.
An SME may face a higher borrowing cost because capital is being allocated toward larger or higher-return projects.
The AI Supercycle can therefore raise the price of both physical resources and financial resources.


⸻


THE FOURTH CHANNEL — AI MAY ALSO SUPPORT DEMAND THROUGH WEALTH AND INCOME
A successful AI boom can increase equity valuations, investment spending and incomes among highly paid workers.
That can sustain aggregate demand.
The mechanism is:
HIGHER AI VALUATIONS
→ HIGHER WEALTH
→ HIGHER CONSUMPTION / LOWER RISK AVERSION
and:
AI CAPEX
→ CONSTRUCTION + ENGINEERING + INDUSTRIAL DEMAND
→ HIGHER SPECIALIST WAGES
→ HIGHER LOCAL SPENDING.
This matters because monetary policy responds to the aggregate economy.
Even if AI eventually expands supply, the investment boom can stimulate demand before new productive capacity becomes operational.


⸻


WHEN DOES AI BECOME DISINFLATIONARY?
The second phase begins only when adoption moves from infrastructure construction into broad productive deployment.
The chain then reverses:
AI ADOPTION
→ AUTOMATION
→ BETTER CAPITAL UTILISATION
→ LOWER ERROR RATES
→ FASTER DESIGN / SOFTWARE / R&D
→ HIGHER OUTPUT PER WORKER
→ LOWER UNIT COSTS.
At that point, companies have several choices.
They can pass savings to customers through lower prices.
Retain them through higher margins.
Use them to increase wages.
Or reinvest them.
Only the first—and indirectly the productivity effects of the others—produces broad macroeconomic disinflation.
That distinction is crucial.
CORPORATE PRODUCTIVITY IS NOT AUTOMATICALLY CONSUMER-PRICE DEFLATION.
AI can substantially increase profits without immediately reducing inflation if firms retain the productivity gains.


⸻


THE TIMING PROBLEM
The macroeconomic outcome therefore depends on which curve moves faster.
Phase I — BUILD
CAPEX ↑
ENERGY DEMAND ↑
CHIP DEMAND ↑
CONSTRUCTION ↑
CAPITAL DEMAND ↑
Inflationary bias.
Phase II — DEPLOY
AUTOMATION ↑
OUTPUT PER WORKER ↑
CAPACITY ↑
UNIT COSTS ↓
Disinflationary bias.
Phase III — DISTRIBUTE
LOWER COSTS → LOWER PRICES / HIGHER REAL WAGES / HIGHER MARGINS
The monetary-policy problem is that Phase I can last years, while markets may price Phase II almost immediately.
That creates one of the central risks of the AI Supercycle:
MARKETS CAPITALISE THE PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND BEFORE THE ECONOMY HAS BUILT THE CAPACITY REQUIRED TO DELIVER IT.
If productivity arrives quickly, the investment validates itself.
If it arrives slowly, interest rates and financing costs may rise sufficiently to weaken the investment cycle before the productivity gains scale.
The adverse sequence becomes:
AI CAPEX
→ INFLATION / HIGHER CAPITAL DEMAND
→ HIGHER RATES
→ HIGHER FINANCING COSTS
→ LOWER AI AND INDUSTRIAL CAPEX
→ DELAYED PRODUCTIVITY
→ WEAKER FUTURE SUPPLY GROWTH.
That is the core AI inflation paradox.
The investment required to create future deflation can itself generate current inflation—and the monetary response to that inflation can delay the future deflation.

VIII. THE CAUSAL TRANSMISSION MAP
Secular Volatility matters because shocks no longer remain in separate silos.
They propagate through one another.
LOOP 1 — GEOPOLITICS BECOMES INFLATION, THEN FINANCIAL TIGHTENING
WAR / STRATEGIC RIVALRY
→ ENERGY DISRUPTION + TRADE RESTRICTIONS + DEFENCE SPENDING
→ HIGHER INPUT COSTS + HIGHER FISCAL DEMAND
→ HIGHER INFLATION
→ HIGHER POLICY RATES + HIGHER TERM PREMIUMS
→ HIGHER CORPORATE AND SOVEREIGN FUNDING COSTS
→ LOWER INVESTMENT
→ WEAKER PRODUCTIVITY.
A geopolitical shock can therefore become a structural productivity problem if the resulting cost of capital remains elevated long enough.


⸻


LOOP 2 — SOVEREIGN STRESS TRANSMITS INTO THE PRODUCTIVE ECONOMYHIGH PUBLIC DEBT + STRUCTURALLY HIGHER SPENDING
→ GREATER SOVEREIGN ISSUANCE
→ HIGHER RISK PREMIUM
→ HIGHER BANK FUNDING COSTS
→ HIGHER CORPORATE BORROWING COSTS
→ LOWER CAPEX
→ LOWER PRODUCTIVITY
→ WEAKER TAX BASE
→ HIGHER SOVEREIGN RISK.
Sovereign risk becomes productivity risk.


⸻


LOOP 3 — AI CAN RAISE RATES BEFORE IT LOWERS COSTS
AI CAPITAL FORMATION
→ HIGHER ENERGY + EQUIPMENT + LABOUR + CAPITAL DEMAND
→ HIGHER CURRENT COSTS
→ HIGHER INFLATION / HIGHER REAL RATES
→ HIGHER INVESTMENT HURDLES.
Only later:
AI DIFFUSION
→ HIGHER PRODUCTIVITY
→ LOWER UNIT COSTS
→ DISINFLATION.
The crucial variable is the lag between the two.


⸻


LOOP 4 — FINANCIAL VELOCITY AMPLIFIES EVERY OTHER SHOCK
SHOCK
→ ALGORITHMIC REPRICING
→ COLLATERAL LOSSES
→ MARGIN CALLS / REDEMPTIONS
→ FORCED DELEVERAGING
→ LOWER LIQUIDITY
→ WIDER CREDIT SPREADS
→ LOWER INVESTMENT.
This creates a speed mismatch:
MARKET TIME — HOURS
MONETARY TIME — MONTHS
FISCAL TIME — YEARS
INFRASTRUCTURE TIME — YEARS
PRODUCTIVITY TIME — YEARS TO DECADES
The system becomes more fragile when the fastest-moving variable can destroy the financing required by the slowest-moving one.

IX. A COMPACT SCENARIO MATRIX
The next decade does not have one inevitable outcome.
The most useful framework is a matrix built around two variables:
How fast does productivity arrive?
and
How restrictive does the cost of capital become?
 LOW / MODERATE COST OF CAPITAL HIGH / PERSISTENT COST OF CAPITAL
FAST PRODUCTIVITY DIFFUSION PRODUCTIVITY BOOM — AI scales into the real economy. Unit costs fall. Earnings broaden. Inflation moderates without recession. Winners: productive AI adopters, industrial automation, infrastructure, quality cyclicals. HIGH-RATE PRODUCTIVITY REGIME — Productivity rises while sovereign borrowing, defence, energy and AI capex keep real rates elevated. Growth survives because returns on capital justify higher discount rates. Winners: cash-rich compounders, banks with pricing power, infrastructure, fiscally credible sovereigns.
SLOW PRODUCTIVITY DIFFUSION LIQUIDITY-DEPENDENT BOOM — AI investment stays strong but productivity disappoints. Easy finance extends valuations and increases capital misallocation. Risk: eventual capital destruction. CAPITAL SQUEEZE — Investment inflation arrives before productivity while high rates, sovereign stress and geopolitical shocks constrain capital formation. Losers: leveraged firms, weak sovereigns, energy-dependent economies, low-productivity sectors.
The matrix exposes the key investment point.
High rates are not necessarily bearish if productivity is rising fast enough.
And low rates are not necessarily bullish if they merely preserve capital misallocation.
The decisive variable is the spread between:
RETURN ON PRODUCTIVE CAPITAL
and
COST OF CAPITAL.
That spread will determine whether the AI Supercycle becomes a durable productivity regime or another capital cycle followed by financial destruction.

X. FROM THE GREAT MODERATION TO THE GREAT DISPERSION
The investment consequence is not uniformly poor returns.
It is wider dispersion.
Between countries.
Between companies.
Between sovereigns.
Between currencies.
Between energy producers and importers.
Between AI leaders and laggards.
Between cash-rich companies and leveraged companies.
Between governments with fiscal credibility and governments without it.
The Great Moderation compressed dispersion.
The new regime should widen it.
The dominant investment approach may therefore move from:
BUY THE INDEX
toward:
PRICE THE DIFFERENCE.
That creates a more demanding market, but potentially a much richer one for active capital allocation.

XI. FIVE LESSONS FROM 150 YEARS OF CRISIS
1. Do not confuse the destruction of capital with the destruction of technology.
Railways survived railway bubbles.
The Internet survived the dot-com crash.
AI can survive an AI valuation collapse.
2. Leverage usually matters more than the narrative.
Technologies fail less often than financing structures.
Debt maturity, collateral, liquidity and refinancing frequently determine who survives.
3.Institutions determine whether shocks become depressions.
Central-bank credibility, fiscal capacity, market liquidity and political legitimacy remain economic assets.
4. Crises accelerate competitive reallocation.
The strongest companies, countries and institutions frequently gain share during stress.
5. Time is becoming the scarcest buffer.
The defining risk is that adaptation to one shock may still be underway when the next arrives.

XII. FIVE TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS AND BOARDS
FIRST — STOP ASKING WHEN VOLATILITY WILL END. BUILD FOR A WORLD IN WHICH IT DOES NOT.
The better question is:
WHICH ASSETS, COMPANIES AND COUNTRIES CAN COMPOUND THROUGH MULTIPLE REGIMES?
A company should no longer be evaluated only on expected earnings under a central scenario.
It should be evaluated across:
higher inflation,
lower growth,
energy shocks,
financial tightening,
trade fragmentation,
technological disruption,
and geopolitical stress.
The investment framework therefore shifts from forecasting toward adaptability.
Can the company reprice?
Can it refinance?
Can it secure energy?
Can it substitute suppliers?
Can it keep investing during stress?
Can it redeploy capital rapidly?
In the Great Moderation, quality meant predictable earnings.
In the new regime, quality increasingly means preserving strategic freedom under uncertainty.
The investment principle becomes:
DO NOT INVEST FOR THE NEXT CRISIS.
INVEST FOR THE ABILITY TO SURVIVE MANY DIFFERENT CRISES.

SECOND — BALANCE-SHEET RESILIENCE BECOMES OFFENSIVE
During the Great Moderation, companies were rewarded for optimisation.
That model becomes more fragile when shocks overlap.
The critical variables are:
**CASH FLOW
LIQUIDITY
DEBT MATURITY
ENERGY ACCESS
SUPPLY-CHAIN REDUNDANCY
PRICING POWER
TECHNOLOGICAL ADAPTABILITY.**
Strong companies do not merely survive downturns.
They buy assets.
Hire talent.
Acquire technologies.
Gain customers.
Take market share.
Resilience becomes offensive optionality.

THIRD — SOVEREIGN RISK RETURNS TO THE CENTRE OF GLOBAL ASSET ALLOCATION
Governments face structurally increasing commitments:
**DEFENCE
AGING
HEALTHCARE
ENERGY SECURITY
CLIMATE ADAPTATION
INDUSTRIAL POLICY
AI INFRASTRUCTURE
HIGHER INTEREST COSTS.**
The likely consequence is not necessarily sovereign default.
It is greater sovereign differentiation.
The sovereign equation becomes:
DEBT SUSTAINABILITY = FISCAL CREDIBILITY × NOMINAL GROWTH × PRODUCTIVITY × MARKET TRUST.
The negative feedback loop is clear:
HIGHER SOVEREIGN RISK PREMIUM
→ HIGHER GOVERNMENT FUNDING COST
→ HIGHER BANK FUNDING COST
→ HIGHER CORPORATE COST OF CAPITAL
→ LOWER INVESTMENT
→ LOWER PRODUCTIVITY
→ WEAKER TAX BASE.
Europe is particularly exposed because it combines one currency and one policy rate with multiple sovereign balance sheets.
The deeper reversal is simple.
For decades, investors asked:
Who will the government rescue?
The next crisis may force a different question:
WHO WILL FINANCE THE GOVERNMENT THAT IS DOING THE RESCUING?
That restores country selection to the centre of global asset allocation.

FOURTH — ENERGY BECOMES PART OF THE COST OF INTELLIGENCE
The AI economy is physical.
Compute requires electricity.
Electricity requires grids.
Grids require investment.
Industrial competitiveness therefore becomes inseparable from energy strategy.
Countries combining reliable, competitively priced electricity with deep capital markets will possess a structural advantage.
Energy is becoming an input into intelligence itself.


⸻


FIFTH — VELOCITY BECOMES THE NEW COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE
In a stable world, scale creates resilience.
In an unstable world, adaptability creates resilience.
The companies and countries capable of sensing change, reallocating capital and executing faster should outperform structures designed for static optimisation.
Velocity is becoming a strategic and financial asset.

XIII. THE EUROPEAN QUESTION
Europe faces perhaps the most consequential strategic challenge.
Its postwar economic model rested, implicitly or explicitly, on four assumptions:
cheap Russian energy,American security,
Chinese demand,
and globally integrated trade and capital.
Each is being reconsidered.
Europe must therefore move from an efficiency architecture toward a resilience architecture.
That requires:
greater energy security,
greater defence capability,
deeper capital markets,
Industrial-AI deployment,
faster infrastructure investment,
more scalable companies,
and selective technological sovereignty.
But resilience cannot mean autarky.
The objective is not withdrawal from globalisation.
It is reducing vulnerability within it.
Strategic autonomy without scale produces expensive isolation.
Strategic autonomy supported by integrated European capital, energy and technology markets can create resilience.


⸻


XIV. THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
The world has repeatedly survived catastrophe because crisis forced adaptation.
The next quarter-century may test whether that mechanism still works when crises overlap and propagate through one another.
If institutions adapt faster than instability accumulates, the new regime could produce extraordinary innovation, capital formation and competitive renewal.
If instability compounds faster than adaptation, the result could be fragmentation, weaker growth and more frequent financial accidents.
The race that matters is:
THE VELOCITY OF DISRUPTION
versus
THE VELOCITY OF ADAPTATION.

THE FINAL TAKE — THE SCARCE RESOURCE IS TIME
History argues strongly against pessimism.
Technology advances.
Institutions learn.
Capital reallocates.
Companies adapt.
The world can survive.
The harder question is at what cost, and who emerges stronger.
The next regime will not be defined by whether crises occur.
It will be defined by whether institutions, companies and governments can prevent the causal chain from becoming self-reinforcing:
SHOCK
→ INFLATION
→ HIGHER RATES
→ HIGHER FUNDING COSTS
→ LOWER INVESTMENT
→ LOWER PRODUCTIVITY
→ WEAKER FISCAL CAPACITY
→ GREATER VULNERABILITY TO THE NEXT SHOCK.
Breaking that loop is the strategic challenge of the next twenty-five years.
The winners will be those capable of doing so fastest.
THE RESPONSE MUST ARRIVE BEFORE THE NEXT CRISIS DOES.
