THE $1 TRILLION TEST
AI has won the technology argument. Now it has to win the return-on-capital argument.
The AI Supercycle has crossed a threshold.
Global AI investment is now forecast to exceed $1 trillion in 2026. Goldman Sachs estimates roughly $580 billion of that investment will occur in the United States alone, while J.P. Morgan puts hyperscaler capital expenditure at approximately $697 billion this year. (Goldman Sachs)
These are no longer technology-sector numbers.
They are macroeconomic numbers.
And they force a much harder question on financial markets:
WHO EARNS THE RETURN ON $1 TRILLION OF AI CAPITAL?
That is today’s Midday Pulse.
The market has spent three years pricing the scarcity of intelligence.
It must now price the abundance of intelligence—and the scarcity of everything required to industrialize it.
⸻
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
What happens when intelligence becomes cheaper faster than the infrastructure required to produce and deploy it?
The answer is potentially one of the largest value migrations of this cycle.
Because AI is simultaneously producing two opposite economic forces:
INTELLIGENCE → DEFLATION
while
INFRASTRUCTURE → CAPITAL INTENSITY
Models improve.
Inference costs fall.
AI adoption accelerates.
But electricity, grids, data centers, memory, networking, cooling, land and financing do not become abundant at the same speed.
Scarcity therefore migrates.
And capital follows scarcity.
⸻
5 SIGNALS THAT MATTER
1 | $1 TRILLION: AI HAS BECOME A CAPITAL-FORMATION EVENT
Goldman Sachs now forecasts more than $1 trillion of global AI investment in 2026. Its analysis suggests commonly quoted hyperscaler capex estimates alone understate the true scale of the investment cycle by roughly $200 billion. (Goldman Sachs)
This changes the comparison.
AI is no longer merely another software cycle.
It increasingly resembles the construction of a new industrial system:
SEMICONDUCTORS → ACCELERATED COMPUTING → DATA CENTERS → POWER → NETWORKS → INDUSTRIAL AI
The decisive metric is therefore moving from:
AI adoption
to:
AI capital productivity.
⸻
2 | $5.3 TRILLION: THE FINANCING PROBLEM IS ONLY BEGINNING
Goldman Sachs estimates hyperscalers could spend approximately $5.3 trillion on AI and data centers through 2030. It also expects private markets to play an increasingly important role as the financing requirement expands beyond what corporate cash flows and traditional liquid credit markets can comfortably absorb. (Goldman Sachs)
That means AI is beginning to migrate:
from P&L → to balance sheet → to credit markets → to infrastructure funds → to private capital.
This is consequential.
The next constraint on AI may not be technological capability.
It may be the cost and availability of capital.
⸻
3 | 35–45 GW: THE BOTTLENECK IS MOVING INTO THE PHYSICAL WORLD
U.S. data-center power demand could reach approximately 35–45 GW by 2030—roughly twice 2024 levels. (Ropes & Gray)
Data-center investment itself increased approximately 32% in 2025 and is projected by Allianz to rise another 75% in 2026. (Allianz.com)
This exposes the fundamental misconception behind much of the AI debate.
The world does not have an unlimited supply of:
electricity, grid connections, transformers, generation capacity, land, cooling systems, memory or capital.
So while intelligence becomes digital and abundant, its industrialization remains intensely physical.
AI is leaving the cloud—and discovering scarcity.
⸻
4 | 3.4%: THE OLD ECONOMY STILL PRICES THE NEW ONE
Today’s U.S. CPI release is expected to show headline inflation around 3.4% year-on-year, with core inflation around 2.5%. (Reuters)
Why does this matter to AI?
Because the most capital-intensive technology transformation in modern history is occurring while the risk-free rate remains economically meaningful.
Every basis point matters when trillions of dollars of long-duration infrastructure must be financed.
The equation is brutally simple:

REQUIRED CASH FLOWThe AI revolution may be technological.
Its valuation remains financial.

5 | THE GREAT SPLIT IS NOW HAPPENING INSIDE AI
This may be today’s most important signal.
The market has treated AI exposure as broadly positive.
That phase is ending.
The relevant distinction is increasingly between:
CAPITAL FORMERS
Companies generating enough cash flow to finance AI investment while maintaining strong balance sheets and acceptable returns on invested capital.
and
CAPITAL CONSUMERS
Companies required to spend aggressively simply to remain technologically competitive—without yet demonstrating corresponding cash-flow creation.
That distinction will become brutal.
Because AI spending is not AI value creation.
And:
CAPEX IS NOT COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE UNLESS IT BECOMES CASH FLOW.

THE DIVISIVE PROPOSITION
AI may democratize intelligence while concentrating economic returns.
That is the paradox markets have not fully priced.
The cost of accessing intelligence can collapse.
Millions of companies can adopt AI.
Billions of individuals can use it.
But the economic rents may still concentrate around those controlling the scarce complementary assets:
accelerated computing energy memory networking data centers industrial platforms proprietary data distribution capital
Cheap intelligence therefore does not automatically imply distributed value creation.
It may produce exactly the opposite.
Abundant intelligence can coexist with concentrated capital.

5 KEY TAKEAWAYS
AI has moved from a technology cycle into a global capital-formation cycle.
The critical financial variable is shifting from AI spending to AI return on invested capital.
Falling intelligence costs will not eliminate scarcity. They will relocate scarcity toward energy, infrastructure and capital.
The Great Split will increasingly separate companies capable of converting AI investment into cash flow from those forced to spend simply to remain relevant.
The biggest winners of Industrial AI may ultimately include companies controlling the physical bottlenecks—not merely those producing the models.
⸻
THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
The first phase of AI was about models.
The second was about scale.
The third is about capital.
And the fourth will be about returns.
The market is approaching the transition between the third and fourth.
That is why the defining question of the AI Supercycle is no longer:
How intelligent can AI become?
It is:
WHO CAPTURES THE RETURN ON THE TRILLION-DOLLAR MACHINE REQUIRED TO BUILD IT?
Because the next phase of AI will not be won by those who spend the most.
It will be won by those who transform capital into intelligence, intelligence into productivity, and productivity into cash flow—faster than everyone else.
That is Velocity.
