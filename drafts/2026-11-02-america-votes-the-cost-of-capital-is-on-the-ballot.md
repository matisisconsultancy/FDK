The Big Take
The midterms could turn the affordability backlash into a reckoning for AI infrastructure, private capital and the political foundations of American growth.
On the eve of the US midterm elections, The Velocity Edge launches a dedicated watch on the political forces reshaping the American economy—and their consequences for global markets.
The central investment question is whether the election changes the conditions under which capital earns its returns.
Our thesis: affordability is becoming the political constraint on America’s investment boom. The election could accelerate the passage from a market that rewards announced investment to one that demands evidence of sustainable returns, credible financing and public consent.
That transition reaches well beyond Washington. It connects household electricity bills to data-centre economics, congressional scrutiny to private-credit underwriting, and the Treasury market to equity valuations worldwide.
Five signals define the inflection point:
Republican electoral vulnerability has widened. Investors must consider a broader range of congressional outcomes.
Affordability connects the campaign’s economic grievances. Housing, healthcare and energy costs are becoming tests of the legitimacy of corporate growth.
AI infrastructure and private capital share that exposure. Their financial interests increasingly intersect with household budgets.
An equity relief rally would leave the fundamental questions unresolved. Political clarity does not establish investment profitability.
The election will begin defining the economic contest of 2028. Both parties will draw conclusions about who should capture the gains—and absorb the costs—of technological transformation.
The electorate is testing the growth model
The final polling points to pressure extending beyond the usual battlegrounds. Siena’s November 1 release showed Democrats narrowly ahead in Alaska and Iowa; its October national survey recorded particularly weak approval of President Trump’s handling of the cost of living. Those findings establish vulnerability, not a settled result. 
The consequential issue is the gap between aggregate economic performance and household experience.
Investment can expand while voters feel poorer. Technology valuations can rise while housing remains inaccessible. Data-centre construction can accelerate while communities question the effect on their electricity bills.
When those experiences diverge, the political response can change project economics.
Capital markets price future growth. Elections determine how much of its cost society is willing to carry.
Our assessment is that this tension could outlast whichever party wins Congress. Affordability offers both parties a powerful organising principle—and exposes businesses whose returns depend on costs being absorbed elsewhere.
AI’s political exposure is becoming financial exposure
The AI investment thesis rests on several distinct propositions: demand will expand, infrastructure will arrive on schedule, financing will remain available, and revenues will justify the capital committed.
An election cannot settle the first proposition. It can influence the others.
The selected Bloomberg AI coverage identifies the industry’s alignment with Trump as a political vulnerability. The financial consequence would depend on how that vulnerability translates into oversight, infrastructure decisions and operating obligations. 
The critical transmission runs through project cash flows:
Greater scrutiny → changed obligations or slower delivery → later revenues → higher financing needs → lower equity returns.
This is a conditional mechanism, not a forecast that every project will face obstruction. But it explains why strong demand for computing does not guarantee strong returns for every owner of computing infrastructure.
A project delayed while interest accrues can lose value before its first customer arrives.A development required to fund more of its grid connection can remain commercially viable while delivering a lower return than originally promised.
The decisive investment distinction is therefore becoming clearer: which businesses can finance the full cost of their expansion—and still generate attractive cash flows?
Political access can help secure a meeting. It cannot service debt.
Private capital faces an accountability test
Bloomberg reports that investment-sector contributions to Republican candidates and associated committees exceeded comparable Democratic contributions by nearly 70%, citing OpenSecrets. Its reporting also describes preparations for increased scrutiny of private equity’s involvement in housing, healthcare and data centres. 
That defensive positioning reveals the stakes.
Private capital is increasingly exposed to businesses that voters encounter through essential household expenses. The more directly those investments touch living costs, the harder it becomes to keep their economics outside political debate.
The first financial consequence may arrive through disclosure rather than legislation.
Congressional investigations can expose contractual terms, leverage, valuation assumptions and the allocation of risk. Investors may then reassess the price at which they are prepared to supply additional capital.
Oversight can change underwriting before Congress changes the law.
This does not establish a systemic crisis. It establishes a differentiation process. Transparent structures with resilient cash flows should withstand scrutiny better than investments dependent on optimistic refinancing assumptions or uninterrupted project delivery.
Congressional control changes the route—not the existence—of risk
Republicans entered the contest with a 53–47 Senate majority. Democrats therefore need a net gain of four seats to establish control without relying on the Republican vice president’s tie-breaking vote. Even Democratic control of both chambers would leave Trump with veto power and substantial executive authority. 
The three principal outcomes imply different financial pressures:
Outcome Immediate consequence Consequential market test
Democrats win both chambers Broader oversight and stronger constraints on legislation and nominations Whether investigations and fiscal confrontation alter earnings expectations or required returns
Democratic House, Republican Senate Legislative bargaining, House investigations and continuing Republican control of confirmations Whether markets underestimate persistent policy uncertainty beneath the familiar “gridlock” label
Republicans retain both chambers Greater capacity to pursue the administration’s legislative programme Whether supportive policy is offset by higher financing costs or concerns about fiscal expansion
The third outcome deserves particular attention as a counterintuitive risk.
A result welcomed by deregulation-sensitive equities could still become difficult for those same businesses if bond investors demand higher yields. Policy support and financial conditions can move in opposite directions.
Conversely, a Democratic victory could increase scrutiny without derailing the investment cycle. Executive agencies would remain under the administration, and major legislative changes would face institutional constraints. 
There is no congressional outcome that abolishes the discipline of cash flow, interest rates and delivery.
A relief rally could conceal the next repricing
Bloomberg’s selected market coverage highlights expectations of a post-election bounce. Such a move has a plausible mechanism: uncertainty recedes, hedges unwind and investors resume purchases. 
But investors should distinguish the removal of an event risk from an improvement in the investment outlook.
The initial reaction will answer: Was the result a surprise?
The following weeks will answer: What will the new balance of power do?
Corporate results and financing conditions will answer the more important question: What is the growth actually worth?
Those answers may diverge.A broad equity rebound could coexist with pressure on exposed technology companies. Strong infrastructure demand could coexist with weaker developer returns. Greater political restraint on new spending could coexist with more contentious negotiations over existing obligations.
The danger is treating a familiar post-election market pattern as a substitute for underwriting. J.P. Morgan Asset Management’s election analysis similarly emphasises the return of investor attention to fundamentals once electoral uncertainty fades. 
The vote can clear the calendar. It cannot clear the balance sheet.
The global consequences begin in the Treasury market
For international investors, the transmission extends through three channels.
First, the cost of money. Fiscal negotiations and expectations for borrowing can affect Treasury yields. Those yields influence the discount rates applied to assets far beyond the United States.
Second, the timing of American investment. Changes to AI infrastructure delivery would reach overseas suppliers of semiconductors, power equipment, cooling systems and industrial components. A delayed order book can damage returns even when long-term demand remains intact.
Third, policy durability. Congressional losses would not automatically remove executive trade-policy uncertainty. International businesses must distinguish a president’s reduced legislative capacity from his continuing executive powers. 
The most revealing market signal may therefore be the relationship between equities, bonds and credit after the result.
If shares rally while financing conditions deteriorate, the market is celebrating political resolution while the investment hurdle is rising.
That divergence would matter more than the first day’s index performance.
The economic contest of 2028 starts here
The midterms will also influence which economic arguments both parties take into the presidential campaign. Reuters identifies the struggle between competing Democratic strategies—and the lessons Republicans draw from the result—as a central consequence of the vote. 
Our assessment is that the most durable pressure will concern the distribution of technological gains.
Can AI produce visible productivity benefits beyond the companies building it? Can infrastructure expand without undermining household affordability? Can private investment demonstrate that its returns reflect value created rather than costs transferred?
A credible answer would strengthen the political foundations of the investment cycle.
Failure would invite a more interventionist contest over its proceeds.
The political durability of American growth will increasingly depend on whether households can recognise themselves among its beneficiaries.
Five decisions for investors and boards
Underwrite projects at their full economic cost. Test who pays for power, grid connections, delays and refinancing.
Separate demand growth from shareholder returns. Capacity expansion alone does not establish value creation.
Treat oversight as a financial variable. Greater disclosure can change counterparties’ willingness to provide capital.
Read bonds and credit alongside equities. A relief rally is less reassuring when the cost of funding rises.
Prepare for several stages of resolution. Close counts may extend beyond election night; policy priorities will take longer to emerge. Delays alone do not establish wrongdoing. 
The decisive question after November 3 will be which companies can preserve their returns when political permission becomes harder to secure and capital becomes less forgiving.
