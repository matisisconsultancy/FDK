A genuinely consequential development today: the Bank of Japan has moved the AI debate directly into the core architecture of monetary policy — demand, productivity, financial conditions and the natural rate of interest.

In remarks published on October 5, BOJ Deputy Governor **Shinichi Uchida** described AI as a large positive demand shock that is already putting upward pressure on activity and prices. He also argued that AI could raise productivity and accelerate capital accumulation, potentially affecting ==r*==, the natural real rate of interest. Crucially, his sequencing is the same one increasingly visible across the Fed, ECB and Bank of England: the demand shock is arriving before the productivity dividend.

That is an important step beyond the existing central-bank discussion. The question is no longer simply whether AI will eventually be disinflationary. It is whether the AI Supercycle is changing the equilibrium price of capital before Industrial-AI deflation arrives.

::pull THE DEMAND SHOCK IS ARRIVING BEFORE THE PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND.

::section The Financial-Conditions Paradox | Two channels, opposite signs

Uchida identified a striking financial-conditions paradox. Rising AI valuations are lifting asset prices and easing financial conditions, while massive bond issuance by AI companies is simultaneously pushing long-term yields higher. On balance, he said, the demand side appears so far to have made conditions more accommodative — but warned of a correction if profits fail to validate the investment boom.

The emerging monetary chain runs one way: AI capex → demand shock → asset-price wealth → easier financial conditions → inflation pressure. And simultaneously the other: AI capex → debt issuance → higher long yields → higher hurdle rates → tighter financial conditions.

Central banks now have to determine which channel dominates.

::section The ECB Is Confronting The Same Paradox | A European dimension

ECB Chief Economist **Philip Lane** added an important European dimension today. He said the jump in AI-related investment is supporting growth, but that heavy borrowing by technology companies to finance unusually large investment programs is also contributing to higher long-term interest rates. Lane argued that those higher yields — together with expensive energy and diminishing fiscal support — may themselves slow demand enough to reduce the amount of additional ECB tightening required.

This sharpens yesterday's thesis: AI is becoming both an inflationary force and a tightening mechanism. The investment boom raises demand, energy consumption and capital requirements. But the debt required to finance that boom raises yields and ultimately destroys some demand elsewhere in the economy. That is why the policy rate alone is becoming a progressively weaker measure of monetary restrictiveness.

::section The Neutral-Rate Question Is Now Unavoidable | From productivity forecast to r*

The significance of Uchida's intervention is not that the BOJ has concluded that r* has risen. It has not. Uchida explicitly said the overall effect remains difficult to determine. The significance is that AI has moved from the productivity forecast into the neutral-rate debate.

If AI permanently increases desired investment and expected returns on capital, equilibrium real rates should tend to rise. If productivity ultimately expands supply significantly faster than demand, disinflationary forces strengthen. Both can happen — but at different points in the cycle.

::patterns The Two-Phase Regime | What monetary policy must survive
# Phase I — Capital Formation
Inflationary, energy-intensive, rate-positive. The build-out raises demand, energy consumption and capital requirements before any supply dividend lands.

# Phase II — Productivity Diffusion
Supply-expanding, margin-enhancing, potentially disinflationary. Monetary policy has to survive Phase I before markets receive the full benefits of Phase II.

::signals The Great Split As A Monetary Variable | Implications across the complex

# For bonds
AI capex should be treated as a structural competitor for global capital. Long yields may remain higher even if headline inflation eventually falls.

signal: Structural competition for capital keeps the long end elevated.

# For equities
The crucial variable is moving from AI revenue growth to return on invested AI capital. If profits fail to validate capex, the correction can tighten financial conditions abruptly.

signal: Return on invested AI capital now matters more than revenue growth.

# For central banks
Strong equity markets may partially offset rate hikes even while debt issuance raises the long end. Monetary restrictiveness must increasingly be assessed across the entire financial-conditions complex.

signal: The policy rate alone no longer measures restrictiveness.

# For Europe
The ECB faces an asymmetric problem. Europe can import the higher global cost of capital generated by the AI build-out without capturing an equivalent share of AI investment and productivity.

signal: Europe imports the cost of capital without the investment share.

# For Industrial AI
The disinflation thesis remains intact, but its timing matters more than its destination. Productivity arriving in 2029 cannot automatically neutralize demand inflation in 2026–27.

signal: Timing, not destination, is the open question.

::takeaways What changed today | The harder question
- Central banks were largely asking: how much will AI increase productivity?
- The BOJ has now framed the harder question: what happens to the natural rate, financial conditions and inflation while the world finances the transition?
- That is the next stage of the Central Banks–AI Watch.
- Source: Bank of Japan — Shinichi Uchida, *AI, Big Data, and Monetary Policy*, October 5, 2026.

::close The AI Supercycle is no longer merely changing the economy central banks regulate. It is beginning to ==change the variables with which they regulate it==.
