THE BORDER INSIDE THE DATA CENTRE
Washington’s New Computing Restrictions, OpenAI’s 457 MW Expansion and the Great Repricing of AI Capital Reveal a New Architecture of Economic Power
The Big Take | Where Intelligence, Capital Formation and Financial Markets Collide
THE BIG TAKE
The AI Supercycle is entering a new phase in which the right to access and commercialize computing capacity may become as financially consequential as the ability to build it.
The developments of November 16–17 reveal a striking contradiction.
The United States is tightening controls on foreign access to advanced American computing technology, extending restrictions beyond the physical shipment of semiconductors toward remote computing services.
At the same time, OpenAI has signed a six-year agreement for 457 megawatts of AMD-powered computing capacity, demonstrating that large infrastructure commitments continue despite market volatility.
Institutional investors are becoming more selective. New analysis of almost 6,400 regulatory filings shows that more funds exited data-centre equities than initiated positions during the third quarter, while semiconductor investment remained comparatively resilient.
And Europe is attracting new capital into industrial AI: Flagship Pioneering announced a £100 million commitment to establish AI-native biotechnology companies in Britain, while Italy’s Prysmian is reconsidering a US dual listing as AI-related demand expands its American operations. THE CENTRAL THESIS
The next Great Split may emerge between companies that own computing capacity and companies that can reliably monetize it across an increasingly fragmented geopolitical, financial and regulatory landscape.
The distinction is fundamental.
A data centre can be fully financed but lack permission to serve particular customers.
A semiconductor supplier can possess substantial production capacity but face restrictions on where its products may be deployed.
An AI company can secure long-term infrastructure commitments while facing uncertainty over customer utilization, future model economics and financing costs.
And an industrial company can benefit from AI investment without owning frontier models or computing facilities.
The new causal chain is:
Capital Formation → Physical Computing Capacity → Regulatory Market Access → Commercial Utilization → Cash-Flow Conversion → Risk-Adjusted Returns.
The important addition is regulatory market access.
Until recently, much of the financial debate concerned whether companies could finance and construct the extraordinary infrastructure required by AI.
The latest US measures demonstrate that the ability to serve customers can itself become a binding constraint.
For Europe, this introduces an important strategic opportunity.
Technological sovereignty must increasingly mean the ability to maintain commercially viable access to intelligence under changing international conditions—not simply the ownership of additional computing infrastructure.
1 | GEOPOLITICS: THE UNITED STATES EXTENDS EXPORT CONTROLS INTO REMOTE COMPUTING
A new regulatory architecture for international AI infrastructure
The most consequential official development is the November 16 announcement by the US Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security.
BIS released an interim final rule addressing the use of advanced American semiconductors through overseas data centres.Its purpose is to prevent companies subject to US restrictions from obtaining sensitive computing capabilities indirectly through third-country facilities. Bureau of Industry and Security
What is changing
The rule introduces controls on certain exports, reexports and transfers involving data centres that provide substantial computing capacity to companies headquartered in restricted jurisdictions.
A key threshold concerns access exceeding 12 million aggregate total processing performance units, including linked data-centre facilities.
The measure also introduces additional licensing requirements affecting five countries:
Singapore
Malaysia
Thailand
Indonesia
Brazil
The rule provides exceptions and licensing mechanisms, including specified arrangements involving US-controlled entities. Its coverage depends on the products, end users, end use and relevant licensing conditions.
The interim final rule is expected to be formally published and become effective on November 19, 2026. Nixon Peabody LLP
What changed versus November 16
Yesterday’s baseline consisted principally of reports about possible American efforts to strengthen AI exports and concerns about offshore access to restricted technology.
Today’s evidence establishes a formally announced regulatory measure.
This is a material policy development, not merely an indication of future political intentions.
However, it is not a universal ban on providing computing services in the five countries.
Nor does it establish that all existing commercial contracts must immediately terminate.
The financial mechanism
The consequences depend on customer concentration and the structure of individual infrastructure projects.
New US Remote-Computing Controls
Commercial exposure
Licensing, customer eligibility, contractual restrictions
Financial exposure
Potential utilization delays, refinancing uncertainty, project valuation
Potential Repricing of Computing Assets by Jurisdiction and Customer Mix
A developer whose prospective revenues depend heavily on customers affected by licensing restrictions may face greater uncertainty over utilization.
That uncertainty can influence expected cash flow, financing availability and asset valuation.
But projects serving permitted customers may remain largely unaffected.
Second-order consequences
The restrictions could make customer eligibility and contractual flexibility increasingly important underwriting criteria.
Banks and infrastructure investors may require more detailed information about end users, ownership chains and the jurisdictions in which computing services are ultimately consumed.
The distinction between the location of the physical data centre and the location of the economic beneficiary is becoming more important.
The geography of computing demand is now also a question of regulatory jurisdiction.
Implications for Europe
Europe must avoid excessive dependence on computing supply chains whose international availability could change abruptly.
But the appropriate response is not blanket technological isolation.
It is greater resilience, access to alternative providers, stronger European capabilities and regulatory predictability.

2 | ACCELERATED COMPUTING: OPENAI SIGNS FOR 457 MW OF AMD CAPACITY
A new commercial commitment confirms that physical expansion continues
On November 16, TensorWave announced a six-year agreement to supply OpenAI with 457 MW of dedicated computing IT capacity across multiple US data-centre locations.
The infrastructure will use AMD Instinct MI455X processors and the AMD Helios rack-scale architecture.
The parties expect capacity to begin entering service in the first half of 2027, with full deployment planned by early 2028.
TensorWave states that it has secured more than 2 GW of long-term data-centre capacity across its broader development portfolio. Yahoo Finance
What changedPrevious editions established OpenAI and AMD’s multigigawatt strategic relationship.
The new announcement identifies a specific cloud-infrastructure operator, capacity quantity and deployment schedule.
This is a new signed commercial agreement within an existing strategic partnership.
It is not 457 MW of computing infrastructure already operating.
The financial value of the agreement has not been publicly disclosed.
Why it matters
The announcement reinforces the commercial expansion of alternative accelerator ecosystems.
AMD is seeking to strengthen its position in large-scale AI computing through an integrated hardware and software architecture.
OpenAI gains additional procurement options.
TensorWave gains a substantial long-duration customer commitment.
The potential financial mechanism is:
Processor Diversification → Greater Procurement Flexibility → Competitive Computing Economics → Potentially Lower Cost per Useful AI Task.
However, the benefits must be demonstrated.
Performance, software compatibility, installation schedules and operating costs will determine the ultimate economics.
The Great Split
A major model developer can enter long-term computing agreements to secure future capacity.
A cloud operator must finance and deliver that capacity.
A semiconductor manufacturer can benefit from processor sales.
These are complementary but financially distinct positions.
The commercial value of the agreement depends on actual delivery, customer utilization and the revenues generated from the computing services.
For investors, the decisive variables are contractual quality, capital expenditure, operating utilization and cash-flow timing.
NER-C assessment: MAJOR NEW COMMERCIAL INFRASTRUCTURE AGREEMENT. No new 457 MW operating facility or independently verified return on invested capital.
3 | CAPITAL FORMATION: THE GREAT SPLIT MOVES INTO INSTITUTIONAL PORTFOLIOS
Data-centre equities face selective exits even as semiconductor exposure survives
Reuters’ November 16 analysis of nearly 6,400 SEC institutional filings provides a new perspective on capital-market behavior.
Institutional positions disclosed for Q3 2026
Reuters analysis published November 16 of holdings as of September 30. Position counts do not measure capital flows or disclose the reasons for trading. Reuters
Institutional holders across eight major data-centre-related companies declined from 3,411 to 3,392.
Semiconductors showed a different pattern: 2,951 funds increased positions, while 2,584 reduced them.
The data demonstrate selectivity, but not a universal retreat from AI.
What changed
Newly released regulatory filings provide evidence that institutional differentiation began during the third quarter.
The important temporal distinction is that the positions were held on September 30.
They do not directly measure transactions made in response to Nvidia’s November leadership crisis or the latest US export restrictions.
BlackRock’s contrasting move
In a separate development, Bloomberg reported November 16 that BlackRock increased the equity overweight in its model portfolios from 1% to 3% while broadening its AI exposure.
The changes concern a model-portfolio business managing more than $300 billion.
BlackRock also adjusted duration and credit exposures, reflecting a strategy of retaining participation in economic growth while managing valuation and financing risks. Yahoo Finance
The contrast is revealing.
Some investors reduced exposure to selected data-centre and utility companies.
Other investors increased equity risk while seeking broader diversification.
The market is not simply choosing between owning AI and abandoning AI. It is reconsidering which forms of AI exposure justify their valuations and financing risks.
The financial implication
The AI Supercycle encompasses several different balance-sheet and operating models.
Semiconductor suppliers can generate current revenue from equipment demand.
Infrastructure developers require substantial financing and construction expenditure.AI service providers depend on converting computing capacity into recurring customer revenues.
And enterprise adopters may capture productivity improvements without owning much of the underlying infrastructure.
These differences can generate distinct risk-adjusted returns.
The broader portfolio challenge
Reuters also reported that a basket of 52 AI-related companies represented 38.5% of public equity holdings across a sample of large pension funds as of June 2026.
That figure does not represent 38.5% of their total investment assets.
Nevertheless, it demonstrates why direct and indirect AI concentration is becoming important for institutional portfolio management. Reuters
NER-C assessment: MATERIAL NEW EVIDENCE OF INVESTOR SELECTIVITY AND PORTFOLIO REPOSITIONING. No definitive, industry-wide capital rotation is established.
4 | INDUSTRIAL AI AND PRODUCTIVITY: EUROPE’S SCIENTIFIC CAPITAL ATTRACTS NEW INVESTMENT
Flagship Pioneering establishes its first international company-creation laboratory
The most important new European industrial-AI investment comes from Flagship Pioneering.
On November 16, the company announced a £100 million initial commitment to create an AI-native biotechnology company-development laboratory in London.
The initiative represents Flagship’s first dedicated company-creation unit outside the United States.
It intends to recruit approximately 50 scientist-entrepreneurs and operators.
Flagship expects to seek an additional £500 million or more from partners.
That additional amount is an ambition, not completed financing. Flagship Pioneering
What changed
The previous baseline emphasized the deployment of AI within existing businesses and the expansion of European computing infrastructure.
This investment introduces a different mechanism.
AI is being incorporated into the creation of new scientific and industrial enterprises, rather than simply deployed to improve existing processes.
That distinction is strategically important.
The economic opportunity extends from operational efficiency toward new products, intellectual property and scientific discovery.
The causal chain
AI-Enabled Scientific Research → Faster Hypothesis Generation → Experimental Validation → New Commercial Technologies → Intellectual Property and Enterprise Value.
The UK offers specialized research institutions, clinical capabilities and access to health-related data under applicable safeguards.
These assets can become economically more valuable when combined with advanced computational methods.
But commercial returns remain uncertain.
AI-generated scientific hypotheses must survive laboratory validation, regulatory evaluation and, where applicable, clinical trials.
What remains unproven
The £100 million commitment does not establish successful drug discovery, regulatory approval or profitable commercialization.
Nor does it prove that AI-native biotechnology produces superior returns relative to conventional life-sciences investment.
Nevertheless, the development strengthens the case for treating scientific discovery as a distinct component of the Next Economy.
For European policymakers, this is an argument for connecting universities, data infrastructure, venture capital and industrial commercialization.
For investors, it creates potential exposure to new intellectual property and technology platforms.
NER-C assessment: MAJOR NEW EUROPEAN INDUSTRIAL-AI CAPITAL COMMITMENT. No verified commercial productivity or clinical-efficacy inflection yet.
5 | ENERGY AND GRIDS: THE ENVIRONMENTAL COST BECOMES MORE MEASURABLE
New academic evidence adds a physical constraint to the investment model
On November 16, Reuters reported newly published peer-reviewed research from Harvard University examining US hyperscale data-centre emissions.
The study estimated that 403 operating US hyperscale data centres generated approximately 25.7 million tonnes of CO₂ between May 2024 and April 2025.
The study estimated that this was approximately three times the corresponding level in 2018. Reuters
What changedPrevious editions emphasized future electricity-demand projections and the risks of on-site gas generation.
The new study provides a retrospective estimate of emissions associated with operating facilities.
It therefore adds empirical evidence about the environmental footprint of existing infrastructure.
This is different from forecasts concerning projects that may never be built.
The financial interpretation
Environmental and electricity-system constraints can affect project economics through permitting requirements, electricity costs, grid investments and potential mitigation expenditure.
The transmission mechanism is:
Computing Expansion → Electricity and Infrastructure Requirements → Environmental and Community Costs → Regulation and Financing Conditions → Project Returns.
The impact depends on location, electricity mix, project design and policy.
Europe: Prysmian illustrates the complementary industrial opportunity
Italy’s Prysmian provides a significant counterpoint.
On November 16, CEO Massimo Battaini said that the company would reconsider a potential US dual listing as acquisitions and demand for electricity and telecommunications cables expand its American business.
North America accounts for approximately 40% of Prysmian’s revenue.
Management reported stronger demand for power and fibre-optic cables during the second half of 2026.
A dual listing remains under consideration and has not been approved or implemented. Reuters
The financial implications are twofold.
First, European industrial companies can capture revenues from global AI infrastructure investment without directly owning the computing assets.
Second, the possible relocation or duplication of capital-market listings raises a question about Europe’s ability to provide internationally competitive financing and valuation conditions for its industrial champions.
Europe may retain technological and industrial expertise while parts of the financial valuation and capital-formation process migrate toward deeper American markets.
That possibility requires empirical testing; it is not established by Prysmian’s preliminary deliberations alone.

6 | CHINA: THE COMPETITION SHIFTS FROM PROCESSORS TO KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER
Distillation becomes a more important geopolitical mechanism
Reuters’ November 17 investigation reviewed more than 300 Chinese academic papers, patents, company disclosures and policy documents.
It found growing use of model distillation, in which the outputs of powerful AI models are used to develop smaller, more specialized systems.
The research included examples involving Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Alibaba and Meituan.
A November 2024 research example involved approximately 5,000 selected answers from a US-developed model used to construct 10,750 bilingual training examples. Reuters
Fact versus interpretation
Model distillation is a well-established technical method and is not inherently unlawful.
However, particular forms of using model outputs to develop competing systems may breach contractual terms or raise intellectual-property concerns.
There is no verified basis for attributing all Chinese model improvements to distillation.
The second-order effect
Access to Frontier Model Outputs → Training of Smaller Specialized Models → Lower Potential Deployment Costs → Wider Industrial AI Adoption.
This matters because the economic competition between the United States and China is not determined exclusively by the largest training clusters.
Efficiency improvements, software engineering and the adaptation of models to specialized industrial tasks also influence competitiveness.
The financial consequence
Restrictions on advanced processors may increase incentives for algorithmic efficiency and alternative development approaches.
But distillation cannot completely replace the computing requirements of frontier-model development.The commercial consequences will depend on model quality, cost, licensing and industrial deployment.
For Europe, the episode reinforces the value of independent research, model interoperability, intellectual-property protection and competitive specialized AI systems.
NER-C assessment: MATERIAL NEW DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE OF INDUSTRIAL AI DEVELOPMENT PRACTICES. NO VERIFIED GENERAL TECHNOLOGICAL PARITY OR NEW MODEL-PRODUCTIVITY INFLECTION.

8 | THE NEW NER-C RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS
THE JURISDICTIONAL UTILIZATION DISCOUNT
Could Regulatory Access to Computing Markets Become a Measurable Determinant of Data-Centre Valuations?
The new US remote-computing restrictions create an important research opportunity.
Hypothesis: Data centres with greater exposure to customers whose access is restricted, licensed or legally uncertain may command lower risk-adjusted valuations than comparable infrastructure serving commercially accessible markets.
This extends the NER-C framework beyond the physical and financing constraints previously examined.
The new variable is the regulatory accessibility of commercial demand.
The proposed mechanism
Regulatory Restrictions → Eligible Customer Base → Expected Computing Utilization → Cash-Flow Reliability → Financing Costs → Asset Valuation.
The financial impact may depend on the share of capacity committed to potentially restricted customers, the ability to substitute customers and the duration of existing contracts.
An infrastructure asset may possess strong technical capabilities while facing reduced commercial flexibility.
Conversely, a project designed to serve diversified and legally accessible customers may have more resilient utilization.
Empirical research framework
Dimension Proposed measurement
Regulatory exposure Share of customers affected by export-control or licensing requirements
Customer diversification Revenue concentration by geography and ultimate parent
Computing utilization Actual versus contracted use
Commercial pricing Revenue per unit of usable computing capacity
Financing Credit spreads, debt-service coverage, refinancing costs
Market valuation Enterprise value relative to operating capacity and cash flow
Regulatory adaptation Licensing outcomes, customer substitution and redesign costs
A useful approach would be an event study examining affected infrastructure and semiconductor companies around the November 16 announcement and November 19 effective date.
A second phase could compare data-centre projects in affected jurisdictions with otherwise similar projects in jurisdictions not subject to the additional destination-based requirements.
The analysis would need to control for financing leverage, construction stage, electricity availability, customer quality and broader market volatility.
The falsification test
The hypothesis would be weakened if regulatory exposure failed to predict financing costs, utilization or relative asset valuation after controlling for other factors.
It would be strengthened if greater jurisdictional restrictions systematically reduced revenue visibility or increased financing premiums.
The European extension
The research could inform European sovereign-computing investment, cloud procurement and infrastructure finance.
The critical question is whether Europe can preserve commercially valuable access to intelligence during periods of geopolitical disruption without unnecessarily increasing the cost of computing.
Research status: NEW AND EMPIRICALLY TESTABLE.The regulatory change is confirmed; its consequences for asset valuation and realized commercial utilization remain unmeasured.

FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS, BOARDS AND EUROPEAN POLICYMAKERS
1. Computing market access has become a financial variable. The US restrictions demonstrate that physical infrastructure and sufficient financing do not necessarily guarantee unrestricted commercial use.
2. The underlying investment cycle remains active. OpenAI’s new 457 MW agreement confirms substantial continuing commitments to alternative accelerator capacity, even as financial markets reassess valuations and risk.
3. Institutional investors are increasingly differentiating among AI exposures. Data-centre equity exits coexist with continuing semiconductor investment and broader AI allocations. This is selectivity, not a verified generalized retreat.
4. Europe’s industrial AI opportunity is becoming more concrete. Flagship’s biotechnology investment and Prysmian’s global infrastructure exposure show two distinct routes through which European capabilities can generate economic value.
5. The next investment leaders may be those combining technological capability with reliable commercial access and strong capital productivity. The financial measure is not capacity announced but cash flow generated after accounting for financing, electricity, regulation and operating costs.
THE FINAL VELOCITY INSIGHT
THE NEXT GREAT CAPITAL ROTATION WILL FOLLOW THE RIGHT TO GENERATE RETURNS
The AI Supercycle continues expanding the world’s capacity to produce intelligence.
But the events of November 16–17 expose an increasingly important limitation.
Possessing computing capacity is no longer sufficient. Companies must also secure the commercial, regulatory and financial conditions required to make that capacity economically productive.
The United States is extending its control over how sensitive computing capabilities can be accessed internationally.
OpenAI is signing substantial new infrastructure agreements.
Institutional investors are reconsidering the financial risks associated with data centres and the broader AI ecosystem.
European industrial companies are capturing commercial opportunities through electricity infrastructure, scientific innovation and specialized technologies.
And China is investing in methods that may reduce the computing intensity of industrial applications while introducing new questions about intellectual property and technological dependence.
These developments point toward three evolutionary consequences.First, the geographical location of AI infrastructure may become less important than the commercial jurisdictions it is permitted to serve. Regulation can alter customer accessibility and therefore expected financial returns.
Second, the allocation of capital may increasingly favor companies with diversified revenue sources and credible cash-flow conversion. Greater computing capacity creates opportunity, but financing flexibility and commercial utilization determine its economic value.
Third, Europe’s competitiveness may depend on its ability to connect sovereign capabilities with industrial deployment and global financial markets. Technological infrastructure must be supported by productive demand, competitive electricity and capital capable of sustaining innovation.
For investors, the opportunity is to identify companies whose technological assets remain commercially valuable under changing political and financial conditions.
For boards of directors, the imperative is to assess not only the cost of building intelligence, but the durability of the markets in which that intelligence can be sold.
For policymakers, the challenge is to preserve strategic resilience without imposing costs that undermine innovation and productivity.
The next stage of the AI Supercycle will increasingly distinguish between the ownership of technological capacity and the economic right and ability to monetize it.
THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
Capital builds intelligence. Infrastructure makes it available. Regulation determines who can access it. But only productive demand converts it into enduring financial value.
