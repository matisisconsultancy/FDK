Brussels Resets Tender Submissions, Legrand Reveals a 40% AI Infrastructure Growth Engine, and Skanska Signals a More Selective Investment Cycle
THE BIG TAKE — Three consequential developments today expose a widening distinction in Europe’s AI infrastructure market: demand for computing infrastructure continues accelerating, but financing discipline and the ability to execute complex projects are becoming increasingly decisive.
The most immediate development comes from EuroHPC, which has issued an important operational warning affecting existing Gigafactory tender submissions.
In France, Legrand has raised its 2026 financial guidance following exceptional growth in data-center infrastructure.
In Sweden, construction group Skanska is explicitly distinguishing financially credible AI infrastructure developers from less established competitors.
Together, these developments suggest an important transition.
Europe’s next AI infrastructure investment cycle may reward execution certainty and industrial profitability more than announced investment scale.
1 | EUROHPC — A NEW PROCUREMENT WARNING REQUIRES CONSORTIA TO RESUBMIT THEIR BIDS
November 5 — Official tender update.
EuroHPC’s updated procurement notice introduces an immediate administrative requirement for AI Gigafactory applicants.
For technical reasons, existing draft and submitted tenders in the current system will not be considered. Applicants must preserve their materials and recreate and resubmit their applications through replacement submission links.
EuroHPC says the new links will be announced shortly and advises applicants not to continue working on existing electronic drafts until instructions are available.
The procurement deadlines themselves remain unchanged from the previous assessment:
Large-scale Gigafactories — Lot 2: December 3, 2026, at 17:00 CET.
Medium-scale Gigafactories — Lot 1: February 4, 2027, at 17:00 CET.
The extension for medium-scale projects was already known. The consequential new element is today’s warning about the validity of existing submissions. 
Why this matters
This is an administrative development rather than a change in industrial policy, but it has practical implications for participating consortia.
For Spain’s ACS–Telefónica–Santander consortium, Italy’s emerging industrial partnership and competing French, German and Nordic proposals, submission management is now an immediate execution priority.
Investor implication: Tender participation must not be confused with procurement success, and the resubmission requirement creates an additional operational dependency.
CEO and board implication: Consortium governance should ensure that completed application documents, financing commitments and supporting evidence are preserved and correctly transferred to the new submission system.
The broader importance is the increasing proximity of formal procurement decisions.
The EU initiative envisages up to seven Gigafactories, supported by €10 billion of European and national funding and intended to mobilize at least €20 billion in private investment. 
2 | FRANCE — LEGRAND’S RESULTS CONFIRM ACCELERATING AI INFRASTRUCTURE DEMAND
November 5 — Confirmed financial results and upgraded guidance.
Legrand reported exceptional growth in its data-center infrastructure business.
The company’s first nine months of 2026 delivered:
Financial indicator Reported performance
Group revenue €8.33 billion
Organic group revenue growth +12.7%
Data-center organic growth Nearly +40%
Adjusted operating margin 20.8%
Net profit attributable to shareholders +19.6%
Revised 2026 organic growth guidance 11%–13%
Data centers now account for approximately one-third of Legrand’s revenue.
The company also increased its expected full-year sales growth excluding currency effects to 20%–22%, compared with its previous 16%–19% guidance.
These figures represent confirmed operating performance, not projected Gigafactory investment. 
The hidden patternLegrand’s results provide evidence that the AI infrastructure investment cycle is generating substantial revenues for European industrial suppliers.
Importantly, Legrand is not dependent on owning advanced processors or developing proprietary AI models.
It supplies essential electrical infrastructure, power-distribution systems, cooling equipment and related technologies.
The investment transmission mechanism is:
AI COMPUTE EXPANSION → ELECTRICAL INFRASTRUCTURE DEMAND → INDUSTRIAL REVENUE GROWTH → CASH GENERATION → REINVESTMENT
This illustrates an important opportunity for European capital formation.
Europe can capture value from the global AI Supercycle through industrial technology even when the largest computing customers remain American hyperscalers.
But that distinction also exposes Europe’s structural challenge.
Supplying the world’s AI infrastructure is not equivalent to controlling the infrastructure or retaining the full economic value of AI services.
Implications for investors
Legrand’s results strengthen the investment case for selected European electrical infrastructure suppliers.
They also suggest that investors should distinguish between two business models.
The first involves financing new computing facilities, with exposure to utilization, technological obsolescence and substantial capital expenditure.
The second involves supplying essential infrastructure components to multiple operators.
The second model may offer a different risk profile, although it remains exposed to the same underlying investment cycle.
For European policymakers, Legrand demonstrates that AI competitiveness should encompass the entire industrial supply chain rather than focusing exclusively on GPUs and cloud platforms.
3 | NORDICS — SKANSKA SIGNALS GREATER FINANCING SELECTIVITY
November 5 — New corporate assessment of AI infrastructure demand.
Swedish construction group Skanska reported strong demand from established technology customers while expressing greater caution toward newer data-center developers.
Its third-quarter construction orders increased 70% to SEK 68 billion.
Its order backlog reached SEK 328.3 billion.
The company’s CEO indicated that established clients continue commissioning larger projects, while Skanska is scrutinizing newer developers more carefully.
The reported figures include major American projects and cannot be interpreted as European Gigafactory orders. 
Why this matters
Skanska’s position highlights an emerging distinction within AI infrastructure development.
Demand remains strong, but developers do not necessarily possess comparable financial strength.
The potential investment hierarchy is becoming clearer:
CONTRACTED CUSTOMERS → SECURED FINANCING → EXECUTABLE CONSTRUCTION → OPERATIONAL CAPACITY → SUSTAINABLE RETURNS
For European banks and infrastructure funds, the implications are significant.
Financing structures based primarily on anticipated future demand may become less attractive than projects supported by creditworthy customers and contractual revenue visibility.
For Nordic developers, which continue attracting international computing investment, the quality of the customer and the durability of financing arrangements could become increasingly important competitive differentiators.
The next phase of Europe’s AI infrastructure market may therefore produce simultaneous growth and consolidation rather than uniform expansion.

4 | A NEW EUROPEAN RISK — THE SOCIAL LICENSE TO BUILD
A separate development reinforces the growing importance of project acceptance.
On November 5, reporting confirmed plans for coordinated demonstrations against major data-center developments across 15 European countries on November 6–7.
More than 100 organizations are participating in the campaign, which raises concerns about electricity consumption, environmental impacts and local economic benefits.
These are planned protests, not enacted moratoria or cancelled projects. 
Nevertheless, they introduce an additional dimension to infrastructure risk.For Spain, France, Germany and the Nordics, project developers increasingly need to demonstrate that computing facilities generate benefits extending beyond their immediate investors.
The financial consequences could include longer permitting processes, additional environmental obligations and higher development costs.
The relevant distinction is between a temporary public controversy and regulatory action that materially changes a project’s economics.

THE EUROPEAN COMPETITIVE ASSESSMENT
Spain: The priority is protecting the readiness of its Gigafactory application, including previously established public financing, industrial participation and site documentation. No new binding financing or grid agreement was identified today.
France: Legrand’s results strengthen the evidence that French industrial suppliers can capture substantial AI infrastructure expenditure. No new EuroHPC award was identified.
Germany: The Bull–Schwarz Digits alliance announced November 3 remains strategically relevant, but no additional binding commitment was verified today.
Italy: The immediate priority is completion of consortium arrangements, national financing commitments and the procurement submission. No new confirmed project-specific financial close was identified.
Nordics: Skanska’s comments suggest increasing selectivity in construction contracting. No new Nordic Gigafactory commissioning or grid-connection milestone was confirmed today.

KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS AND BOARDS
First — Europe’s Gigafactory procurement is approaching an execution test. The latest EuroHPC instructions require immediate attention from participating consortia.
Second — Industrial suppliers are demonstrating tangible AI-related revenue growth. Legrand’s results are a more direct indication of present commercial activity than many announced computing campuses.
Third — Financing quality is becoming a competitive differentiator. Skanska’s selectivity suggests that established customers and credible funding structures increasingly matter.
Fourth — Regulatory and community acceptance belong in infrastructure valuations. Project-development risk extends beyond power rights and construction costs.
Fifth — Europe must connect infrastructure investment with Industrial AI demand. The strategic objective is not simply additional computing capacity, but greater European productivity and sustainable economic returns.
THE CLOSING VERDICT
November 5 reveals a fundamental distinction in the AI capital cycle.
Demand for AI infrastructure remains substantial.
European industrial suppliers are demonstrating that the cycle can produce significant revenues and attractive operating margins.
Yet the firms developing computing infrastructure face increasingly demanding financing, procurement and execution requirements.
This divergence could reshape capital allocation.
Investors may become more selective toward speculative development pipelines while continuing to favor suppliers with demonstrated profitability and operators with contracted demand.
For Europe, the strategic challenge is to convert this industrial momentum into sovereign computing capabilities that generate measurable economic value.
The ultimate measure of success will not be the number of Gigafactories announced, but the amount of productive intelligence Europe can finance, deploy and monetize.
VELOCITY INSIGHT
The AI Supercycle is still expanding. The next financial advantage belongs to those who can transform infrastructure demand into executable investment and durable cash flow.
