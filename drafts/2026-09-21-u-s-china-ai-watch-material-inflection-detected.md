The thesis needs to be upgraded: China is no longer challenging only the economics of the frontier. It has begun touching the frontier itself — while still attacking its price far more aggressively.

The consequential development on September 21, 2026 is the convergence of capability, cost and actual usage. For months our framework has been clean: the United States leads frontier intelligence; China challenges the economics and diffusion of that intelligence. Today, the first half of that formulation has become less absolute.

On OpenRouter’s current benchmark view, Alibaba’s Qwen3.8 Max scores 53.4 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index — tied with Anthropic’s Claude Fable 5.1 and marginally above OpenAI’s GPT‑6 Astra at 52.7. The benchmark is only one composite measurement and should not be treated as proof of universal model superiority, but it is nevertheless a significant milestone: a Chinese model has reached statistical frontier territory on a broad independent evaluation rather than merely approaching it from below. 

That changes the strategic question.

The Great Split is becoming a Great Convergence

The United States still possesses the deeper frontier ecosystem: leading research laboratories, substantially more advanced accelerated computing, frontier HBM, hyperscale AI factories, world-leading cloud platforms and extraordinary capital formation. Artificial Analysis still shows multiple U.S. models clustered at the top across different effort levels and agentic workloads. 

But China’s position is evolving from:

“slightly weaker intelligence at dramatically lower cost”

toward:

“frontier-adjacent — and sometimes frontier-parity — intelligence at structurally lower diffusion costs.”

That is substantially more consequential.

Alibaba lists Qwen3.8-Max globally at about $1.65 per million input tokens and $4.95 per million output tokens, with a one-million-token context window. 

So the competitive frontier is increasingly two-dimensional:

Capability × Cost.

The U.S. advantage remains strongest at the maximum-capability end of the system.

China is pushing the capability/cost frontier outward.

⸻

A second signal makes today more important: this is no longer only Alibaba, DeepSeek or Moonshot

Shanghai-based StepFun has now released Step 5 Preview, a 600-billion-parameter sparse MoE model activating only 27 billion parameters per token, with a one-million-token context window and native vision input. StepFun’s own evaluations still show clear frontier gaps on difficult coding: on DeepSWE v1.1 it reports 67.7, versus 74.1 for GPT‑6 Astra and 74.0 for Claude Opus 5. That is useful evidence that U.S. frontier leadership has not disappeared. 

But the independent economics are striking.

Artificial Analysis measures Step 5 at an Intelligence Index score of 44, roughly 100 output tokens per second, and list prices of just $1 per million input tokens and $2.70 per million output tokens, with cached input at $0.05. It calculates an average cost of around $0.71 per Intelligence Index task. 

StepFun says full weights will be released on October 15; until that actually occurs, it remains an API model rather than an open-weight deployment option. 

The important implication is not StepFun itself.

It is the proliferation.

DeepSeek is no longer an anomaly.

China increasingly has a portfolio of labs pushing the same optimization function:

Alibaba → capability
DeepSeek → inference efficiency
Moonshot → agents / long context
Z.ai → cost-performance
Tencent → large sparse systems
StepFun → agentic intelligence per dollar

That breadth makes the Chinese challenge structurally more durable.

⸻

And now we have the diffusion evidence

The most important corroborating signal may come from actual developer traffic rather than benchmarks.

For the seven-day period ending September 20, OpenRouter ranks DeepSeek V4.1 Flash first by tokens processed at 15.8 trillion, up 219% from the preceding week. Z.ai’s GLM 5.3 Flash is second and Tencent’s Hy4 Preview third; Chinese-developed models occupy eight of the top ten positions on that particular public routing platform. 

OpenRouter itself explicitly warns against overinterpreting this: its rankings measure traffic routed through OpenRouter, not the entire AI market; token volumes are not equivalent to users, revenue, quality or preference, and long-context workloads can distort comparisons. 

But the directional evidence is nevertheless powerful.

DeepSeek leads OpenRouter’s latest weekly request-share ranking as well, at 25.4%, compared with 17.0% for OpenAI. Again, this is OpenRouter traffic—not global AI market share—but it is exactly the kind of revealed-use evidence we need to test the diffusion thesis. 

This creates an extraordinary combination:

CAPABILITY GAP ↓

PRICE GAP REMAINS LARGE

USAGE ↑

That is the inflection.

⸻

The thesis therefore needs one important revision

I would no longer frame the competition simply as:

AMERICA = FRONTIER

CHINA = DIFFUSION

The better formulation after September 21 is:

UNITED STATES — FRONTIER SYSTEM LEADERSHIP

Frontier research
→ advanced semiconductors
→ accelerated computing
→ AI factories
→ recursive AI R&D
→ global cloud infrastructure
→ next-generation capability.

CHINA — FRONTIER CONVERGENCE + ECONOMIC DIFFUSION

Sparse architectures
→ lower active compute
→ rapidly improving models
→ cheaper tokens
→ open / portable ecosystems
→ massive developer adoption
→ industrial deployment
→ more optimization.

The distinction is subtle but critical.

China may now be reaching the frontier model layer before it reaches parity in the industrial system that produces the frontier.

America can therefore still lead the AI frontier as an ecosystem even when individual Chinese models equal or surpass particular American systems on selected benchmarks.

That distinction will become increasingly important.

⸻

Why this matters for The Velocity Edge

This strengthens our core theory of competitive advantage in the AI economy.

The scarce asset may no longer be merely model intelligence.

If high-level model capability begins converging, competitive advantage migrates toward:

compute economics → inference economics → distribution → proprietary data → energy → physical deployment → capital formation → velocity.

The model becomes progressively less differentiated.

The surrounding system becomes more valuable.

That implies a profound redistribution of economic rents.

MODEL SCARCITY ↓

INTELLIGENCE CONSUMPTION ↑

INFRASTRUCTURE VALUE ↑

The economic winner may therefore not necessarily be the company owning the single highest-scoring model.

It may be the ecosystem capable of turning collapsing intelligence prices into the largest volume of economically productive inference.

⸻

Why this matters for the AI Supercycle

Today’s developments actually make the Supercycle stronger.

Falling intelligence prices do not necessarily reduce infrastructure demand. They can increase it dramatically through a Jevons-style effect:

better models
→ lower cost per useful task
→ more economically viable applications
→ more agents
→ more inference
→ more memory
→ more networking
→ more accelerated computing
→ more electricity
→ more AI factories.

The competitive battle among models may therefore become increasingly deflationary while the physical infrastructure underneath them remains profoundly expansionary.

That is the paradox at the center of the AI Supercycle:

THE PRICE OF INTELLIGENCE FALLS.

THE QUANTITY OF INTELLIGENCE CONSUMED EXPLODES.

CAPITAL FORMATION RISES.

⸻

The Velocity Insight

September 21 gives us a stronger conclusion than yesterday.

The defining divide is no longer simply:

American capability versus Chinese efficiency.

It is becoming:

AMERICAN SYSTEM SUPERIORITY versus CHINESE INTELLIGENCE COMMODITIZATION.

The United States still possesses the stronger overall system for producing the next technological frontier.But China is demonstrating that individual model capability can converge much faster than semiconductor capability, capital formation or infrastructure depth.

And once sufficiently powerful intelligence becomes abundant and cheap, the strategic competition moves downstream toward who deploys it fastest and into the largest number of economically valuable tasks.

That may be the most consequential Chinese strategy of all.

China does not necessarily have to reproduce every American advantage.

It has to make enough of those advantages economically non-exclusive.

Today’s evidence — Qwen reaching benchmark parity, StepFun broadening the low-cost frontier, and DeepSeek leading visible public-gateway consumption — suggests that process is accelerating. 

The updated Velocity equation

AI POWER = FRONTIER CAPABILITY × COST PER COMPLETED TASK × INFRASTRUCTURE × DISTRIBUTION × DEPLOYMENT VELOCITY

The U.S. still leads most decisively in frontier-system capability and infrastructure.

China is now challenging not only cost and diffusion, but increasingly the model-capability boundary itself.

That is a genuine material inflection for The Velocity Edge and makes the AI Supercycle broader, faster—and considerably harder to analyze through a simple U.S.-leads/China-follows framework.
