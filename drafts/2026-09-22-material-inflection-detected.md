MAS PUTS THE AI SUPERCYCLE INTO THE STRESS TEST

22 September 2026 | FDK

The most consequential central-bank development today comes from Singapore.

The Monetary Authority of Singapore has moved the AI debate from macroeconomic scenario analysis into explicit financial-stability stress testing. Its 2026 Financial Stability Review, released on 22 September, tests what happens if the AI investment cycle reverses sharply—and the result adds an important new layer to the Great Split. 

The new central-bank question is no longer simply:

Will AI raise productivity or inflation?

It is now also:

WHAT HAPPENS TO THE FINANCIAL SYSTEM IF THE AI CAPEX BOOM DISAPPOINTS?

MAS subjected Singapore-listed companies to AI-related revenue shocks of as much as 30% and interest-rate shocks of up to 400 basis points, calibrated to firms’ exposure to the AI supply chain. Under that severe scenario, around 32% of listed companies would become financially at risk, although they represent a smaller 16% of total corporate debt. The vulnerability is concentrated among highly leveraged, capital-intensive firms, companies dependent on working-capital financing and smaller businesses with thinner cash buffers. 

That distinction is important.

The problem is not presently a generalized solvency crisis.

It is concentrated fragility inside the AI capital-formation ecosystem.

⸻

THE AI SUPERCYCLE NOW HAS TWO CENTRAL-BANK TRANSMISSION CHANNELS

Until now we have focused primarily on the monetary channel:

AI investment
→ semiconductors + data centres + electricity
→ scarcity
→ higher prices
→ higher neutral rates / tighter policy.

MAS now formalizes the second channel:

AI investment
→ high valuations
→ debt + private credit + corporate leverage
→ earnings expectations
→ disappointment
→ repricing
→ tighter financial conditions.

MAS explicitly warns that a material shortfall in expected AI earnings or returns could produce a broader reassessment of valuations across public equities, corporate bonds and private-credit markets. It also argues that strong AI investment, together with fiscal pressures and recurrent supply shocks, is contributing to a persistently higher global cost of capital. 

This is an important evolution.

AI is becoming simultaneously a monetary-policy variable and a financial-stability variable.

⸻

THE BANKING SYSTEM PASSES — BUT THAT IS NOT THE END OF THE STORY

MAS’ stress test of Singapore’s systemically important banks is reassuring but revealing.

Under an adverse scenario combining a sharp correction in AI-related valuations, tighter financial conditions, higher credit losses and global recession, aggregate Common Equity Tier 1 capital would trough around 10.9%, remaining above MAS’ combined 9% capital requirement. 

So the immediate vulnerability is not bank capitalization.

The more interesting risk lies in transmission:

AI equity losses
→ corporate credit
→ private credit
→ working-capital financing
→ investment contraction
→ employment
→ bank credit quality.

This is exactly why the AI Supercycle is beginning to resemble previous capital-investment booms: the risk moves from the underlying technology into the financing architecture around it.

⸻

THE SECOND SIGNAL: AI CYBER AND CONCENTRATION RISK ARE BECOMING SYSTEMIC

MAS’ 2026 survey of financial-sector chief risk officers provides another significant signal.

Around 80% cited cyber and operational risks—including AI-assisted attacks and dependence on critical third-party providers—as major concerns. 57% identified financial-market stresses, including AI-driven market fragilities, as a major risk category. 

This introduces another causality chain:

AI adoption
→ common models + common cloud infrastructure
→ technological concentration
→ correlated operational failures
→ systemic financial risk.

This mirrors concerns already being raised by the ECB, Bank of England and BIS.

The architecture of financial stability is therefore changing from:

institution-specific risk

towardcommon-infrastructure risk.

That is particularly important as agentic AI enters payments, trading, credit analysis and fraud prevention.

⸻

THE BANK OF ENGLAND HAS ALREADY IDENTIFIED THE INFLATION SIDE

MAS’ financial-stability warning connects directly with the monetary-policy signal coming from the Bank of England.

In its 17 September 2026 meeting, the MPC explicitly noted that strong global demand for AI-related technology goods and primary materials was creating price pressures. Three members who preferred a 25-basis-point rate increase specifically cited AI supply constraints, alongside energy and food pressures, as part of the upside inflation risk. 

Put the two developments together:

BANK OF ENGLAND

AI CapEx → physical scarcity → inflation.

MAS

AI CapEx → leverage + valuations → financial-stability risk.

These are not contradictory.

They are two phases of the same cycle.

⸻

THE CENTRAL-BANK AI REACTION FUNCTION IS EXPANDING

We can now define four separate AI transmission mechanisms central banks increasingly need to monitor:

1. MONETARY CHANNEL
AI CapEx → scarcity → inflation → rates.

2. PRODUCTIVITY CHANNEL
Industrial AI → productivity → unit-cost deflation → lower inflation.

3. FINANCIAL CHANNEL
AI investment → leverage → valuations → repricing → financial conditions.

4. INFRASTRUCTURE CHANNEL
AI concentration → cloud / models / energy / cyber dependencies → systemic operational risk.

The implication is powerful:

CENTRAL BANKS CAN NO LONGER ANALYSE AI THROUGH THE INFLATION FORECAST ALONE.

They increasingly need a combined:

Monetary Policy + Macroprudential + Supervisory + Infrastructure framework.

⸻

INVESTOR IMPLICATIONS

The MAS stress test changes how the AI trade should be evaluated.

The question is no longer merely whether AI revenues justify current equity multiples.

Investors increasingly need to ask:

How much leverage sits behind the AI investment chain?

Who finances the data centres, power infrastructure and semiconductor capacity?

How much exposure has migrated into private credit?

Which companies remain solvent if expected AI returns fall while financing costs rise?

The most vulnerable segment is therefore likely to be neither the frontier-model companies nor the banking system itself.

It is the layer between them:

CAPITAL-INTENSIVE AI INFRASTRUCTURE + LEVERAGED SUPPLIERS + PRIVATE CREDIT + WORKING-CAPITAL DEPENDENCE.

That is where the next credit differentiation should become most visible.

⸻

THE EU POLICY CONSEQUENCE

The lesson for Europe is immediate.

The EU needs AI infrastructure investment aggressively—but it should not reproduce the financing vulnerabilities that can accompany an uncontrolled capital boom.

European policymakers should therefore treat AI infrastructure as a single interconnected system:

**Compute

• Energy
• Grids
• Data centres
• Banks
• Capital markets
• Private credit
• Cyber resilience.**

Stress testing should increasingly include AI-specific revenue shocks, energy-price shocks, higher-for-longer rates and simultaneous valuation corrections.

The objective should not be to slow AI investment.

It should be to accelerate the transition from:

AI CAPITAL FORMATION

to

INDUSTRIAL-AI PRODUCTIVITY

before leverage and infrastructure bottlenecks become systemic constraints.

⸻

THE VELOCITY INSIGHT

Yesterday the decisive question became:

Who captures the AI productivity surplus?

Today MAS adds another:

WHO CARRIES THE BALANCE-SHEET RISK WHILE WE WAIT FOR THAT SURPLUS TO ARRIVE?

That completes the architecture:

AI EXPECTATIONS
→ CAPITAL FORMATION
→ INFRASTRUCTURE SCARCITY
→ HIGHER COST OF CAPITAL
→ LEVERAGE
→ PRODUCTIVITY OR DISAPPOINTMENT
→ CASH FLOW OR REPRICING
→ FINANCIAL STABILITY.

The AI Supercycle is no longer merely something central banks forecast. It is becoming something they must stress-test.

That is today’s material inflection.
