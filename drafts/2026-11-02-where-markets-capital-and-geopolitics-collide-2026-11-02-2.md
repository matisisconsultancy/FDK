THE BOTTLENECK HAS MOVED
From Computing Power to Capital Productivity: Why the Next Market Rotation Will Redraw the Geography of Economic Power
THE BIG TAKE — THE AI SUPERCYCLE IS NOT RUNNING OUT OF GROWTH. IT IS RUNNING INTO THE ECONOMICS OF GROWTH.
The most consequential development emerging from global markets this morning is a fundamental shift in the constraints governing the AI Supercycle. The decisive bottleneck is moving from access to computing capacity toward the financial capacity to transform computing into sustainable economic returns.
Three developments expose this transition.
First, global capital is becoming more selective. The Nasdaq suffered its worst monthly performance since April 2025, losing almost 7% in October. Yet US equity futures are recovering this morning, with Nasdaq futures advancing approximately 0.9%. Investors are not abandoning artificial intelligence. They are reassessing which companies can finance its expansion without sacrificing financial returns.
Second, Asia is revealing an increasingly uneven industrial transformation. South Korea’s semiconductor exports surged 259.4% in October, while its broader manufacturing PMI slowed to 51.3. China presents an equally revealing divergence: its official manufacturing PMI indicates contraction at 49.3, while the private RatingDog survey shows expansion at 52.0.
Third, the geopolitical risk premium is shifting. Brent crude has fallen more than 3% toward $110 as diplomatic contacts involving Iran continue. Yet the Strait of Hormuz remains vulnerable, and OPEC+ has maintained its December production targets. The energy shock has moderated this morning; the underlying supply-security problem has not disappeared. 
These developments point toward a larger structural transformation.
The Next Economy is no longer being shaped primarily by the speed of technological adoption. It is increasingly being shaped by differences in capital productivity, financing conditions and the ability of companies and nations to convert technological advancement into economic power.
The financial implications are substantial.
Goldman Sachs estimates that hyperscaler capital expenditure could approach $800 billion in 2026 and $1.2 trillion in 2027. These are forecasts, not realized expenditure.
At the same time, corporate earnings growth is expected to moderate.
The result is a growing tension between the scale of technological investment and the incremental financial returns required to sustain it. 
This is where the next market rotation begins.
Our central investment thesis: capital will increasingly migrate from technological capacity toward capital productivity, from indiscriminate AI exposure toward measurable economic returns, and from financially constrained economies toward those capable of mobilizing investment without compromising long-term resilience.
The Great Split is entering a new phase.
It is no longer simply separating technological leaders from laggards.
It is separating economies and corporations that can turn acceleration into financial power from those that increasingly finance acceleration without capturing its full economic value.

THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
Is the AI Supercycle creating a new era of productive capital formation—or an increasingly expensive infrastructure economy in which the companies financing technological progress capture less value than the technologies themselves create?
The distinction is decisive.
In the first scenario, accelerated computing reduces production costs, raises productivity and expands corporate profitability across the economy.
In the second, the financial benefits remain concentrated among scarce suppliers and dominant platforms, while a growing share of infrastructure investors experiences declining marginal returns.
Both developments can occur simultaneously.
The central investment risk is not that AI fails technologically.It is that technological success becomes increasingly disconnected from the financial returns earned by the capital supporting it.

III. FIVE SIGNALS SHAPING THE NEXT ECONOMY
1 | ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE — THE NEXT MARKET LEADERS WILL BE SELECTED BY CAPITAL PRODUCTIVITY
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
Global technology markets are recovering modestly this morning after October’s substantial correction.
Nasdaq futures have advanced approximately 0.9%.Investors are awaiting Apple’s fourth-quarter earnings later today.
Analysts expect approximately $113 billion in revenue and earnings per share of $2.00.
Apple has become a relative source of stability because its business model requires substantially less direct data-centre investment than the largest cloud infrastructure providers.
Analysts expect gross margins near 47–48%, with memory and component costs becoming increasingly important.
Apple’s results will be released after the US market close, rather than during today’s European trading session. 
VELOCITY INTERPRETATION
The market is beginning to distinguish three different forms of competitive advantage.
Technological advantage: The ability to develop superior AI capabilities.
Infrastructure advantage: The ability to deploy computing capacity at scale.
Financial advantage: The ability to convert those capabilities into sustainable earnings and free cash flow.
These advantages do not necessarily coincide.
A company can possess substantial technological capabilities but generate insufficient returns on the capital required to deploy them.
Conversely, businesses capable of deploying AI through existing platforms and customer relationships may generate attractive returns without proportionately expanding their physical infrastructure.
This introduces a new competitive hierarchy.
The next winners may not be the companies controlling the greatest amount of computing capacity, but those generating the highest economic value from each unit of capital and computing consumed.
MARKET GUIDANCE
Investors should increasingly distinguish among three categories.
Companies providing scarce technology retain potential pricing power.
Companies financing infrastructure face greater capital-intensity and utilization risks.
Companies deploying AI productively may capture an increasing share of downstream economic value.
Our call: maintain exposure to the AI Supercycle, but progressively favour businesses demonstrating stronger incremental returns on invested capital, operating leverage and free-cash-flow conversion.
The decisive indicator is whether AI-related incremental profits begin growing faster than the capital employed to generate them.

2 | ASIA — THE GREAT SPLIT IS DEVELOPING INSIDE THE WORLD’S INDUSTRIAL ENGINE
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
China’s private RatingDog manufacturing PMI reached 52.0 in October, slightly below September’s 52.1.
The survey exceeded expectations and indicated that factory output expanded at its fastest pace in six months.
But export-order growth slowed, pricing competition intensified and business confidence declined to its lowest level in nine months.
China’s official manufacturing PMI, meanwhile, fell to 49.3.
The contrasting readings partly reflect differences in survey coverage and company composition. They should not be interpreted as evidence that one survey is necessarily incorrect. 
South Korea offers a second example.
Semiconductor exports surged 259.4% in October.
Yet its broader manufacturing PMI slowed to 51.3, an eight-month low.
Taiwan’s PMI remained expansionary at 54.9, while Japan’s manufacturing PMI stood at 53.3. 
VELOCITY INTERPRETATION
The underlying message is significant.
The AI Supercycle is creating extraordinary growth in particular industrial segments without generating equivalent acceleration across the broader manufacturing economy.
This has three consequences.
First, semiconductor demand and conventional manufacturing demand are becoming less synchronized.
Second, technological scarcity is concentrating economic rents within increasingly specialized supply chains.
Third, countries with strong AI-related export sectors can experience substantial external surpluses while other domestic industries face weaker demand.
The process can become self-reinforcing.
Profitable technology exporters generate cash flow.
Those profits support additional investment.
New investment strengthens technological capabilities.
Stronger capabilities reinforce pricing power.But the broader economy may not benefit proportionately unless capital and productivity gains spread into other industries.
SOUTH KOREA’S NEW STRATEGIC ADVANTAGE
A consequential development emerged this morning.
South Korea’s government is proposing a record 2027 budget of approximately ₩820.9 trillion, including ₩21.3 trillion for AI-related investment—nearly double its 2026 allocation.
The programme includes semiconductors, physical AI and data centres.
A proposed Future Response Fund is intended to support broader industrial development, education and regional opportunities.
The measures still require parliamentary approval. 
The hidden pattern is that South Korea is attempting to convert technological scarcity into a broader national investment advantage.
That is a fundamentally different strategic position from economies forced to increase borrowing merely to maintain existing industrial capacity.
MARKET GUIDANCE
Maintain selective exposure to advanced semiconductor manufacturing, memory, packaging and computing efficiency.
But distinguish these opportunities from the broader industrial economy.
Watch the relationship among semiconductor exports, manufacturing PMIs, industrial employment and capital expenditure.
Our call: the next Asian winners will be the economies capable of converting concentrated technological profits into diversified productivity gains.

3 | ENERGY AND GEOPOLITICS — A FALLING OIL PRICE DOES NOT MEAN THE RISK PREMIUM HAS DISAPPEARED
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
Brent crude declined approximately 3.3% to $109.70 per barrel during Asian trading.
The decline followed reports of continuing mediation involving Iranian and Qatari representatives.
However, attacks on shipping in the Gulf remain a concern, and no comprehensive settlement has been confirmed.
OPEC+ maintained December output targets at its November 1 meeting.
The wider group is expected to address its 2027 production framework on November 29.
October’s oil-price increases were substantial, leaving energy costs elevated despite today’s correction. 
VELOCITY INTERPRETATION
The significance extends beyond oil prices.
Energy costs influence inflation.
Inflation influences monetary policy.
Monetary policy affects the cost of financing productive investment.
Investment conditions determine how quickly economies can deploy new technologies.
The transmission is:
Energy Security → Inflation → Cost of Capital → Productive Investment → Competitiveness.
The AI Supercycle intensifies the importance of this relationship because accelerated computing requires substantial electricity infrastructure.
The strategic contradiction is clear.
AI promises future productivity improvements.
But the infrastructure required to generate those improvements may increase near-term demand for energy, equipment and capital.
If geopolitical disruptions remain persistent, the cost of building technological capacity can rise before its productivity benefits materialize.
MARKET GUIDANCE
Today’s oil correction may provide temporary support for energy-intensive industrial companies, airlines and transportation operators.
But investors should avoid treating diplomatic negotiations as confirmed supply normalization.
The critical indicators are Brent’s forward curve, tanker security, European gas prices and inflation expectations.
Our call: energy efficiency, grid modernization and reliable power infrastructure remain strategically attractive, even if oil prices retreat further.
The enduring opportunity is not simply exposure to high energy prices.
It is investment in technologies capable of reducing the energy intensity of economic growth.

4 | EUROPE — THE NEXT GREAT SPLIT WILL BE DETERMINED BY THE COST OF PRODUCTIVE CAPITAL
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
Fitch revised France’s sovereign outlook from stable to negative on October 30.
The rating itself was affirmed.
The agency cited weaker economic growth, higher borrowing costs, a deteriorating fiscal outlook and political difficulties surrounding consolidation.
It projects French public debt approaching 124% of GDP by 2028.The latest Eurostat figures reveal increasing differences in economic performance.
Eurozone GDP expanded 0.4% in the third quarter.
France contracted 0.1%.
Spain expanded 0.6%.
Italy recorded no quarterly growth.
The estimates remain preliminary and may be revised. 
The ECB’s October lending survey adds another dimension.
A net 9% of eurozone banks reported tighter corporate lending standards.
In France, the corresponding figure reached 27%.
Italian banks reported net easing, while Spanish banks reported broadly unchanged standards.
The ECB maintained its deposit rate at 2.50% on October 29, acknowledging that higher energy prices and tighter financing conditions create risks for both inflation and growth. 
VELOCITY INTERPRETATION
Europe is confronting an increasingly consequential divergence between technological ambition and financing capacity.
The European Union has substantial industrial capabilities.
It also has ambitious investment programmes involving AI infrastructure, electrification, transport, defence and industrial modernization.
But these programmes must compete for capital within a monetary union experiencing increasingly different national financing conditions.
The underlying mechanism is:
Sovereign Credibility → Financing Conditions → Corporate Investment → Productivity → National Competitive Advantage.
The importance of this relationship is growing.
Companies do not make investment decisions exclusively according to technological opportunities.
They also consider financing costs, energy availability, regulatory predictability and expected returns.
Countries capable of providing competitive conditions may attract a growing share of industrial investment.
Countries facing persistent sovereign and credit uncertainty risk losing future productive capacity.
THE EUROPEAN PARADOX
Europe possesses the technological and engineering capabilities required to participate in the Next Economy.
But it increasingly risks allocating capital less efficiently than competing regions.
The strategic danger is not that Europe stops innovating. It is that the continent becomes less capable of financing and monetizing its own innovation.
This distinction matters for investors.
A country’s current industrial capabilities do not necessarily determine where future capital formation will occur.
MARKET GUIDANCE
Differentiate European sovereign and corporate exposures.
Monitor French OAT–Bund spreads, bank funding costs, corporate credit standards and new investment commitments.
Spain’s stronger current growth is an advantage, but should be evaluated against project-specific returns and fiscal resilience.
Italy’s comparatively easier corporate credit standards may support investment, although stagnant output remains a constraint.
Our call: European competitiveness will increasingly depend on financing credibility, energy costs and investment execution—not industrial capabilities alone.

5 | BANKING, INDUSTRIAL POLICY AND MOBILITY — PRODUCTIVITY IS BECOMING THE NEXT INVESTMENT FRONTIER
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
A particularly revealing development emerged from Hong Kong this morning.
HSBC announced plans to establish an AI and emerging-technology institute in the city in early 2027, following the creation of a similar centre in Singapore.
The initiative reflects the growing importance of AI deployment within financial services.
Hong Kong’s monetary authorities report that approximately 80% of local banks already use AI or generative AI.
The Hong Kong Monetary Authority is also developing initiatives to support responsible deployment of AI agents in financial services. 
Meanwhile, Europe’s industrial transformation continues.
Battery-electric vehicles accounted for 22.7% of EU new-car registrations during January–September 2026, compared with 16.1% a year earlier.
Hybrid-electric vehicles represented 36.3% of registrations. 
The European Commission estimates that completing the trans-European high-speed rail network by 2040 will require approximately €345 billion in infrastructure investment alone. 
VELOCITY INTERPRETATIONThese developments reveal the next major source of competitive differentiation.
The first wave of the AI Supercycle rewarded the companies providing computing capacity.
The next wave may increasingly reward those applying AI to improve economic performance.
Banking provides an immediate example.
AI can improve risk assessment, operating efficiency, customer service and the management of financial operations.
But the relevant measure of success is not the number of AI applications deployed.
It is whether those applications improve the cost-to-income ratio, risk-adjusted returns and capital efficiency.
The same principle applies to industry and mobility.
Electrification can increase adoption without guaranteeing manufacturer profitability.
High-speed rail investment can expand infrastructure without automatically improving operating efficiency or financial sustainability.
Digital technology creates value when it improves safety, reliability, utilization and lifecycle economics.
The next investment frontier is the productivity of existing capital—not merely the accumulation of additional capital.
MARKET GUIDANCE
Look for companies demonstrating measurable operational improvements.
In banking, monitor costs, credit quality and returns on tangible equity.
In industry, monitor automation-driven margin improvements and capital efficiency.
In mobility, prioritize reliable infrastructure, intelligent operations, predictive maintenance and higher asset utilization, while treating safety as an essential requirement rather than a tradeable financial objective.
Our call: the next market rotation may increasingly favour productive users of AI rather than exclusively its infrastructure suppliers.

IV. BEYOND THE HEADLINES — FOUR HIDDEN PATTERNS
FIRST — THE NEXT FINANCIAL SHOCK COULD OCCUR DURING TECHNOLOGICAL ACCELERATION
This is the central paradox.
AI demand may remain strong.
Computing investment may continue expanding.
Semiconductor suppliers may continue reporting exceptional profits.
Yet equity valuations and infrastructure financing conditions can deteriorate if marginal returns fail to justify incremental investment.
The next correction may therefore be driven by the financial economics of AI success rather than a collapse in technological demand.
The critical indicators are free-cash-flow conversion, infrastructure utilization, depreciation and corporate credit spreads.
SECOND — THE GLOBAL ECONOMY IS BECOMING LESS SYNCHRONIZED AS ITS TECHNOLOGIES BECOME MORE INTERCONNECTED
South Korea’s semiconductor exports are accelerating.
China’s broader industrial economy remains uneven.
Europe is experiencing differentiated credit conditions.
The United States continues supporting extraordinary technology investment.
These developments indicate a growing distinction between technological interdependence and economic convergence.
The Great Split may produce a more integrated technological system alongside a more fragmented economic and financial system.
That combination could increase both competitive concentration and market volatility.
THIRD — THE COST OF CAPITAL IS BECOMING A DETERMINANT OF NATIONAL COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE
Technological leadership requires investment.
Investment requires financing.
Financing conditions depend partly on sovereign credibility, institutional quality and capital-market depth.
Countries possessing strong technological capabilities but persistently expensive financing may struggle to deploy those capabilities at scale.
The emerging competitive divide is between economies capable of financing productive acceleration and those forced to ration it.
This creates a potentially self-reinforcing advantage for financially resilient economies.
FOURTH — VALUE MIGRATION MAY BE FOLLOWED BY A NEW WAVE OF CAPITAL CONCENTRATION
As AI infrastructure expands, the distribution of economic rents may change.
Scarce semiconductor suppliers currently capture substantial profits.
Infrastructure operators absorb major capital commitments.Over time, increasingly capable software and industrial applications may capture greater value as computing becomes more accessible.
But the winners may then reinvest those profits to extend their competitive advantages.
The resulting cycle is:
Accelerated Computing → Token Abundance → Higher Velocity → The Great Split → Value Migration → Capital Concentration → Renewed Acceleration.
The fundamental implication is that technological acceleration can reinforce financial concentration unless competitive entry, diffusion and productivity growth distribute its benefits more broadly.

THE FINAL TAKE — THE NEW ECONOMICS OF COMPETITIVE POWER
The opening of November reveals a more fundamental transformation than a conventional correction in technology valuations.
The AI Supercycle is continuing to accelerate.
The demand for computing remains substantial.
Semiconductor supply chains are generating extraordinary profits.
Financial institutions are expanding their deployment of artificial intelligence.
Governments are increasing investment in technological infrastructure.Yet the financial conditions supporting that transformation are becoming increasingly differentiated.
The United States is confronting the enormous capital requirements of accelerated computing.
South Korea is attempting to transform semiconductor leadership into a broader national investment advantage.
China is navigating the divergence between technological capabilities and weaker parts of its industrial economy.
Europe faces a more difficult challenge: financing technological and industrial transformation amid sovereign uncertainty, elevated energy costs and fragmented credit conditions.
These developments are connected by a single economic mechanism.
The capacity to innovate is no longer sufficient. The ability to finance innovation, deploy it productively and capture its economic returns is becoming the decisive source of competitive advantage.
This is the next evolution of The Great Split.
The first phase separated technological leaders from laggards.
The second separated companies capable of deploying technology at scale.
The third is separating those capable of generating sustainable financial returns from those increasingly constrained by the cost of technological acceleration.
For investors, the implication is a structural reassessment of capital allocation.
For corporate boards, it is a renewed emphasis on financial returns and productive investment.
For policymakers, it is the necessity of ensuring that national financing systems can support the transformation required for long-term competitiveness.
The consequences may extend across global financial markets.
Capital may migrate toward the companies generating the greatest economic returns from AI.
Industrial investment may become more concentrated in economies offering competitive financing and energy conditions.
And technological leadership may become increasingly self-reinforcing where superior profitability allows continued investment in the next generation of capabilities.
The next hierarchy of economic power will therefore be determined not simply by who possesses the most advanced technologies, but by who can continuously transform those technologies into productivity, cash flow and financial strength.
THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
The bottleneck has moved. The decisive scarcity in the Next Economy is no longer computing power alone. It is the capacity to turn technological acceleration into sustainable economic returns.
The next market leaders will not be determined by who builds the most capacity—but by who captures the greatest value from it.
