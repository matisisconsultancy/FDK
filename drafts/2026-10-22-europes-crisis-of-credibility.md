When Sovereign Spreads Become a Test of Political Legitimacy—and the Future of the European Union
Europe does not face an inevitable sovereign-debt crisis. It faces something potentially more consequential: a progressive erosion of the credibility of its political commitments, fiscal institutions and capacity to deliver economic renewal. The response must go beyond austerity, monetary intervention and another appeal for European solidarity.
THE CENTRAL ARGUMENT
In July 2012, Mario Draghi’s commitment to preserve the euro changed the course of Europe’s sovereign-debt crisis. His intervention succeeded not because it immediately resolved the structural weaknesses of European economies, but because it restored confidence in the institutional determination to prevent the monetary union from disintegrating.
Fourteen years later, Europe faces a different test.
On October 22, 2026, France’s ten-year government-bond yield reached approximately 5.14 percent, its highest level in almost a quarter-century. The spread over German Bunds widened beyond 170 basis points, reaching territory last observed during the sovereign-debt crisis of 2011. France—long considered one of the pillars of European financial stability—has become the principal source of renewed sovereign anxiety.
Yet the real significance of these numbers lies elsewhere.
Europe is discovering that membership in a monetary union does not permanently insulate governments from the financial consequences of political paralysis.
A shared currency can eliminate exchange-rate risk between its members. It cannot eliminate differences in fiscal credibility, institutional competence or political willingness to honor long-term commitments.
The fundamental question facing Europe is no longer whether its institutions possess the instruments to contain another sovereign crisis. It is whether its governments retain the political credibility required to prevent one.
This distinction is critical.
The crisis of 2011 was driven by concerns about public debt, banking fragility and the possibility that the euro itself might fragment.
The current danger is emerging from a more complex intersection: fiscal deterioration, political polarization, social resistance, expensive energy, slowing productivity and unprecedented investment requirements associated with defense and artificial intelligence.
European governments need to spend more to protect their economic futures. Yet in several countries, they possess diminishing fiscal space and weakening political authority to make difficult choices.
The result is a contradiction that could define Europe’s next decade.
Europe must finance its transformation precisely when investors are becoming less confident in its capacity to govern that transformation.
The response cannot consist simply of another fiscal compact or a larger central-bank safety net.
It requires a new European doctrine of credibility—one that reconciles democratic legitimacy, fiscal responsibility, financial integration and productive economic growth.
I. THE MEANING OF CREDIBILITY HAS CHANGED
For much of the postwar era, the credibility of European governments rested on three foundations.
The first was the capacity to deliver economic prosperity.
The second was the ability to preserve social stability through public services, redistribution and the welfare state.
The third was the institutional expectation that governments would honor financial obligations while maintaining continuity across electoral cycles.
European integration reinforced these commitments.
The single market expanded commercial opportunity. Monetary union reduced currency fragmentation. The European Central Bank supplied an independent monetary anchor.
But the economic environment sustaining these arrangements has changed.
Population aging is increasing demands on pension and healthcare systems.
Defense requirements are rising.
The energy shock has increased costs for households and industry.Technological competition with the United States and China is forcing governments to reconsider their investment priorities.
At the same time, productivity growth has been insufficient to provide the fiscal resources required to meet all these objectives comfortably.
Governments are therefore confronting a distributional conflict.
They must choose among protecting existing spending commitments, supporting vulnerable households, financing new strategic priorities and preserving sustainable debt trajectories.
Political systems built around the gradual expansion of benefits are being asked to manage increasingly difficult trade-offs.
France represents the most visible manifestation.
Its public debt has reached approximately 119 percent of GDP, while its projected 2026 deficit is around 5.4 percent. Political fragmentation and social unrest have made the adoption of credible fiscal adjustments exceptionally difficult.
The IMF has warned that France needs a clearly specified multiyear consolidation strategy accompanied by reforms capable of improving its growth potential.
The problem is not simply excessive borrowing.
It is the absence of a sufficiently credible political mechanism for reconciling competing national priorities.
Fiscal credibility ultimately depends on political credibility. And political credibility depends on whether citizens believe that adjustment is necessary, fairly distributed and capable of producing a better economic future.
A government may announce an ambitious deficit target.
Markets will nevertheless discount that commitment if parliament cannot enact the required measures or if successive governments are likely to reverse them.
This is why sovereign spreads increasingly reflect the perceived capacity to govern—not merely the arithmetic of public debt.
II. THE SECOND-ORDER DANGER: WHEN POLITICAL RISK BECOMES AN ECONOMIC COST
The widening of sovereign spreads is often interpreted as a financial-market judgment on a government’s budget.
That interpretation is incomplete.
Persistent sovereign stress can reshape an economy long before it produces an outright fiscal emergency.
The first transmission channel operates through the financial system.
Government bonds serve as central assets in bank portfolios, collateral arrangements and institutional investment strategies.
When their yields rise sharply, their market values decline.
Banks may experience valuation pressure, higher wholesale funding costs and more demanding capital-market conditions.
Those effects can influence the price and availability of credit to businesses.
The second channel operates through private investment.
A company evaluating a factory, data center, transport network or energy project must compare expected returns with financing costs.
If sovereign uncertainty increases domestic borrowing costs, investment can become less attractive even when the company’s technology and operating capabilities remain competitive.
The third channel is political.
As governments pay more to finance debt, fewer resources remain available for visible public priorities.
Citizens experience deteriorating services or higher taxes without necessarily understanding the financial transmission mechanism.
Dissatisfaction can intensify.
Political opposition may become more radical.
Investors may then demand still greater compensation for uncertainty.
This creates a potentially destabilizing feedback loop.
THE SOVEREIGN CREDIBILITY TRAP
Political fragmentation and weak fiscal commitments
Higher sovereign risk premiums
Higher bank and corporate financing costs
Weaker investment, growth and fiscal revenues
Further loss of political and market confidence
The loop need not become a full sovereign crisis to inflict serious economic damage.
A decade of higher financing costs and insufficient investment could be more damaging to Europe’s long-term competitiveness than a short-lived bout of financial panic.The European Central Bank has already identified the risk that higher sovereign yields, changing investor behavior and financial-market interconnections could amplify shocks across the Eurozone.
This is the central danger.
Europe could avoid another sovereign-debt collapse while nevertheless experiencing a prolonged sovereign-financing penalty that gradually erodes its industrial future.
That outcome would be less dramatic than 2011, but potentially more consequential for the continent’s competitive position.
III. FRANCE IS THE TRIGGER, NOT THE WHOLE PROBLEM
France’s predicament deserves special attention, but a European response designed exclusively around France would misdiagnose the challenge.
The Eurozone is not a homogeneous financial system.
Its members have different debt structures, growth prospects, political institutions and banking exposures.
Nor should sovereign-risk rankings be determined by debt-to-GDP ratios alone.
A country with substantial debt but improving primary balances, stable institutions and credible refinancing arrangements may be treated more favorably than a country with lower debt but persistent fiscal uncertainty.
Italy illustrates the distinction.
Its public debt burden remains substantial, but recent market behavior has shown that investors distinguish its financing and political circumstances from those of France.
Spain benefits from stronger recent growth and significant opportunities associated with renewable electricity and digital infrastructure, although it remains exposed to political risk and European financial integration.
Belgium deserves attention because of its fiscal vulnerabilities and close economic links with France.
Germany and the Netherlands retain stronger sovereign financing positions, but they are not isolated from the consequences of a French crisis.
Their banks, pension funds, industrial exporters and institutional investors are integrated into the wider European economy.
The appropriate framework is therefore a hierarchy of transmission exposure rather than a simplistic ranking of supposedly safe and unsafe countries.

These are structural assessments, not a numerical probability ranking.
The objective should be to identify where sovereign stress is transmitted first and where it becomes most economically damaging.
For France, the danger originates in political and fiscal credibility.
For Italy and Belgium, it could emerge through changes in sovereign-risk perception.
For Spain, it could involve higher financing costs despite stronger domestic growth.
For Germany and the Netherlands, it could arrive indirectly through banks, institutional portfolios and reduced European demand.
The Eurozone’s vulnerability lies not simply in the weakest sovereign. It lies in the mechanisms through which uncertainty in one sovereign raises the cost of capital for others.
IV. WHY EUROPE CANNOT REPEAT THE 2012 PLAYBOOK
The European response must begin with a recognition that the ECB cannot substitute indefinitely for credible fiscal government.
The central bank possesses instruments to preserve monetary transmission and address disorderly fragmentation.
Its Transmission Protection Instrument was specifically designed to counter unjustified, disorderly market dynamics threatening the transmission of monetary policy.
But its use is conditional.The ECB considers compliance with the EU fiscal framework, debt sustainability, macroeconomic imbalances and the credibility of national economic policies.
This distinction must be preserved.
There is a legitimate role for the central bank in preventing self-reinforcing financial panic.
There is no legitimate long-term strategy in which monetary intervention permanently shields governments from the consequences of fiscal choices.
The ECB must distinguish between two forms of sovereign stress.
Fundamental repricing occurs when investors demand higher returns because debt trajectories, growth prospects or political commitments have deteriorated.
Disorderly fragmentation occurs when market dysfunction creates financing disruptions disproportionate to underlying fundamentals and threatens monetary transmission.
The first requires political and fiscal action.
The second may justify central-bank intervention within the ECB’s mandate.
Confusing them would weaken both fiscal discipline and central-bank credibility.
This is the lesson Europe must preserve from 2012.
Draghi’s intervention restored confidence in the institutional integrity of the euro.
But today’s challenge involves the capacity of elected governments to deliver credible economic choices.
Monetary institutions can provide time.
They cannot create the political consensus required to use that time productively.
The ECB can defend the integrity of the monetary union. Only governments can restore the credibility of the fiscal commitments on which its long-term stability depends.
V. A NEW EUROPEAN DOCTRINE OF CREDIBILITY
Europe needs a framework that treats credibility as an economic asset rather than a narrow accounting obligation.
The necessary response rests on six mutually reinforcing commitments.
1. Replace Annual Fiscal Improvisation With Binding Multiyear Credibility
Governments should establish credible four- to five-year fiscal programs with transparent expenditure paths, realistic macroeconomic assumptions and independently verifiable milestones.
The objective should not be mechanical austerity.
It should be demonstrating that public debt can be stabilized through policies that are both economically coherent and politically executable.
Fiscal programs should clearly distinguish existing spending commitments from new discretionary initiatives.
Major assumptions concerning growth, revenues, interest rates and implementation should be independently assessed.
Independent national fiscal institutions must have sufficient authority and resources to challenge unrealistic projections.
Where possible, the core framework should obtain parliamentary support extending beyond the governing coalition.
That does not eliminate political disagreement.
It reduces uncertainty about the continuity of essential commitments.
France, in particular, needs a fiscal agreement capable of surviving political transition.
Investors need confidence not merely that a government has announced a target, but that the institutional system can implement it.
2. Reform Fiscal Adjustment Around the Quality of Spending
Europe must stop treating all public expenditure as economically equivalent.
A transfer sustaining current consumption, an ineffective subsidy and a productive investment in an electricity network have different long-term effects.
But productive investment cannot simply be declared exempt from fiscal discipline.
Projects still require financing and can still destroy value.
The appropriate reform is to assess spending through transparent economic appraisal, execution quality and expected productivity gains.
Governments should protect high-return investment while reducing structurally inefficient expenditure.
Every major industrial program should identify its financing model, expected economic benefits, implementation milestones and exposure to delays.
An investment program that cannot demonstrate its contribution to productive capacity should not be protected merely because it carries the label of strategic autonomy.Credibility requires fiscal discipline, but growth requires investment discipline. Europe needs both.
3. Establish a European Sovereign-Risk Early-Warning Framework
European authorities should move from episodic reactions to market volatility toward systematic surveillance of the mechanisms through which sovereign stress can spread.
The framework should monitor seven indicators:
Sovereign spreads relative to Germany, including the speed and persistence of widening.
Debt-auction demand, refinancing calendars and changes in maturity structure.
Sovereign credit-default swaps and bond-market liquidity.
Bank funding spreads and exposures to sovereign securities.
Corporate borrowing costs and bank-lending conditions.
Fiscal-policy execution, parliamentary decisions and rating reviews.
Energy-price shocks and their effects on inflation, growth and public finances.
These indicators should be assessed jointly.
A widening spread accompanied by stable auctions, liquid markets and resilient bank funding may indicate differentiated risk pricing rather than systemic distress.
A simultaneous deterioration in sovereign liquidity, bank funding and corporate credit would be far more concerning.
The Commission, ECB, European Stability Mechanism and national debt-management agencies should coordinate analysis within their respective mandates.
The objective would not be to target a politically acceptable spread.
It would be to identify when country-specific stress begins threatening financial integration.
4. Complete the Banking Union and Build a Genuine European Capital Market
Europe cannot credibly aspire to technological and industrial autonomy while maintaining fragmented mechanisms for financing growth.
The ECB has emphasized the importance of completing banking union, developing a European deposit-insurance framework and advancing the Savings and Investments Union.
These reforms should become immediate political priorities.
Europe needs deeper cross-border equity markets, greater institutional investment capacity and more efficient channels connecting household savings with productive companies.
It also needs stronger risk sharing across national financial systems.
That does not require eliminating financial discipline or providing unconditional guarantees to sovereign borrowers.
It requires reducing barriers that prevent capital from moving toward productive investment because of institutional fragmentation.
An integrated capital market can improve Europe’s ability to absorb shocks and finance strategic projects.
It may also reduce the dependence of industrial transformation on national public budgets.
Europe’s strongest defense against financial fragmentation is not permanent central-bank intervention. It is a genuinely integrated financial system.
5. Build a European Productivity Compact
Fiscal consolidation will be politically difficult unless citizens can see a credible path toward stronger economic prospects.
Europe therefore needs a productivity compact linking national reforms with a limited number of continental investment priorities.
The priorities should be selected according to their ability to strengthen long-term economic capacity.
Reliable electricity infrastructure, industrial AI, advanced manufacturing, secure digital systems, defense interoperability and integrated mobility are strong candidates.
But project selection must remain disciplined.
Governments should prioritize measurable productivity improvements and the mobilization of private capital rather than public expenditure as an end in itself.
The Age of Pervasive Intelligence creates an important opportunity.
AI can potentially increase industrial output, improve infrastructure utilization and reduce coordination costs.
Yet those gains will materialize only if companies can finance deployment, acquire skills and reorganize operations.
The European productivity compact should therefore connect industrial policy with capital-market reform, skills and energy competitiveness.
Its governing objective should be straightforward:Every euro of strategic capital should be judged by the sustainable economic capacity it creates.
6. Restore Democratic Legitimacy Through Honest Economic Choices
The most difficult requirement is political.
European governments must explain the constraints facing their economies without reducing fiscal adjustment to an exercise in punishment.
Citizens are unlikely to support reforms they perceive as protecting financial markets while imposing the cost of adjustment disproportionately on households.
The legitimacy of fiscal consolidation depends on transparent priorities, fair burden sharing and credible improvements in public services and economic opportunity.
Governments must distinguish unavoidable structural choices from discretionary political promises.
They must demonstrate that benefits and sacrifices are distributed fairly.
And they must avoid portraying every protest as an obstacle to reform.
Social resistance often reflects genuine concerns about economic security, inequality and the distribution of adjustment costs.
Credibility cannot be restored through financial arithmetic alone.
It must be rebuilt through political institutions capable of negotiating durable compromises.
The most credible government is not the one announcing the largest fiscal adjustment. It is the one capable of sustaining a realistic adjustment without destroying the social consent required to implement it.
VI. THE NEXT SIX MONTHS: A EUROPEAN CREDIBILITY COMPACT
Europe’s response should follow a sequence designed to prevent financial stress from becoming a prolonged economic crisis.

The immediate test will be France.
Its scheduled Moody’s review on October 23, the evolution of its budget process and the 2027 electoral cycle will determine whether market doubts become persistent.
But the policy response must not be improvised around a single ratings decision.
A downgrade would not automatically produce a crisis. Nor would an unchanged rating resolve the underlying problem.
What matters is whether France can demonstrate that its institutions are capable of delivering a credible adjustment.
The broader European test concerns whether contagion develops through banking and corporate financing.
Markets will scrutinize Italy, Belgium and Spain for evidence that the French shock is changing sovereign-risk assessments.
Germany and the Netherlands will remain essential to preserving institutional confidence and supporting deeper European integration.
The appropriate European response is neither indiscriminate fiscal expansion nor generalized austerity.
It is credible national adjustment combined with more efficient common financing of genuinely European productive priorities.
VII. THE THREE SCENARIOS FOR EUROPE
Scenario One — Credibility Is Restored
France reaches an executable fiscal settlement. Sovereign spreads gradually stabilize. European banks maintain sound funding conditions, and capital-market integration advances.
This would provide an opportunity to redirect political attention toward industrial productivity and investment.
The critical requirement is that fiscal credibility improves through credible policies rather than temporary market reassurance.
Scenario Two — The Slow Erosion of Credibility
France avoids a disorderly crisis but fails to establish a durable fiscal consensus.
Spreads remain elevated.Other countries experience intermittent contagion.
European banks continue operating, but corporate investment increasingly reflects national financing differences.
This may be the most dangerous medium-term outcome because its costs accumulate gradually.
Europe remains financially intact while becoming less competitive.
Scenario Three — Sovereign Stress Becomes Systemic
French market pressure intensifies and begins materially affecting banking liquidity, corporate credit and other sovereign markets.
The ECB faces increasingly difficult decisions about monetary transmission and financial fragmentation.
Governments become compelled to accelerate adjustments under adverse conditions.
The risk of a negative feedback loop between austerity, growth and social instability increases.
This scenario is not inevitable and would require a further deterioration beyond today’s evidence.
It nevertheless justifies credible contingency planning.
VIII. FIVE DIRECTIVES TO EUROPE’S LEADERS
First, restore the credibility of fiscal commitments before markets impose harsher adjustments. Publish realistic multiyear plans and make their implementation independently verifiable.
Second, distinguish fiscal consolidation from productive investment. Reduce structurally ineffective expenditure while preserving projects that can demonstrate economically sustainable returns.
Third, prevent sovereign-risk repricing from fragmenting private capital formation. Strengthen surveillance, banking integration and the instruments required to preserve orderly monetary transmission.
Fourth, make productivity—not expenditure—the organizing principle of European industrial policy. Accelerated computing, Industrial AI, energy, defense and mobility investments must generate measurable economic value.
Fifth, rebuild the political legitimacy of reform. Credibility requires democratic consent, transparent trade-offs and an equitable distribution of adjustment costs.
These directives should form the basis of a new European Credibility Compact.
Its purpose would not be to eliminate differences among national sovereign yields.
Differences reflecting genuine fundamentals are an important component of market discipline.
Its purpose would be to prevent political paralysis and institutional fragmentation from imposing unnecessary financing costs on Europe’s productive future.
CLOSING VIEW
CREDIBILITY IS EUROPE’S LAST STRATEGIC RESERVE
Europe has survived financial crises, sovereign emergencies, a pandemic, an energy shock and successive geopolitical disruptions.
Its institutions have repeatedly demonstrated their capacity to preserve the integrity of the monetary union.
But institutional survival should not be confused with economic success.
A currency can remain intact while its member economies lose productive dynamism.
A government can continue financing its debt while progressively sacrificing investment.
And a continent can preserve its welfare institutions in the short term while weakening the productive foundation required to sustain them.
This is the danger now confronting Europe.
The widening of French sovereign spreads is not simply a warning about the finances of one country.
It is a warning about the relationship between democratic legitimacy, fiscal commitments and long-term competitiveness throughout the Union.
Europe’s next crisis may not resemble 2011.
There may be no sudden threat of a country leaving the euro.
There may be no immediate collapse of major banks.
The more consequential danger is a gradual normalization of expensive capital, weak investment and declining political confidence.
Europe could remain financially stable while becoming structurally less capable of financing its own future.
That is the outcome policymakers must prevent.
The continent does not lack savings, industrial capabilities or technological opportunities.
It lacks sufficient integration among political decision-making, financial markets and productive investment.
The remedy is therefore institutional as much as economic.National governments must restore confidence in their capacity to honor commitments.
European institutions must complete the mechanisms through which capital can move efficiently across the Union.
And political leaders must articulate a credible vision of economic renewal that citizens can support.
The European project has always depended on transforming shared vulnerability into common institutional strength.
That principle must now be applied to sovereign credibility.
The future of Europe will not be decided solely by the level of its public debt or the decisions of the ECB.
It will be determined by whether its political institutions can reconcile fiscal sustainability, social legitimacy and economic transformation.
The next European crisis will not begin when governments can no longer borrow. It will begin when their cost of borrowing prevents them from delivering the future they have promised.
And the next European renewal will begin when governments recognize that credibility cannot be purchased through central-bank intervention, manufactured through fiscal declarations or restored through austerity alone.
It must be earned through institutions that deliver.
THE FINAL PROPOSITION
In the twenty-first century, credibility is not merely the foundation of sovereign creditworthiness. It is a factor of production, a determinant of national competitiveness and the ultimate measure of political capacity.
Europe’s greatest vulnerability is not the absence of capital. It is the risk that its governments lose the credibility required to mobilize that capital productively.
The euro can be defended by institutions. Europe’s future can only be secured by governments capable of governing.
