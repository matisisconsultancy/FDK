DAILY INTELLIGENCE BRIEF | OCTOBER 13, 2026 | FDK
THE GREAT SPLIT BECOMES A FINANCIAL STABILITY TEST
The AI Supercycle Is Supporting Global Growth While Creating New Sources of Systemic Risk
THE BIG TAKE
The International Monetary Fund has delivered an important new assessment of the AI-native economy.
Its October 13 World Economic Outlook raises projected global growth for 2026 to 3.1%, from 3.0% in July, despite the energy shock associated with the Middle East conflict.
Yet its accompanying Global Financial Stability Report warns that the same AI investment cycle supporting economic growth could become a source of financial instability.
Three numbers capture the emerging contradiction.
3.1% — projected global GDP growth in 2026, supported by AI investment.
10% — the share of global investment-grade bond issuance now attributed to hyperscalers, compared with less than 1% in 2024.
32 countries — the number accounting for 95% of global growth, down from 54 in the IMF’s April assessment. 
The numbers describe different economic phenomena, but their interaction is consequential.
Artificial intelligence is supporting aggregate growth. Its financing is becoming increasingly important to global credit markets. And the geographical distribution of its benefits is becoming more concentrated.
The emerging risk is that AI becomes simultaneously a source of macroeconomic resilience and a transmitter of financial instability.
Today’s developments strengthen the Great Split thesis developed by NER-C.
The distinction is no longer simply between economies that invest in AI and those that do not.
It is increasingly between economies capable of transforming AI investment into productive economic capacity and those accumulating financial exposure without equivalent productivity gains.
The causal sequence deserves particular attention:
AI investment → concentrated earnings growth → greater capital requirements → rising credit-market exposure → financial sensitivity to AI profitability.
A parallel transmission operates through energy and sovereign debt:
Energy inflation → higher bond yields → increased financing costs → weaker investment capacity → greater sovereign vulnerability.
The interaction of these two mechanisms is becoming a defining characteristic of the next economy.

1 | AI AND ACCELERATED COMPUTING
China’s Computing Architecture Is Becoming a Competitive Alternative
THE FACT
A report released by IDC and covered by Reuters on October 13 reveals a significant shift in China’s market for data-centre AI accelerators.
Nvidia’s share of unit shipments fell to 34% during the first half of 2026, while Huawei reached 28%.
Nvidia shipped approximately 678,000 accelerators, a decline of 44% year over year. Huawei shipped approximately 572,000.
Total shipments reached approximately 2.01 million units.
Most notably, non-GPU accelerators overtook GPUs by shipment volume for the first time in the reported period.
The results reflect both American export restrictions and China’s industrial policy promoting domestic alternatives. They measure unit shipments, not computing performance or revenue market share. 
THE INTERPRETATION
China is developing an increasingly differentiated accelerated-computing ecosystem.
Its approach combines domestic semiconductors, specialized accelerators, alternative software architectures and growing investment in AI models.
This does not establish that Huawei has achieved technological parity with Nvidia’s most advanced hardware.
It does indicate that China’s domestic computing ecosystem is developing commercial scale under conditions of restricted access to American technology.
The competitive dynamics are changing.
The central competitive unit is shifting from the individual accelerator toward the performance and economics of the entire computing system.
That includes hardware, networking, software optimization, inference efficiency and electricity consumption.
WHAT CHANGED
The emergence of a Chinese computing ecosystem was already part of our structural baseline.The new evidence quantifies its growing domestic market presence.
The implications extend beyond China.
Greater competition in accelerators and memory technologies could compress hardware margins while making computing more affordable for downstream users.
That creates potentially opposing financial effects: lower returns for some infrastructure suppliers, but stronger economics for companies deploying AI applications.
MARKET TRANSMISSION
Asian semiconductor equities sold off sharply on October 13 as investors reassessed competition and the durability of exceptional AI-related profits.
Reuters reported intraday declines of approximately 8% for Samsung Electronics and 10% for SK Hynix, alongside a decline exceeding 6% in South Korea’s KOSPI index.
Reports of technological advances by Chinese memory producer CXMT contributed to the repricing. The commercial significance of those advances remains subject to verification. 
NER-C ASSESSMENT: Material new evidence of competitive realignment. High confidence in IDC’s reported shipment data; lower confidence in predictions of future technological parity.
Implications: Europe should diversify computing architectures and strengthen software portability. Companies must evaluate total cost per useful AI workload. Investors should distinguish accelerator demand growth from sustainable supplier pricing power. Policymakers should avoid equating technological sovereignty exclusively with domestic chip manufacturing.

THE INTERPRETATION
The results illustrate an increasingly important distinction within enterprise AI.
AI embedded within existing revenue-generating processes may commercialize more rapidly than large-scale enterprise transformation projects.
In marketing, AI can improve targeting, content production and campaign optimization within established commercial workflows.
By contrast, enterprise-wide transformation often requires substantial upfront expenditure, organizational redesign and uncertain implementation schedules.
This distinction is directly relevant to industrial AI.
Manufacturers, logistics companies and infrastructure operators face similar choices between incremental process optimization and comprehensive organizational transformation.
WHAT CHANGED
Publicis provides new company-level evidence of commercial demand for AI-enabled services.
It does not establish that AI caused the company’s entire organic growth.
Nor does it demonstrate a new acceleration in economy-wide productivity.
The broader productivity baseline remains unchanged: AI adoption is advancing faster than independently verified improvements in enterprise-wide profitability.
SECOND-ORDER EFFECT
Capital may progressively rotate toward AI applications with short implementation cycles, measurable savings and established revenue channels.
That would favor operational AI in manufacturing, logistics, energy management and business services over projects requiring large investments before measurable returns emerge.
NER-C ASSESSMENT: New commercial evidence. No verified economy-wide productivity inflection.
Implications: European companies should prioritize applications with measurable operating effects. Investors should monitor incremental margins, customer retention and free cash flow rather than AI-related revenue claims alone.Policymakers should reward industrial deployment and productivity improvements, not simply technology expenditure.

3 | CAPITAL FORMATION AND THE GREAT SPLIT
AI Credit Exposure Is Becoming Systemically Important
THE FACT
The IMF’s October 13 Global Financial Stability Report identifies increasing vulnerabilities arising from AI-related leverage, sovereign borrowing requirements and concentrated technology exposure.
Its most consequential finding concerns the growing weight of hyperscalers in investment-grade debt issuance.
Their share has risen from less than 1% in 2024 to approximately 10%.
This is a share of new issuance, not a claim that hyperscalers hold 10% of outstanding investment-grade debt.
The IMF also warns that adverse developments in AI profitability could trigger asset-price adjustments and financial spillovers. 
A NEW MARKET TEST: TENCENT
Tencent began marketing a four-tranche international bond offering on October 13 that could raise at least $4.5 billion.
The proposed dollar bonds have maturities of 10 and 20 years.
Initial pricing guidance was approximately 115 and 140 basis points above comparable US Treasury yields.
The company also offered offshore-yuan bonds with 10- and 30-year maturities.
Tencent’s first-half capital expenditure reached approximately 96.3 billion yuan, more than double the corresponding 2025 level.
The proceeds of the proposed bonds are designated for general corporate purposes, rather than exclusively for AI. Pricing and final issuance remain subject to market conditions. 
THE INTERPRETATION
The AI financing market is entering a more selective phase.
Established companies possessing substantial operating cash flows and diversified businesses may continue accessing long-duration financing.
Less-established developers dependent on repeated external funding could face materially different conditions.
This is consistent with the previously identified slowdown in AI-related borrowing and the withdrawal of the proposed Firmus IPO.
But today’s IMF findings elevate the issue.
AI financing is no longer solely a technology-sector question. It is becoming a macrofinancial stability consideration.
THE FINANCIAL TRANSMISSION
The danger arises from the interaction of three balance sheets.
The corporate balance sheet finances AI infrastructure.
The financial-sector balance sheet absorbs the associated debt and market exposure.
The sovereign balance sheet becomes more vulnerable if energy inflation, higher yields and weaker growth increase public borrowing costs.
An adverse AI valuation adjustment could therefore propagate through multiple financial channels.
The IMF’s stress testing finds that banks representing approximately 22% of global banking assets have weak capital positions under its adverse scenario, compared with 18% in the previous year’s exercise.
This is a hypothetical stress-test result, not a finding that those banks currently breach capital requirements. 
NER-C ASSESSMENT: Material new evidence. Systemic vulnerability has increased, but a systemic crisis is not the baseline forecast.
Implications: Investors should monitor AI-linked credit spreads, debt maturities and financing concentration. Corporate boards should test their capital expenditure plans against higher funding costs and weaker utilization. European policymakers should integrate technology financing into financial-stability surveillance.

4 | ENERGY, GRIDS AND AI INFRASTRUCTURE
The Energy Shock Threatens to Delay AI’s Productivity Dividend
THE FACT
The IMF’s October outlook projects global inflation of 4.7% in 2026, followed by 4.1% in 2027.
Its baseline assumes average oil prices near $96 per barrel in 2026 and $87 in 2027.
Under an adverse scenario in which worsening conflict drives oil toward $130 through the first quarter of 2027, the IMF estimates that global growth would decline by approximately 0.5 percentage point in 2027 relative to its baseline.
Inflation would rise by an additional 1.1 percentage points.
These are scenario estimates, not current market prices or realized outcomes.THE INTERPRETATION
AI infrastructure faces two simultaneous financial pressures.
Higher energy costs increase operating expenses.
Persistent inflation can raise financing costs and discount rates.
Their combination reduces the present value of future AI infrastructure cash flows.
The resulting financial constraint may appear before the anticipated productivity benefits are fully realized.
WHAT CHANGED
The IMF has quantified the potential macroeconomic consequences of a prolonged energy shock.
However, the underlying electricity infrastructure thesis remains unchanged.
The October 6 Google–Constellation agreement remains an important baseline, providing for 890 MW of incremental nuclear capacity and a separate 2,700 MW supply arrangement involving existing generation.
No newly verified comparable power agreement establishes a further infrastructure inflection this morning. 
EUROPEAN IMPLICATIONS
The economic value of AI infrastructure increasingly depends on delivered electricity costs, grid access, supply reliability and speed of commissioning.
Europe cannot assess competitiveness exclusively through the scale of planned GPU investment.
Electricity availability and the cost of capital jointly determine the economics of deploying productive intelligence.
For companies, power procurement and financing strategy must become integrated decisions. For investors, the important metric is operating computing capacity supported by reliable energy and contracted demand. Policymakers should accelerate grid connections while ensuring that large electricity consumers contribute appropriately to system expansion.
NER-C ASSESSMENT: New IMF downside scenario. Structural energy-infrastructure baseline unchanged.

5 | ECONOMIC GEOGRAPHY AND GEOPOLITICS
The Great Split Is Becoming Geographical
THE FACT
The IMF’s October assessment provides new evidence of increasingly concentrated global growth.
According to its analysis, 32 countries account for 95% of global growth, compared with 54 in April.
The IMF also reports that global trade increased 8.3% between 2023 and 2025, while AI-related trade grew 29%.
It estimates that without AI-related trade, overall trade growth would have been approximately 4.6%. 
THE INTERPRETATION
The AI Supercycle is reinforcing the advantages of countries integrated into advanced technology value chains.
These include semiconductor production, accelerated computing, specialized electronics, software platforms and related infrastructure.
The mechanism has important consequences.
Countries supplying the physical infrastructure receive an immediate industrial and export stimulus.
Countries investing primarily in imported equipment may experience an immediate increase in capital formation but only delayed improvements in productivity.
Countries lacking both technology production and significant industrial adoption risk falling further behind.
EUROPE’S POSITION
The IMF has upgraded its 2026 Eurozone growth forecast to 1.1%, from 0.9% in July.
The revision partly reflects AI-related investment, industrial participation in technology value chains and higher defence spending.
This is a positive development, but the Eurozone remains far from the technological scale of the United States and China. 
The European opportunity lies in developing complementary specializations.
Germany possesses industrial scale and advanced engineering capabilities.
Spain offers an expanding data-centre investment pipeline and attractive renewable-energy resources, subject to grid and permitting constraints.
France retains substantial technological and industrial capabilities despite fiscal and political pressures.
Italy possesses strengths in industrial automation, engineering, machinery and specialized manufacturing.
Nordic countries combine attractive power-system characteristics with digital capabilities.
These are strategic comparative advantages, not evidence that every country has already translated them into superior AI productivity.
WHAT CHANGED
The IMF’s updated growth forecasts and geographical-concentration analysis are new.The relative European investment geography has not materially changed since yesterday’s assessment.
NER-C ASSESSMENT: Material new macroeconomic evidence supporting geographical divergence.
The policy implication is clear: Europe needs to capture a larger share of the domestic value added generated by AI investment rather than competing only to attract infrastructure commitments.

6 | EUROPE 2030 POLICY WATCH
Europe’s Financing Model Faces a Decisive Negotiation
THE FACT
The Irish Presidency of the Council of the EU published its revised 2028–2034 budget proposal on October 10.
It envisages a total budget of approximately €1.62 trillion, representing an 8% reduction relative to the European Commission’s initial proposal.
Despite the reduction, the overall budget remains approximately 30% larger than the current seven-year framework.
The Presidency says the proposal would provide more than €400 billion, in current prices, for competitiveness-related funds.
The proposal is not an approved budget. 
THE INTERPRETATION
Europe’s investment debate is becoming increasingly consequential.
The problem is not simply whether Brussels spends more or less.
It is how effectively limited public resources mobilize private investment and productive capacity.
This is particularly important for AI gigafactories, energy infrastructure, advanced manufacturing and industrial AI deployment.
The next European Council meeting on October 15–16 will address the budget negotiations, energy prices and broader competitiveness priorities. 
WHAT CHANGED
The proposed reductions were already incorporated into yesterday’s baseline.
There is no verified new agreement.
The important development is the approaching political decision point.
SECOND-ORDER EFFECT
Greater dependence on national financing and private capital could accelerate divergence among EU member states.
Economies offering competitive electricity, reliable regulation, financing capacity and efficient permitting may attract a disproportionate share of investment.
Others may remain dependent on delayed or constrained European funding.
NER-C ASSESSMENT: Policy baseline unchanged. October 15–16 remains the next decisive catalyst.
Implications: Investors should differentiate European infrastructure opportunities by national execution capacity. Corporate boards should evaluate projects against realistic funding availability. Policymakers should prioritize cross-border grids, capital-market integration and measurable industrial productivity rather than investment announcements alone.

8 | FIVE SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS
The interaction of today’s developments suggests five broader changes in the evolutionary dynamics of the AI-native economy.
1. Semiconductor competition could accelerate AI adoption while reducing hardware profit margins.Technological diffusion and financial returns need not move in the same direction.
2. Credit-market differentiation could become more important than headline capital expenditure. Access to financing, maturity structure and cost of capital may increasingly determine which infrastructure projects reach commercial operation.
3. AI’s contribution to global growth may increase systemic financial exposure. Greater reliance on concentrated investment flows raises the consequences of a subsequent profitability disappointment.
4. Energy competitiveness could become an increasingly important determinant of sovereign resilience. Countries able to attract productive capital without excessive fiscal support may strengthen growth and debt sustainability relative to more vulnerable economies.
5. Europe’s competitiveness may increasingly diverge within the Single Market. Common regulation and monetary policy do not eliminate differences in electricity costs, permitting, industrial capabilities and access to capital.

9 | THE NEXT RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS
THE AI MACRO-FINANCIAL CONVERSION GAP
A New Framework for Understanding the Great Split
Today’s IMF findings suggest a new research hypothesis for the Next Economy Research Centre.
Hypothesis: As AI-related investment becomes a larger contributor to economic growth and credit issuance, countries and companies that fail to convert that investment into sustainable productivity and operating cash flow will become increasingly vulnerable to simultaneous technology and financing shocks.
This creates a potential divergence between three variables:
Capital formation: How much investment is mobilized.
Productive conversion: How much economically valuable output the investment generates.
Financial exposure: How much leverage, refinancing risk and market concentration the investment creates.
A country or company could perform strongly on the first variable while remaining weak on the second and increasingly exposed through the third.
That is the proposed AI Macro-Financial Conversion Gap.
The research agenda
NER-C could develop a comparative framework measuring realized productivity improvements, incremental domestic value added, cash-flow generation, debt-service capacity, financing concentration and electricity costs.
The research should distinguish hardware producers, infrastructure owners and downstream AI adopters.
It should also control for differences in national industrial structures and business cycles.
The central empirical question would be whether economies achieving stronger AI-related productivity improvements experience lower subsequent financial vulnerability than economies primarily benefiting from AI-related capital expenditure.
A second test could examine whether falling accelerator prices improve downstream productivity sufficiently to offset lower hardware-sector profits.
This would distinguish technological progress from the distribution of financial returns.
Research status: New, testable hypothesis. Not yet empirically validated.

FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS AND BOARDS OF DIRECTORS
1. AI has become a macroeconomic growth variable. The IMF’s improved global outlook confirms the increasing importance of AI-related investment to aggregate activity.
2.AI financing has become a financial-stability variable. Hyperscalers’ growing share of investment-grade issuance requires more rigorous assessment of leverage, refinancing and concentrated exposure.
3. China’s accelerated-computing ecosystem is becoming a material competitive force. Investors should distinguish semiconductor industry growth from the sustainability of individual manufacturers’ margins.
4. Europe must make productivity—not infrastructure announcements—the principal measure of AI competitiveness. Lower energy costs, faster permitting and industrial adoption should translate into verifiable economic returns.
5. The next market rotation could reward productive AI adoption over indiscriminate infrastructure expansion. The relevant investment distinction is increasingly between capital that builds technological capacity and capital that generates sustainable cash flow.

THE FINAL VELOCITY INSIGHT
THE NEXT GREAT SPLIT
The IMF’s October assessment marks an important development in the economics of artificial intelligence.
The AI Supercycle is no longer simply a technological transformation financed by global capital markets.
It is becoming a consequential source of global growth and financial exposure.
That creates a new form of economic interdependence.
The same investments supporting economic resilience today could amplify financial volatility tomorrow if profitability expectations are not realized.
The implications are especially important for Europe.
Technological sovereignty will not be achieved through computing infrastructure alone. It will require competitive electricity, productive industrial deployment, financing capacity and the ability to generate returns exceeding the cost of capital.
The next phase of the AI Supercycle will therefore be judged by two outcomes: how broadly the economic benefits of intelligence are distributed, and how sustainably the required investment is financed.
The decisive question is whether productivity can grow fast enough to justify the capital structure financing the AI revolution.
VELOCITY INSIGHT
AI is becoming too important to global growth to remain a purely technological investment—and too capital-intensive to escape the discipline of financial returns.
