AI investment is accelerating. Consumption, credit formation and employment absorption are not.
The Fundamental Question
Can the Next Economy sustain extraordinary capital formation if the broader economy progressively loses its capacity to absorb employment, credit and consumption?
The most consequential development of the week is no longer another record equity close or another AI infrastructure announcement.
It is the widening divergence between where capital is being created and where demand is being absorbed.
U.S. retail sales unexpectedly fell 0.6% in July, the first decline in nine months and the largest in 14 months. The GDP-sensitive control group fell 0.4%. Consumer sentiment dropped to 51.0 in August. Yet the S&P 500 remains less than 0.2% below Thursday’s record, while aggregate Q2 profits for S&P 500 companies are up roughly 52%, driven disproportionately by AI heavyweights.
China provides an even more extreme signal: July new yuan loans contracted by a record 340 billion yuan, despite falling real borrowing costs. Household lending contracted by 460.3 billion yuan.
Europe, meanwhile, grew 0.4% quarter on quarter in Q2—but employment increased only 0.1%. European corporate earnings are now expected to rise 23.4%, their strongest performance in almost four years.
Three economies. Three manifestations of the same underlying phenomenon.
Profits vs. wages. Productivity vs. employment. Capital formation vs. job creation. Industrial capacity vs. demand absorption.
This is The Great Split moving from financial markets into the real economy.
Because today is Saturday, the market references below use Friday, August 14 closes and frame positioning for Monday, August 17 and the week ahead.

Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy
The U.S. consumer is beginning to diverge from corporate America
Verified fact. July retail sales fell 0.6%, consumer sentiment deteriorated sharply, and July employment had already surprised on the downside. Yet corporate earnings remain exceptionally strong, with AI-heavyweight profits driving much of the S&P 500’s Q2 earnings expansion.
FDK interpretation.
This expands The Great Split.
The original market narrative was:
AI winners vs. non-AI winners.
It became:
profits vs. wages.
Then:
productivity vs. employment.
Now it is becoming:
capital formation vs. consumption capacity.
The significance is enormous.
The AI economy does not require household consumption to finance every new data center. Hyperscalers, sovereign funds, private credit, banks and infrastructure investors can continue allocating hundreds of billions of dollars even while household demand weakens.
That creates the possibility of something historically unusual:
an investment boom coexisting with a consumption slowdown.
⸻
China’s problem is no longer the availability of capital. It is the willingness to use it
Verified fact. China’s July bank lending contracted by 340 billion yuan, against expectations for a 45 billion yuan increase. Household loans fell 460.3 billion yuan and corporate loans fell 130 billion yuan. Outstanding loan growth reached a record-low 5.1%, even as real borrowing costs have declined.
FDK interpretation.
This is perhaps the most important Chinese number of the week.
China does not primarily face a liquidity shortage.
It faces a credit-demand shortage.
That distinction matters.
Lower interest rates cannot automatically create investment when households are deleveraging and private-sector confidence remains weak.
The transmission mechanism is breaking:
cheaper money ≠ stronger credit demand ≠ stronger consumption.
Yet industrial capacity remains formidable.
The result is the Chinese version of The Great Split:
industrial supply accelerates while domestic absorption decelerates.
That inevitably pushes excess productive capacity outward—toward exports, price competition and global industrial deflation.
⸻
AI capital formation is becoming increasingly detached from the business cycle
While consumer demand weakens, AI infrastructure keeps expanding geographically.
Verified fact. India’s Larsen & Toubro has secured an AI-factory contract worth as much as $1.57 billion from Together AI. The Chennai installation will host 10,000 Nvidia B300 chips, India’s largest deployment of its kind, supporting inference, training and fine-tuning.
At the same time, institutional investors are becoming far more selective. Reuters’ analysis of 6,371 institutional 13F filers shows 44% trimmed Magnificent Seven holdings while 42% increased them. Semiconductors retained a clearer positive bias, with 48% net buyers versus 34.5% net sellers.
FDK interpretation.
The signal is not that the AI trade is ending.
It is that ownership is migrating away from generic AI exposure toward industrial scarcity.
Capital increasingly wants:
**accelerated computing
memory
semiconductor equipment
networking
electricity
data centers
infrastructure finance.**
The market is beginning to distinguish between owning the AI narrative and owning the bottlenecks required to industrialize it.
That is The Great Value Migration.
⸻
Europe is beginning to produce more growth than employment
This deserves considerably more attention.
Verified fact. Euro-area GDP expanded 0.4% quarter on quarter in Q2, while employment increased only 0.1%. Year on year, GDP grew 1.0% while employment grew 0.5%.
At the corporate level the divergence is even more pronounced: STOXX 600 Q2 profits are expected to increase 23.4%, while European equities remain within roughly 1% of record highs despite Friday’s modest decline.
FDK interpretation.
This may be the first visible European expression of the productivity-employment Great Split.
The critical metric is becoming less:
How fast is GDP growing?
and increasingly:
How much employment growth does each incremental unit of GDP require?
Industrial AI, automation, energy infrastructure, software and capital deepening can increase output without proportionally increasing headcount.
That is economically positive for productivity.
But it has profound implications for wages, taxation, consumption, social policy and the political economy of technological adoption.
The real debate over AI therefore will not concern employment destruction alone.
It will concern the economy’s capacity for employment growth as production becomes more intelligence intensive.
⸻
The bond market is refusing to validate the easy-money interpretation
The front end sees weaker consumption and benign inflation.
The long end sees something else.
Verified fact. Markets now assign roughly a 67% probability that the Fed leaves rates unchanged in September, yet this week’s 30-year Treasury auction cleared at 5.216%, its highest yield since 2001. U.S. 30-year real yields are close to 3%, near an 18-year high.
Japan is moving in the opposite monetary direction altogether. Reuters reports the Bank of Japan could raise its policy rate from the current 1% as soon as September, with markets pricing nearly an 80% probability of a move.Strong AI demand, energy costs and yen weakness are among the inflation pressures cited by policymakers.
FDK interpretation.
The world’s most important financial split is becoming:
short-rate expectations vs. long-term capital scarcity.
The Fed may stop tightening.
That does not mean the cost of long-duration capital necessarily falls.
Governments, hyperscalers, AI infrastructure developers and energy systems are all competing simultaneously for capital.
That can keep real yields structurally elevated even as consumption slows.
The price of money is becoming less cyclical and more structural.

Hidden Patterns Beyond the Headlines
The first hidden pattern: demand is becoming the scarce variable
The AI debate began with scarcity of intelligence.
Then scarcity moved to accelerated computing.
Then to electricity, grids and capital.
The latest data suggest another scarce input is emerging:
economic absorption.
An economy can build enormous productive capacity.
But somebody ultimately has to absorb the output—through consumption, investment or exports.
China’s credit contraction and America’s retail slowdown are warnings that supply velocity can exceed demand velocity.
⸻
The second hidden pattern: capital formation can decouple from employment formation
The euro area provides a powerful early illustration: 0.4% GDP growth, 0.1% employment growth and 23.4% corporate earnings growth.
If this pattern strengthens, the conventional chain—
investment → production → employment → wages → consumption
—becomes less reliable.
The emerging chain becomes:
investment → accelerated computing → automation → productivity → profits → reinvestment
with a weaker employment transmission channel.
That is a fundamentally different economic architecture.
⸻
The third hidden pattern: policy is beginning to confront the absorption constraint
Britain’s EV policy is a useful example.
The existing mandate requires EVs to represent 33% of new sales in 2026 and 80% by 2030. The government is now consulting on options that could reduce the 2030 requirement to as little as 50%, citing consumer demand, infrastructure and supply-chain constraints. Battery EVs represented 27.4% of July registrations.
The lesson extends well beyond automobiles.
Industrial policy cannot indefinitely force deployment faster than markets can absorb the underlying technology.
Infrastructure, affordability and consumer economics ultimately matter.
⸻
The fourth hidden pattern: energy is becoming a tax on demand
The Strait of Hormuz was effectively at a standstill Friday: only two vessels were visibly tracked through it and no crude shipment was observed. Average U.S. gasoline prices are around $4.08 per gallon, 29% above a year ago.
Oil therefore operates simultaneously as:
**an inflation shock
a consumption tax
a margin shock
a monetary-policy constraint.**
That makes the energy shock especially dangerous when consumer demand is already weakening.
⸻
Likely Market and Competitive Implications
The strongest structural capital allocation remains toward businesses located at scarce nodes of the Industrial AI propagation chain: semiconductors, semiconductor equipment, memory, networking, electrical infrastructure, power generation, grids, data centers and infrastructure finance. Institutional filings still show a positive bias toward semiconductors even as enthusiasm for megacap technology becomes less unanimous.
But the next phase should produce much greater dispersion.
A company that merely announces AI spending is becoming less interesting than one that converts AI into measurable revenue growth, margin expansion, free cash flow or higher asset utilization.
For the United States, weakening consumer demand increases the relative attractiveness of investment-led sectors versus consumption-sensitive cyclicals. The S&P 500 finished Friday at 7,785.76, down 0.17%, while energy rose 1.4% and semiconductor shares weakened despite strong underlying AI demand.For Europe, the emerging allocation remains particularly interesting: defense, energy infrastructure, industrial automation, software with demonstrable productivity effects, selected financials, logistics and transport infrastructure. European earnings remain unusually strong, but higher oil prices remain the critical external tax on that recovery.
For China, the split argues for continued differentiation between advanced manufacturing/export capacity and domestic consumer or credit-sensitive exposures. Record-negative bank lending reinforces that distinction.

The Velocity Insight
The most important economic question of the AI Supercycle is changing.
It began with:
Who can create intelligence?
Then:
Who can finance accelerated computing?
Then:
Who controls electricity, grids and infrastructure?
The next question is more difficult:
Who will absorb the extraordinary productive capacity being created?
The U.S. consumer is weakening.
Chinese households are deleveraging.
European GDP is growing faster than employment.
Yet AI investment, semiconductor infrastructure and corporate profits continue to accelerate.
This is why The Great Split must now be understood as a propagation mechanism, not simply a market phenomenon.
AI investment → accelerated computing → productivity → profits → capital formation
is accelerating.
But:
employment → wages → credit demand → consumption
is not accelerating at the same rate.
If those two chains continue to diverge, the central challenge of the Next Economy will not be whether AI can produce more.
It will be whether the economy can distribute enough purchasing power, employment capacity and investment opportunity to absorb what AI enables it to produce.
That is the new frontier of The Great Split.
The scarcity of the last decade was productive capacity. The scarcity of the next may be demand capable of absorbing it.
