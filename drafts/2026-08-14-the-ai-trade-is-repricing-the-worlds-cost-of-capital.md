The AI Supercycle has escaped the technology sector. Hyperscaler borrowing is now lifting real yields, crowding corporate credit and forcing companies — and increasingly sovereigns — to compete with AI for long-duration capital.
Data cut: 07:46 CEST
The most important market signal this morning is not another record for the S&P 500.
It is the real interest rate.
U.S. 30-year inflation-adjusted Treasury yields are approaching 3%, close to an 18-year high. German and British 10-year real yields are around their highest levels in more than a decade. And one of the forces increasingly pushing them higher is the extraordinary quantity of capital required to finance the AI infrastructure build-out. Alphabet, Amazon and Meta have issued almost $220 billion of bonds in 2026, already more than twice their combined issuance during the whole of 2025.
This changes the unit of analysis.
The AI trade began by repricing:
semiconductors.
Then:
data centers, power, memory, grids and infrastructure.
Then:
corporate balance sheets and private credit.
Now it is beginning to reprice something far larger:
the opportunity cost of capital itself.
And that is the next stage of The Great Split.
Because the same AI companies generating extraordinary earnings growth are also absorbing an extraordinary share of the world’s incremental savings.
The result is a paradox.
AI can make intelligence cheaper while making capital more expensive.
That is the market dynamic to understand today.
⸻
The Fundamental Question
What happens when the technology expected to lower the cost of intelligence begins raising the cost of capital for the rest of the economy?
That question reaches far beyond technology stocks.
If the AI infrastructure boom structurally raises real yields, the transmission mechanism becomes:
AI CapEx → bond issuance → competition for savings → real yields ↑ → corporate funding costs ↑ → sovereign funding costs ↑ → investment selectivity ↑ → productivity divergence ↑ → The Great Split widens.
The economic impact of AI is therefore no longer confined to companies that own GPUs or models.
It is beginning to affect the discount rate applied to almost every productive asset in the economy.

The Five Signals Shaping the Day
The AI Supercycle Is Beginning to Move the Risk-Free Rate
Verified Fact
Inflation-adjusted borrowing costs have risen sharply across major developed economies. U.S. 30-year real yields are near 3%, while British and German real yields have reached roughly decade highs. Reuters identifies the combination of hyperscaler borrowing, large government deficits and reduced central-bank bond purchases as important contributors to the move. AI-related issuers Alphabet, Amazon and Meta alone have already sold almost $220 billion of bonds this year.
FDK Interpretation
This is a fundamental escalation of the AI propagation mechanism.
Until now, markets treated AI capital formation largely as a sector phenomenon.
That is no longer sufficient.
The relevant chain is becoming:
Accelerated computing demand ↑ → AI infrastructure CapEx ↑↑ → debt issuance ↑↑↑ → demand for savings ↑ → real yields ↑ → WACC ↑ → valuation dispersion ↑↑.
AI is therefore beginning to influence the denominator used to value the rest of capitalism.
This may ultimately prove more consequential than another trillion dollars of market capitalization in technology stocks.
The AI Supercycle is becoming large enough to change the price of money required to finance the Supercycle itself.
That is a feedback loop markets have only begun to price.
⸻
From Credit Risk to Relevance Risk
Verified Fact
BNP Paribas credit strategists argue that the extraordinary competition for high-quality capital created by technology-company issuance is beginning to affect credit outside the technology sector. Bloomberg data cited in a report published this morning show CDS spreads on companies including LVMH, Sanofi and BAE Systems have risen by more than 10% since year-end. BNP’s European credit strategist describes high-quality issuers as increasingly competing with hyperscalers for investor capital and says the effect could ultimately reach sovereign debt.
FDK Interpretation
This is where credit risk becomes relevance risk.
A financially sound company does not need to deteriorate operationally to face a higher cost of capital.
It merely needs to compete for funding against an asset class offering:
higher growth, greater strategic relevance, stronger AI exposure, or superior expected cash-flow resilience.
Capital markets can therefore penalize a company relatively, even when its absolute credit fundamentals remain intact.
The mechanism becomes:
AI exposure → expected cash-flow resilience → investor demand → credit spread → WACC → investment capacity → productivity → still greater AI relevance.
That is potentially one of the most powerful reinforcing mechanisms inside The Great Split.
Companies without a credible AI or productivity angle risk not merely slower growth.
They may eventually face a structurally higher relative cost of capital.

The Demand Side of the AI Trade Is Still Accelerating
Verified Fact
China’s SMIC reported Q2 revenue above $3 billion for the first time, with attributable profit tripling to $479.2 million. Wafer shipments rose 14% sequentially, average selling prices increased 5.7%, and utilization reached 93.7%. Management said AI-related demand remained strong enough to support price increases and additional capacity deployment.
South Korea’s KOSPI, a major financial proxy for the semiconductor cycle, is up about 1.8% this morning and nearly 11% for the week, while Japan’s Nikkei is around 1.5% higher.
FDK Interpretation
This is important because the capital-market warning is not being triggered by collapsing AI demand.
Quite the opposite.
It is being triggered because demand is so strong that the infrastructure required to satisfy it is consuming extraordinary quantities of capital.
That distinction matters enormously.
The risk is not presently:
AI demand failure.
It is:
AI capital-intensity success.
SMIC is another indication that the AI boom is spreading outward from frontier GPUs into the broader semiconductor system.
CPU.
Memory.Foundry capacity.
Networking.
Power electronics.
Optics.
Cooling.
Industrial components.
The propagation mechanism is widening.
The next phase of the AI trade may be less about who creates the frontier model and more about who owns the increasingly scarce industrial inputs required to deploy intelligence at scale.

The Fed Is Giving Equities Relief While the Bond Market Is Sending a Different Message
Verified Fact
July U.S. producer prices were unchanged, below expectations for a 0.2% increase. Combined with mild CPI and weak July payrolls, the report reduced expectations for a September Fed increase; markets now assign only about a 35% probability. The S&P 500 nevertheless closed at a record 7,798.99, up 0.65%, and the Nasdaq added 0.81%.
FDK Interpretation
The market is simultaneously pricing two apparently contradictory signals.
Short-end monetary pressure ↓
but
long-duration real capital scarcity ↑.
This is critical.
The market can be correct that the Fed will not tighten next month and still be wrong to assume that the cost of capital is falling.
The Fed controls an overnight rate.
The economy must finance:
AI factories, electrical grids, defense, energy security, industrial reshoring, infrastructure, and fiscal deficits
over decades.
Those are different capital markets.
The old equation was:
weaker inflation → lower rates → higher multiples.
The emerging equation is more complicated:
weaker inflation → Fed pressure ↓ while structural capital demand ↑ → real long-duration yields remain high.
This is why the next Great Split may run directly through the yield curve.

Europe Is Entering the Capital Competition at Exactly the Wrong Time
Verified Fact
Euro-area inflation stood at 2.9% in July, while an August 10–13 Reuters poll found 57 of 69 economists expect the ECB to increase its deposit rate to 2.50% in September. The euro-area economy expanded 0.4% in Q2, and economists upgraded their 2026 growth forecast to 0.8%.
At the same time, Europe must finance energy security, defense and infrastructure, all while competing with U.S. AI issuance for global savings. Reuters notes that these European investment priorities are important contributors to higher real yields on the continent. European equities remain near record levels, with Q2 STOXX 600 profits now expected to grow more than 22%, their strongest pace since 2022.
FDK Interpretation
Europe’s problem is becoming more urgent.
It must simultaneously fund:
defense, energy independence, electrical grids, transport infrastructure, industrial restructuring, and Industrial AI.
But it is entering this investment cycle at the precise moment that the global cost of long-duration capital is rising.
That creates a strategic choice.
Europe can ration capital across fragmented national priorities.
Or it can increase the productivity of capital itself by integrating projects, financing pools and industrial platforms at continental scale.
This is why European capital-market integration is no longer a technical financial-policy project.
It is becoming an industrial-security requirement.

Five Hidden Patterns Beyond the News
The Law of AI Propagation Has Reached the Discount Rate
The first transmission was easy to observe:
AI → semiconductors.
Then:
semiconductors → data centers.
Then:
data centers → energy and grids.
Then:
infrastructure → logistics, commodities and industrial equipment.
Now:
AI capital formation → credit markets → real yields → cost of capital.
The unit of analysis must therefore expand again.
The market started by asking:
Which AI stock wins?
It now needs to ask:
Which corporate cash flows, credit instruments, infrastructure assets, cities and sovereigns gain — or lose — when AI changes the global price of capital?
That is a much larger investment question.
⸻
The Great Split Is Becoming a Capital-Rationing Mechanism
High real yields do not stop all investment equally.
They stop marginal investment first.
And that has profound consequences.
Projects with compelling AI-linked productivity economics can continue attracting capital.Projects with weaker prospective returns will increasingly struggle.
Therefore:
higher real yields → greater capital selectivity → stronger companies keep investing → weaker companies defer investment → productivity gap widens → cash-flow divergence widens → credit gap widens.
The cost of capital does not merely reflect The Great Split.
It can amplify it.
⸻
AI Could Become Deflationary in Output and Inflationary in Capital
This is the macro paradox the market has not fully internalized.
AI can reduce the cost of producing:
software, research, analysis, design, customer service, engineering and eventually physical output.
But getting there requires extraordinary investment in:
accelerated computing, memory, power generation, transmission, data centers, cooling, construction and networks.
Thus:
Unit cost of intelligence ↓
while
aggregate demand for capital ↑.
This is entirely consistent.
Indeed, it may be the defining macroeconomic mechanism of the AI Supercycle.
⸻
Sovereign Capital Is Becoming a Strategic Arbitrageur of Duration
Sovereign wealth funds managing more than $15 trillion are increasingly aligning investment with strategic national priorities. An IE University study found tracked sovereign-fund investment spending jumped 91% to $404 billion, with roughly one dollar in three directed toward AI-related investments.
This matters because sovereign investors possess something increasingly scarce:
duration.
Private capital can become constrained by:
quarterly performance, fund maturities, credit spreads, redemptions and leverage.
Sovereign capital can potentially invest across decades.
That turns sovereign wealth from passive financial capital into an increasingly important instrument of:
AI strategy, energy strategy, industrial policy, and geopolitical positioning.
The countries possessing both cheap energy and patient sovereign capital may therefore enjoy a double comparative advantage in Industrial AI.
⸻
The Risk Premium Is Not Rising Where the Headlines Are
The S&P 500 is at a record.
Asian technology markets are rallying.
AI demand remains extremely strong.
Yet real yields are approaching multidecade highs, credit markets are becoming more crowded and geopolitical risk remains severe. Brent is around $87 even after Thursday’s fall, the U.S. says it could maintain its naval blockade of Iran indefinitely, and two UAE-owned vessels were attacked in the Strait of Hormuz on Thursday.
The mispricing may therefore not reside in the direction of equities.
It may reside in the low volatility attached to a system experiencing rapidly increasing capital and geopolitical stress.
Markets are rewarding growth.
Credit is beginning to price scarcity.
The bond market may be telling us something equities have not yet had to confront.

The Fundamental Analytics
The institutional dashboard now needs several new ratios.
AI Capital Formation / Global Savings Capacity
This measures how large the AI financing cycle is becoming relative to the pool of long-duration capital available to absorb it.
As this ratio rises, AI begins affecting the risk-free rate itself.
AI Earnings Growth / Real Yield
Strong earnings can tolerate rising real yields.
Weak earnings cannot.
The tipping point for equity valuation will occur when the marginal increase in the discount rate begins exceeding the marginal increase in expected cash flows.
AI Cash Flow / AI Fixed Commitments
This remains the solvency discipline underneath the infrastructure boom.
Debt, leases and contractual commitments eventually require cash.
The Relevance Spread
A useful emerging analytical concept is:
the additional financing premium imposed on companies whose expected cash-flow resilience is perceived to benefit less from AI-driven productivity.
The Relevance Spread does not yet appear as a conventional financial statistic.
But capital markets may increasingly reveal it through:
relative CDS, bond spreads, equity multiples, and access to duration.
Productive Intelligence / Dollar × Watt × Unit of Duration
Ultimately this remains the master efficiency ratio.The winner is not the company that owns the most capacity.
It is the company capable of turning capital, energy and accelerated computing into the largest quantity of economically productive intelligence.

Implications for Investors
The AI trade is broadening from an equity-selection problem into an asset-allocation problem.
The next dispersion may occur between:
companies receiving capital and companies competing against them for it;
AI infrastructure creditors and conventional corporate creditors;
sovereigns capable of attracting long-duration funding and those forced to pay increasing scarcity premiums;
assets whose cash flows accelerate faster than real yields and those whose cash flows do not.
A crucial portfolio question therefore becomes:
Which assets have enough cash-flow velocity to outrun the rising real rate?
High-growth AI assets may.
Regulated infrastructure with pricing power may.
Scarce power and grid assets may.
Some logistics nodes may.
Legacy assets with low productivity growth and no credible Industrial AI pathway may not.
That is a very different map of investment opportunity from a simple “AI versus non-AI” trade.
⸻
Implications for Executives
AI strategy and financing strategy can no longer be separated.
Management teams should now ask not only:
How much do we invest in AI?
But:
What happens to our cost of capital if competitors can demonstrate a more credible AI productivity path than we can?
This changes the boardroom conversation.
Failing to invest in AI may carry an operational cost.
But increasingly it may also carry a financial cost through lower expected growth, weaker cash-flow resilience and reduced investor demand for the company’s debt and equity.
The relevant strategic objective therefore becomes:
AI investment that demonstrably raises cash-flow productivity faster than it raises capital intensity.
Anything less risks participating in the spending boom without participating in the value creation.
⸻
Implications for Policymakers
The policy implication is even larger.
Governments are no longer financing themselves in isolation.
They increasingly compete against:
hyperscalers, private infrastructure, AI factories, energy projects, defense and industrial reshoring
for the same pools of long-duration savings.
That changes fiscal economics.
For Europe in particular, the answer cannot simply be more spending.
It must be higher strategic return on public and private capital.
European policy should increasingly optimize for:
cross-border scale, project bankability, grid readiness, energy security, capital-market integration, and Industrial AI productivity.
Every euro mobilized must create more productive capacity.
Because the Next Economy may not suffer from a shortage of investment opportunities.
It may suffer from a shortage of cheap duration.
