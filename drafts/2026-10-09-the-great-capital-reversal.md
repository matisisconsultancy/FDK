How Italy’s Banking Power Struggle Is Redrawing Europe’s Financial Order

9 October 2026 | Morning Edition

THE DECISIVE SHIFT

What began as a struggle for control of Italy’s banking system is becoming a potential reordering of European financial power.

The original ambition was national: reshape Italy’s banking hierarchy, create a stronger domestic competitor and consolidate influence over strategic financial institutions.

The government led by Giorgia Meloni, MPS, major shareholders and competing banking groups pursued overlapping but distinct objectives.

The outcome is moving beyond any one participant’s original design.

MPS, initially positioned as a consolidator, is now itself a takeover target. Delfin has committed to Intesa. Caltagirone has rejected Lovaglio’s defence. Crédit Agricole has refused to tender its 29.3% Banco BPM stake.

The resulting pressure on MPS could accelerate a broader restructuring involving Mediobanca, Generali, Banco BPM, BPER, Crédit Agricole and UniCredit–Commerzbank.

The reversal is fundamental.

A consolidation process associated with reinforcing Italian financial control may instead accelerate the redistribution of financial influence across Europe.

The lesson extends well beyond banking.

In finance, initiating a transaction does not mean controlling the consequences it unleashes.

⸻

1 | THE ORIGINAL AMBITION: A NEW ITALIAN BANKING HIERARCHY

Italy’s consolidation cycle emerged from the intersection of political objectives, corporate strategies and shareholder interests.

The Meloni government supported the development of a stronger domestic banking competitor around MPS, while major private shareholders pursued their own interests involving Mediobanca and Generali.

These objectives were never fully identical.

The state sought a more competitive domestic banking structure.

Private shareholders sought investment returns, governance influence and strategic positioning.

Bank management teams pursued acquisitions intended to improve scale, profitability and market position.

The original strategic premise was that these interests could produce a coherent new banking architecture.

But the process changed the incentives of every participant.

MPS acquired Mediobanca.

Lovaglio proposed a broader combination involving Banco BPM and Banca Generali.

Intesa launched a competing offer for MPS.

Major shareholders subsequently repositioned.

The institution intended to lead consolidation became the institution being consolidated.

That is the first capital reversal.

⸻

2 | THE SECOND REVERSAL: THE SHAREHOLDER COALITION FRACTURES

The latest developments expose the limits of the original ownership architecture.

Caltagirone has formally opposed Lovaglio’s extraordinary resolutions.

Delfin has committed its MPS stake to Intesa.

Praude has also declared opposition, while Edizione has indicated its intention to tender into Intesa’s offer.

The potential opposition is now substantial, although the exact voting position depends on final entitlements and participation.

MPS’s shareholder disclosures distinguish Caltagirone’s formally notified 10.26% position from a separately reported 13.49% share-capital holding.

That distinction remains important because the extraordinary resolutions require two-thirds of votes cast.

The central conclusion is nevertheless clear.

Lovaglio faces an increasingly difficult shareholder threshold precisely as the execution of his alternative industrial strategy becomes less certain.

Crédit Agricole’s refusal to tender its 29.3% Banco BPM stake creates a second constraint.

Even if MPS shareholders approve the proposed acquisition, the industrial transaction remains exposed to significant execution risk.

The defence is therefore under pressure from both ownership arithmetic and transaction feasibility.

⸻

3 | THE THIRD REVERSAL: THE NATIONAL PROJECT BECOMES EUROPEAN

This is where the consequences become more significant than the original contest.If Intesa completes its acquisition of MPS, it would bring Mediobanca and its strategic Generali exposure into a larger banking perimeter, subject to regulatory remedies.

BPER could expand through branch disposals.

Banco BPM could return to the centre of consolidation.

Crédit Agricole would retain substantial influence over its future.

UniCredit would continue pursuing Continental Scale through Commerzbank.

These are conditional outcomes, not completed transactions.

But together they illustrate the wider transformation.

A contest originally centred on the balance of power within Italian finance could accelerate a restructuring of ownership involving Italian, French and German banking institutions.

The strategic centre of gravity moves from national banking concentration toward European capital allocation.

That is the second-order effect that matters.

⸻

4 | THE GENERALI EFFECT: FROM BANKING CONTROL TO SAVINGS CONTROL

Generali is emerging as a critical strategic asset in the next phase.

Mediobanca’s approximately 13.3% Generali holding creates a link between banking consolidation and the governance of one of Europe’s major insurance groups.

An Intesa acquisition of MPS would change the institutional setting surrounding that holding.

The consequences concern more than ownership.

Generali connects insurance earnings, asset management, financial distribution and long-duration savings.

These capabilities are increasingly valuable because Europe’s next investment cycle requires substantial long-term capital.

Infrastructure, energy systems, artificial intelligence and industrial transformation cannot be financed exclusively through traditional bank lending.

Insurers, pension institutions and asset managers can play an important role, subject to their liabilities, mandates and regulatory requirements.

The next financial power struggle will therefore concern not only who controls banks, but who influences the institutions through which European savings become investment capital.

This is why Generali matters beyond MPS.

⸻

5 | BANCO BPM: THE NEXT CAPITAL-ALLOCATION DECISION

Crédit Agricole’s refusal to tender its Banco BPM holding weakens one of the central pillars of Lovaglio’s proposed defence.

But it also preserves Crédit Agricole’s strategic options.

Its 29.3% stake provides substantial influence without requiring immediate full ownership.

If MPS cannot acquire Banco BPM, alternative consolidation structures could return to consideration.

A deeper combination with Crédit Agricole’s Italian operations is one possibility, although no such transaction has been announced.

The second-order effect is important.

The failure of one acquisition can increase the strategic negotiating value of the asset that was not acquired.

Banco BPM could become the next platform through which European banking consolidation advances.

And Crédit Agricole could become a central participant in defining its ownership structure.

⸻

6 | UNICREDIT–COMMERZBANK: THE CONTINENTAL SCALE TEST

UniCredit–Commerzbank represents a fundamentally different model from Intesa–MPS.

Intesa seeks greater domestic concentration.

UniCredit seeks geographic diversification and cross-border integration.

The distinction matters for Europe.

Domestic consolidation can strengthen national banking champions without necessarily reducing financial fragmentation.

Cross-border consolidation offers the possibility of larger, more diversified institutions capable of allocating capital across multiple European markets.

But those benefits are not automatic.

They depend on integration costs, capital requirements, funding efficiency, governance and regulatory approval.

The political resistance surrounding Commerzbank illustrates the continuing influence of national interests within Banking Union.

Europe can supervise its largest banks collectively. It has not yet demonstrated that it can consistently integrate their ownership and operations across borders.

UniCredit–Commerzbank remains a major test of that institutional gap.

⸻7 | THE MELONI GOVERNMENT AND THE LIMITS OF NATIONAL FINANCIAL STRATEGY

The Italian government’s role illustrates the distinction between influencing a market and controlling its eventual structure.

Rome supported the emergence of a stronger domestic banking competitor and used available policy instruments, including Golden Power, in relation to major banking transactions.

Those actions reflected national financial-policy priorities.

But the subsequent developments demonstrate the independent influence of shareholders, competing banks, market valuations and European regulatory institutions.

The state can establish conditions within its legal authority.

It cannot guarantee that private capital will ultimately follow its preferred ownership architecture.

The same principle applies to corporate management.

A board can initiate a transaction.

It cannot compel shareholders to support it, counterparties to accept it or regulators to approve it.

The power to initiate consolidation is not the power to determine its final outcome.

This is the institutional lesson of the Italian experience.

⸻

8 | THE EUROPEAN CONSEQUENCE: A NEW HIERARCHY OF FINANCIAL POWER

The potential restructuring extends through several connected markets.

Italy: Intesa, MPS, Mediobanca, Banco BPM and BPER.

France: Crédit Agricole’s strategic position in Italy and its broader European ambitions.

Germany: UniCredit–Commerzbank and the political conditions surrounding cross-border ownership.

Insurance and savings: Generali, Banca Generali and the increasing importance of long-duration capital.

European institutions: ECB supervision, competition policy and the unfinished integration of Banking Union.

The emerging question is no longer simply which banks will become larger.

It is which financial groups can combine scale, capital efficiency and cross-border reach while maintaining regulatory legitimacy.

That will influence Europe’s ability to finance technology, infrastructure, energy and industrial transformation.

The next European financial hierarchy will be determined less by national market share than by the capacity to mobilise and allocate capital productively across borders.

⸻

9 | THE STRATEGIC LESSON: THE COST OF UNCONTROLLED CONSEQUENCES

The MPS experience illustrates a broader principle of strategy.

An initial intervention changes the incentives of other actors.

Those actors respond independently.

Their responses alter the available choices.

The resulting outcome can differ fundamentally from the original objective.

In banking, this process is intensified by shareholder voting, capital requirements, competing bids, regulatory approvals and market valuations.

A successful strategy therefore requires more than a compelling opening move.

It requires a credible route through execution, financing, governance and completion.

The critical questions are straightforward.

Can the shareholder coalition be sustained?

Can the transaction be financed?

Will counterparties cooperate?

Can regulatory approval be obtained?

And does the intended end-state create superior economic value?

If these conditions are not secured, the original initiative can produce consequences that favour entirely different participants.

The most expensive strategic error is not necessarily losing a transaction. It is initiating a chain of transactions that reallocates value and control away from the intended beneficiaries.

10 | WHAT COMES NEXT

The immediate calendar will determine the next stage.

October 15 — MPS board. Lovaglio must decide whether to revise the defence, simplify its structure or alter the timetable.

October 29 — MPS shareholder meeting. Voting entitlements, turnout and institutional support will determine whether the extraordinary resolutions can pass.

Intesa–MPS regulatory process. Competition remedies and the treatment of strategic holdings will determine the final economic perimeter if the transaction advances.

Banco BPM–Crédit Agricole.The failure of the MPS route could reopen consideration of alternative ownership structures.

Generali. The future governance of Mediobanca’s strategic holding could become a central post-MPS question.

UniCredit–Commerzbank. The transaction will continue to test the political and regulatory conditions for genuine Continental Scale.

Each outcome will change the opportunities available to the next institution.

⸻

11 | FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS

1 | The original Italian consolidation strategy may produce an unintended ownership structure. MPS, initially positioned as a consolidator, is now itself the target of a larger institution.

2 | Shareholder coalitions are more fragile than political or corporate strategies often assume. Delfin and Caltagirone demonstrate how changing incentives can alter the feasible outcome.

3 | The next strategic assets are Banco BPM and Generali. Their future ownership and governance will influence the next phase of consolidation.

4 | Europe’s financial integration is being tested through transactions before its institutional architecture is complete. UniCredit–Commerzbank remains the clearest cross-border example.

5 | The governing principle is strategic discipline. A transaction should be initiated only with a credible understanding of its execution dependencies, possible counteractions and consequences for capital allocation.

VELOCITY INSIGHT

What began as a contest over the balance of power in Italian banking may accelerate a wider reordering of European finance.

The original objective was to influence the ownership of national banking assets.

The emerging consequence is a contest over the financial institutions that mobilise European savings and allocate capital across the continent.

The distinction is fundamental.

National financial influence is not the same as European financial power.

The former depends heavily on domestic ownership structures and political relationships.

The latter depends on capital efficiency, cross-border scale, regulatory legitimacy and the ability to finance productive investment.

The MPS contest illustrates how the pursuit of the first can unexpectedly accelerate the emergence of the second.
