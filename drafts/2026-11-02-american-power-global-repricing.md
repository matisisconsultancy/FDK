Tracking how America’s political contest reshapes economic policy, capital allocation and financial markets worldwide.

Twenty-four hours ahead of the US midterm elections, The Velocity Edge launches US Political Economy Watch, a new section examining how the evolution of America’s political contest could reshape the US economy—and transmit change across the global financial system.

Our focus is the economic significance of political change: how shifts in power alter policy incentives, institutional constraints and investment decisions, and how those changes propagate through markets.

Election results are the starting point. The greater consequences emerge afterward.

A change in congressional control can reshape the fiscal outlook, redirect oversight and constrain executive action. An emerging political leader can influence expectations about taxation, trade, immigration, industrial policy and regulation well before becoming a presidential candidate. A divided government can impede legislation while increasing reliance on executive authority, changing both the direction and durability of economic policy.

These developments reach beyond Washington. They affect Treasury issuance and term premiums, the dollar, corporate investment, cross-border capital flows and the relative attractiveness of countries, industries and asset classes.

Our purpose is to identify the second-order effects: the consequences that emerge as governments, companies and investors adjust to a changing distribution of American power.

Each edition will follow a disciplined sequence:

Political development → policy implications → economic transmission → global market consequences.

We will distinguish campaign rhetoric from executable policy, temporary electoral momentum from durable coalitions, and immediate market reactions from changes capable of altering long-term returns. Scenarios will identify the conditions that would strengthen or invalidate our assessment.

The approach will be independent of party preference and grounded in evidence. The central question is what a political outcome changes for growth, inflation, fiscal sustainability, corporate earnings and the pricing of risk.

Our opening analysis examines Jon Ossoff and the Georgia signal: whether an emerging Democratic leader could influence the party’s economic direction and the competitive landscape ahead of the 2028 presidential election—even without entering the race himself.

For investors and boards, the task begins before a new policy becomes law. It begins when political developments change the probability of future decisions—and markets start adjusting to them.

American elections redistribute political power. Their consequences can reprice capital across the world.
