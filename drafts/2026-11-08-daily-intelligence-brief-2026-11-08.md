THE BOTTLENECK ECONOMY
Why Advanced Packaging, Strategic Capital and Infrastructure Scarcity Are Redefining Where the AI Supercycle Creates Financial Value
The Big Take — Where Intelligence, Capital Formation and Financial Markets Collide


⸻


THE BIG TAKE
The AI Supercycle is entering a phase in which the scarcity of complementary capabilities may become as important to financial returns as the scarcity of computing power itself.
The latest evidence points toward a consequential evolution.
The competition for advanced processors is increasingly extending into semiconductor packaging, specialized equipment, electricity infrastructure and the financing arrangements needed to turn individual technologies into commercially productive systems.
A Reuters investigation published November 6 identifies advanced semiconductor packaging as a critical production constraint. Industry research cited in the investigation anticipates approximately $28 billion in advanced-packaging capital expenditure during 2026. Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom are seeking additional capacity and alternative manufacturing partners. 
A second November 6 disclosure adds an important financial dimension. Alphabet reported an approximately $548 million equity position in Qualcomm, a semiconductor partner expanding into AI data-centre processors. The position was accumulated during the third quarter; its disclosure is new, not the underlying investment. 
A third development arrived on November 7. Berkshire Hathaway reported $12.705 billion in quarterly operating earnings and approximately $9.6 billion in third-quarter share repurchases. Its results provide a useful contrast with the extraordinary capital requirements of the AI infrastructure industry: capital allocation remains a choice among competing uses of financial resources. 
These developments do not establish a new technological breakthrough on November 8.
They reveal a more important structural question.
As investment removes one constraint in the AI production system, will the economic value associated with scarcity migrate toward another?
The logic is straightforward.
Additional semiconductor manufacturing capacity has limited commercial value if advanced packaging is unavailable.
Additional processors cannot generate computing revenues without electricity, networking and commissioned infrastructure.
And additional computing capacity cannot create sustainable financial value unless enterprises can deploy it productively.
The AI Supercycle is therefore becoming a system of interdependent bottlenecks.
These constraints are not permanent. They evolve as investment, technological innovation and competition alter relative capacity.
That evolution may redistribute profits across industries and economic geographies.
THE CENTRAL INVESTMENT THESIS
The next source of excess returns may increasingly be found at the points where scarce capabilities constrain the productive deployment of capital throughout the AI value chain.
For investors, the opportunity is to identify which constraints are economically binding, which companies possess pricing power and how long that advantage can persist.
For companies, the imperative is to secure complementary capabilities without committing capital at valuations that assume scarcity will last indefinitely.
For policymakers, the challenge is to develop industrial ecosystems in which infrastructure, technology, finance and productive demand evolve together.
For Europe, the implication is decisive.
The continent’s opportunity is broader than constructing sovereign computing facilities. It lies in capturing greater economic value from the technologies, industrial capabilities and infrastructure that make intelligence commercially productive.

1 | ACCELERATED COMPUTING
Advanced Packaging Emerges as a Critical Constraint
The most important semiconductor development newly incorporated into today’s intelligence assessment concerns advanced packaging.Reuters’ November 6 investigation documents how demand for increasingly powerful AI processors is placing exceptional pressure on the technologies used to integrate semiconductor components.
Advanced packaging combines processors, memory and other components into high-performance assemblies.
It allows faster communication between computing elements and supports increasingly complex processor architectures.
Its economic importance is rising as improvements in transistor density become more difficult and costly.

WHAT CHANGED
The semiconductor baseline already established exceptionally strong demand for AI processors and advanced memory.
The latest investigation provides more granular evidence of where manufacturing capacity is constrained.
The critical production limitation is not necessarily the ability to manufacture individual semiconductor components. It can be the ability to integrate those components into commercially usable computing systems.
This changes the investment framework.
An advanced packaging facility can influence the effective output of multiple upstream suppliers.
Its capacity may therefore have economic importance disproportionate to its share of the total value chain.
THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT
AI Processor Demand → Packaging-Capacity Constraints → Longer Delivery Times → Investment in Additional Packaging → Potential Changes in Pricing Power.
In the short term, limited capacity can benefit suppliers controlling specialized equipment and production capabilities.
Over time, additional investment may reduce scarcity, increase competition and normalize margins.
That creates a critical investment distinction.
The financial value of a bottleneck depends not simply on its existence, but on its persistence and the ability of its owners to capture the associated economic rents.
THE EUROPEAN IMPLICATION
Europe has meaningful capabilities in semiconductor equipment, materials, advanced manufacturing and industrial engineering.
The packaging expansion creates opportunities for these suppliers, including companies that do not directly manufacture frontier AI processors.
But the opportunity should be evaluated through actual contracts, margins, technological differentiation and returns on capital.

2 | THE GREAT SPLIT
Alphabet’s Qualcomm Investment Illustrates a New Dimension of Strategic Capital Allocation
On November 6, Alphabet disclosed a Qualcomm equity stake worth approximately $548 million.
The investment was made during the third quarter.
Qualcomm is seeking to expand beyond smartphones into AI data-centre processors and specialized computing systems.
The company expects data-centre chip revenue to reach approximately $15 billion by 2029, according to the forecast cited by Reuters. That figure remains a management ambition, not realized revenue. 
WHAT CHANGED
The newly disclosed investment reinforces an important financial pattern.
Major technology companies are participating in the AI ecosystem not only as customers and infrastructure operators but also as investors in complementary technologies.
However, the disclosure does not establish that Alphabet acquired the stake specifically to finance Qualcomm’s data-centre expansion.
Nor does it constitute a new semiconductor procurement commitment.The investment is evidence of financial participation, not proof of an exclusive strategic relationship.
THE POTENTIAL FINANCIAL MECHANISM
A major technology platform can benefit from a more competitive semiconductor supplier ecosystem.
Greater processor competition may increase purchasing flexibility.
Alternative architectures may improve the economics of specific computing workloads.
Strategic investments may offer exposure to the resulting commercial success.
The potential transmission is:
Semiconductor Competition → Greater Procurement Flexibility → Improved Computing Economics → Potential Financial Returns for Customers and Suppliers.
But the benefits are conditional.
Alternative processors must demonstrate competitive performance, software compatibility, reliable production and economically attractive pricing.
THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT
The traditional distinction between customers, suppliers and investors is becoming less clear across portions of the AI ecosystem.
Equity stakes, customer warrants, long-term purchasing agreements and infrastructure financing can connect companies through multiple financial relationships.
These structures can facilitate investment and improve revenue visibility.
They can also concentrate exposures to the same underlying technological cycle.
Investor implication: Analyze the full economic relationship among a company’s investments, procurement commitments, financing obligations and operating revenues.
NER-C ASSESSMENT: NEW FINANCIAL DISCLOSURE. THE COMMERCIAL BENEFITS AND FUTURE RETURNS OF THE INVESTMENT REMAIN UNPROVEN.

Its investments include Alphabet and Italian technology company Bending Spoons. These positions were established or expanded before Saturday’s disclosure. 
WHAT CHANGED
The latest results provide new evidence of capital deployment by one of the world’s largest diversified investment groups.
They do not establish a new institutional rotation out of AI.
Berkshire retains substantial exposure to technology while continuing to invest in established businesses and repurchase its own shares.
The more useful comparison concerns capital-allocation discipline.
THE FINANCIAL INTERPRETATION
During the AI Supercycle, some companies are committing extraordinary amounts of capital against future technological opportunities.
Berkshire is demonstrating a different allocation model, combining operating businesses, financial investments, acquisitions and share repurchases.
Neither approach is inherently superior.
The economic test is the same.
Will the expected returns on incremental capital exceed the relevant cost of capital?
The answer depends on valuation, business economics, growth opportunities and financing requirements.
THE GREAT SPLIT
This comparison introduces a broader investment distinction.
Companies pursuing exceptional technological growth may accept negative free cash flow during infrastructure expansion.
Mature businesses can generate substantial current cash flow while offering lower growth.
Investors must evaluate both according to the timing, durability and risk of future returns.The next capital rotation may be less a choice between technology and traditional industries than a differentiation between productive capital formation and investment requiring increasingly demanding assumptions about future profitability.
THE EUROPEAN IMPLICATION
Europe’s established industrial groups possess substantial assets, customer relationships, engineering expertise and operating cash flows.
Their opportunity is to improve the productivity of existing assets through AI while maintaining financial flexibility.
That could represent a different source of value creation from financing entirely new computing infrastructure.

4 | INDUSTRIAL AI AND PRODUCTIVITY
The Investment Cycle Is Advancing Faster Than the Evidence of Productive Returns
The European Central Bank’s October 30 analysis provides a useful reference point for evaluating the evolution of industrial AI.
The ECB estimates that euro-area digital investment increased from approximately 2.3% of GDP in 2014 to around 3.3% by the first half of 2026, using a proxy for the latest period.
Its corporate survey indicates that AI increased companies’ planned capital expenditure by approximately 10% in 2026. 
These figures demonstrate growing investment intentions.
They do not establish an equivalent increase in productive output.
THE INDUSTRIAL BASELINE
The European Commission’s Joint Research Centre reported that AI adoption among companies in EU strategic sectors increased from 7% in 2021 to 18% in 2025.
Adoption reached 34% in energy and 30% in electronics, but remained substantially lower in other sectors.
The JRC also identified organizational adaptation as an important condition for meaningful deployment. 
WHAT CHANGED
No new independently verified European productivity release available this morning demonstrates an acceleration in economy-wide returns from industrial AI.
THE PRODUCTIVITY BASELINE REMAINS UNCHANGED.
This is not evidence that AI lacks productive value.
It means that the scale and distribution of realized economic gains remain insufficiently established.
THE FINANCIAL MECHANISM
The relevant transition is:
AI Adoption → Organizational Redesign → Improved Production and Decision-Making → Higher Asset Utilization → Stronger Operating Cash Flow.
Each stage introduces potential friction.
Companies must integrate data, redesign workflows, develop employee capabilities and manage implementation costs.
Technological efficiency gains may also be passed to customers through lower prices rather than retained as additional profits.
THE INVESTMENT IMPLICATION
The next important distinction will be between companies increasing AI expenditure and companies demonstrating improved capital productivity because of that expenditure.
For industrial investors, the most useful indicators remain incremental operating margins, cash-flow conversion, working-capital efficiency and returns on invested capital.
NER-C ASSESSMENT: NO NEW AGGREGATE PRODUCTIVITY INFLECTION. ORGANIZATIONAL AND FINANCIAL EXECUTION REMAIN THE CENTRAL EMPIRICAL TESTS.

5 | ENERGY, GRIDS AND COMPUTING INFRASTRUCTURE
The Physical Bottleneck Has Not Disappeared
The expansion of advanced computing continues to increase demand for electricity infrastructure.
The European Commission estimates that EU data centres consumed approximately 68 TWh of electricity in 2024.
Consumption could reach 114 TWh by 2030, equivalent to more than 3% of total EU electricity demand.
These are historical estimates and forward projections, not newly measured November consumption. 
The financial importance of this trend is substantial.
Data-centre developers must secure electricity, grid connections and reliable cooling systems before advanced computing equipment can generate commercial revenues.
The constraints interact.
A project may possess financing and customer contracts but lack the electricity required for operations.Another may have secured electricity but face permitting or construction delays.
THE LATEST EUROPEAN SIGNAL
The coordinated demonstrations of November 6–7 reinforce the visibility of opposition to large-scale data-centre development.
Reuters confirmed that activists entered a Microsoft-associated construction site in Amsterdam on November 6.
The campaign also announced actions across multiple European countries. 
The evidence does not establish that a European-wide construction moratorium has been adopted.
Nor does it demonstrate that the Amsterdam project has been canceled.
THE FINANCIAL TRANSMISSION
Electricity Constraints + Permitting Requirements → Commissioning Risk → Financing Costs → Commercial Utilization → Project Returns.
The relevant financial asset is commissioned computing capacity supported by commercially sustainable demand.
Announced megawatts are not equivalent to operational megawatts.
THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT
Infrastructure scarcity may create opportunities for suppliers of electricity equipment, grid technologies, power-management systems, cooling technologies and engineering services.
But substantial investment is required to expand those capabilities.
Higher infrastructure costs can also reduce the returns available to computing operators.
For Europe, the objective should be to increase reliable electricity capacity while ensuring that infrastructure costs are allocated transparently.
NER-C ASSESSMENT: PHYSICAL CONSTRAINTS AND PUBLIC ACCEPTANCE REMAIN MATERIAL. NO NEW VERIFIED EUROPE-WIDE GRID-CAPACITY EXPANSION OR GENERAL DATA-CENTRE MORATORIUM.

6 | ECONOMIC GEOGRAPHY AND GEOPOLITICS
The AI Value Chain Is Becoming More Geographically Distributed—and Financially Interdependent
The latest semiconductor evidence reinforces the importance of Taiwan’s advanced manufacturing ecosystem.
TSMC remains central to high-end packaging.
Taiwanese suppliers are expanding alternative production technologies.
Singapore is attracting proposed investment in advanced packaging.
The United States remains central to frontier-model development, cloud platforms and the financing of global computing infrastructure.
Europe retains important capabilities in semiconductor equipment, industrial engineering, software and electricity systems.
THE IMPORTANT DISTINCTION
The location of computing investment is not necessarily the location where its economic value is captured.
A data centre may be constructed in Europe.
Its processors may be designed in the United States.
Advanced packaging may take place in Taiwan.
Electricity equipment may be supplied by European manufacturers.
And the applications ultimately generating productivity improvements may be developed elsewhere.
The relevant measure of national competitiveness is therefore broader than the location of physical investment.
It includes intellectual property, industrial value added, exports, corporate profitability and reinvestment capacity.
THE GEOPOLITICAL IMPLICATION
Concentration in advanced packaging introduces a supply-chain resilience issue.
Governments and companies may seek greater geographic diversification.
But duplicating specialized manufacturing capacity is expensive.
The economic value of diversification depends on the resilience gained relative to the additional investment required.
For Europe, the strongest approach may involve selective development of critical capabilities combined with continued participation in international technology supply chains.
WHAT REMAINS UNCHANGED
No newly verified formal semiconductor export-control change or new cross-border restriction establishes a structural policy inflection this morning.
NER-C ASSESSMENT: ADVANCED-PACKAGING GEOGRAPHY HAS BECOME MORE FINANCIALLY SIGNIFICANT. THE FORMAL GEOPOLITICAL POLICY BASELINE REMAINS UNCHANGED.

7 | EUROPE 2030 POLICY WATCH
The Critical Test Is Moving from Program Design to Industrial Execution
Europe’s technological strategy increasingly combines semiconductor design, advanced computing and industrial deployment.The European Commission’s November 4 launch of the European Chip Design Platform represents the most important recent policy development.
The initiative will mobilize €525 million to support European semiconductor-design companies.
The Commission states that European companies account for less than 1% of global revenues in the relevant chip-design segment. 
The strategic objective is clear.
Europe must develop more of the intellectual property and commercial design capabilities required to sustain a competitive semiconductor ecosystem.
THE NEW FINANCIAL CONNECTION
The advanced-packaging evidence adds another question to Europe’s industrial strategy.
Can Europe develop competitive chip-design and manufacturing capabilities without strengthening the complementary technologies needed to turn those designs into commercially viable computing systems?
The answer depends on product specialization and international supply-chain arrangements.
Not every European semiconductor company requires domestic access to the most advanced packaging technologies.
But the broader ecosystem needs reliable access to the relevant capabilities.

WHAT CHANGED

THE IMPLEMENTATION BASELINE REMAINS UNCHANGED.

THE EUROPEAN PRIORITY
Europe must connect technological specialization with bankable infrastructure and commercially productive demand.
That requires coordinated development of semiconductor design, manufacturing, computing infrastructure, electricity systems and industrial applications.
The objective is not simply to increase the European share of AI capital expenditure. It is to increase the European share of the economic value generated by that expenditure.

8 | THE NEW NER-C RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS
THE MOVING BOTTLENECK HYPOTHESIS
Can the Migration of Scarcity Across the AI Value Chain Predict the Next Reallocation of Capital?
The latest advanced-packaging evidence provides a foundation for a new research proposition.
As investment expands capacity at one stage of the AI production system, the principal economic constraint may migrate toward another stage, altering pricing power, capital requirements and the distribution of financial returns.
The proposition extends the NER-C Capital Conversion framework.
THE CENTRAL MECHANISM
The AI production system involves multiple complementary capabilities.
Processor design.
Semiconductor fabrication.
Advanced packaging.
Networking.
Electricity infrastructure.
Computing deployment.
Enterprise applications.
Investment can expand capacity at individual stages, but overall output remains constrained by the capabilities that are most difficult to increase or substitute.
The proposed mechanism is:
Investment → Capacity Expansion → Bottleneck Migration → Changes in Scarcity Pricing → New Investment Opportunities → Further Capacity Expansion.
WHY THIS MATTERS FINANCIALLY
A supplier controlling a scarce capability may temporarily generate exceptional profitability.
As competitors invest, capacity expands.
The scarcity premium may then decline.
Meanwhile, increasing output can create new pressure elsewhere in the system.
The next bottleneck may develop in memory, packaging, power delivery or another complementary activity.
This suggests a potentially important investment dynamic.
The financial winners of the AI Supercycle may change as the location and persistence of scarcity evolve.
THE EMPIRICAL RESEARCH FRAMEWORK
NER-C should examine the following variables.
Research dimension Proposed indicator
Manufacturing scarcity Lead times and capacity utilization
Packaging scarcity Available advanced-packaging capacity relative to contracted demand
Infrastructure scarcity Grid access, commissioning delays and electricity costs
Pricing power Gross margins and realized contract prices
Capital response Incremental capacity investment and financing costs
Economic returns Incremental ROIC minus WACC
Scarcity duration Time required for supply expansion or technological substitution
The analysis should distinguish nominal capacity from effective usable capacity.
It should also control for technological substitution, customer concentration, pricing arrangements and changes in final demand.
THE TESTABLE PREDICTION
If bottleneck migration is economically consequential, improving capacity at one stage should be followed by changes in utilization, prices or delivery constraints at complementary stages.
Companies controlling newly binding constraints may experience improving margins or revenue opportunities.
Those benefiting from the previous bottleneck may face normalization as supply expands.
THE FALSIFICATION TEST
The hypothesis would be weakened if changes in relative capacity did not systematically predict shifts in pricing power or economic returns after controlling for demand and technology.
It would be strengthened if bottleneck indicators consistently anticipated changes in profitability and capital allocation across adjacent industries.
THE EUROPEAN EXTENSION
Europe’s capabilities in semiconductor equipment, engineering, power technologies and industrial systems make it a particularly relevant case.
The research should evaluate whether European companies can capture increasing value as constraints migrate from processor production toward complementary technologies and productive deployment.
Research status: NEW, EMPIRICALLY TESTABLE HYPOTHESIS.The latest evidence establishes the relevance of advanced-packaging constraints but does not yet demonstrate a systematic migration of excess returns across the entire AI value chain.

9 | THE FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS
FOR FINANCIAL INVESTORS, BOARDS OF DIRECTORS AND EUROPEAN POLICYMAKERS
1. The economics of AI are becoming increasingly dependent on complementary capabilities. Advanced packaging illustrates how the scarcity of one essential industrial process can constrain the productive deployment of much larger investments.
2. Strategic capital relationships are becoming more important. Alphabet’s Qualcomm holding reinforces the growing financial interdependence among computing platforms, semiconductor suppliers and infrastructure developers.
3. Financial discipline remains a competitive advantage. Berkshire’s latest results provide a contrasting model of capital allocation in an environment where technological investment commitments are becoming exceptionally large.
4. The productivity dividend remains insufficiently established at the aggregate level. Enterprise adoption continues expanding, but investors must distinguish expenditure on intelligence from measurable improvements in economic output and cash generation.
5. Europe’s opportunity lies in capturing more value across the complete AI production system. Semiconductor design, specialized manufacturing, advanced materials, electricity infrastructure and industrial applications can be important sources of competitive advantage.

THE FINAL VELOCITY INSIGHT
THE NEXT CAPITAL ROTATION WILL FOLLOW THE MIGRATION OF SCARCITY
The latest developments reveal an important evolution in the AI Supercycle.
The global economy is not simply increasing its capacity to produce intelligence. It is discovering which complementary capabilities are required to transform that capacity into productive economic value.
The emerging financial consequences are substantial.
Advanced packaging demonstrates that technological leadership can depend on specialized manufacturing processes far removed from the most visible AI applications.
Alphabet’s Qualcomm investment illustrates how technology companies are expanding financial relationships across the infrastructure ecosystem.
Berkshire Hathaway’s results reinforce the importance of evaluating competing uses of capital according to their expected economic returns.
And Europe’s semiconductor initiatives demonstrate that industrial competitiveness depends on capabilities distributed across the complete production system.
Together, these developments suggest three financial dynamics.
First, scarcity can migrate. Investment that expands one capability may increase the economic importance of another. Investors must therefore evaluate the evolution of the entire production system rather than extrapolate today’s constraints indefinitely.
Second, capital allocation will increasingly determine who captures the resulting value. Companies that invest efficiently in scarce capabilities may earn attractive returns, but excessive investment can eliminate the very scarcity supporting those returns.
Third, national competitive advantage may increasingly depend on the coordination of complementary industries. Semiconductor technologies, electricity, engineering, financing and enterprise applications must evolve together to sustain productive investment.
For Europe, the implications are decisive.
The continent possesses industrial capabilities capable of supporting a substantial role in the AI-native economy.
But those capabilities must be connected to commercially competitive technologies, reliable infrastructure and deeper financing markets.
Europe’s objective should not be to replicate every component of the American or Asian technology ecosystems.
It should be to develop and control economically valuable capabilities while maintaining access to the complementary technologies required for commercial success.For investors, the opportunity is to identify where scarcity generates sustainable economic returns—and where new investment is likely to erode those returns.
For corporate boards, the priority is to secure strategic capabilities without sacrificing financial flexibility.
For policymakers, the task is to support productive industrial ecosystems rather than disconnected investment projects.
The next great reallocation of capital may be determined less by the companies producing the largest quantities of intelligence than by those controlling the scarce capabilities required to make intelligence economically productive.
THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
The AI Supercycle is creating extraordinary demand. The next financial advantage will belong to those who anticipate where scarcity moves—and invest before its economic value is fully priced.
