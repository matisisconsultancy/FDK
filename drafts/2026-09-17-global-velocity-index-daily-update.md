Today produces one country-level numerical revision: the United States falls −0.1 to 94.3.

More important than the move itself, which markets had largely anticipated, is the new path: 16 of 18 policymakers expect at least one further increase before year-end, while the Fed raised its 2026 PCE inflation forecast to 3.7% and no longer expects inflation to return to 2% until 2029.

The deeper signal is becoming difficult to ignore:
The AI Supercycle still has abundant capital. What it no longer has is cheap capital.

United States — 
−0.1: the Capital Tax becomes real
The Fed decision crosses the GVI admission threshold because it changes an observable input in the Smart Capital pillar.
The new 3.75%–4.00% policy range is only part of the signal. Updated projections show policymakers expecting rates to end both 2026 and 2027 around 4.00%–4.25%, while the Fed simultaneously upgraded expected 2026 growth to 2.3% and reduced its projected unemployment rate to 4.1%. 
That combination is unusual.
The Fed is not tightening because the economy is collapsing.
It is tightening into resilient demand, AI-related capital expenditure and energy-driven inflation. Reuters reports that policymakers explicitly see tariffs, the oil shock and AI investment as contributing to current price pressure. 
August consumption reinforces the point. Retail sales surged 1.2%, the strongest increase since March, while the control group used in GDP calculations rose 1.4%. At the same time, import prices increased 0.7% month on month and 7.0% year on year, reflecting higher energy and capital-goods costs. 
The new causality is therefore:
AI investment ↑ → economic demand ↑ → infrastructure scarcity ↑ → inflation persistence ↑ → policy rates ↑.The US score falls only 0.1, rather than more, because stronger consumption and continued financing of AI infrastructure demonstrate substantial economic absorption capacity.
The counterweight: AI infrastructure becomes bankable
A consortium of ten banks is arranging approximately $22 billion of debt for Crux AI, the Blackstone-Alphabet cloud venture. Blackstone has already committed $5 billion of equity, Google will supply TPUs, software and services, and the platform aims to activate its first 500 MW in 2027. The debt is expected to be secured by chips and customer contracts and could later migrate into investment-grade capital markets. 
The structural importance is greater than the transaction itself:
AI accelerators → collateral → customer contracts → bank debt → institutional refinancing.
Accelerated computing is increasingly being transformed into a financeable infrastructure asset class.
Because some financing details come from unnamed sources and syndication is not complete, the transaction offsets the US Smart Capital deduction only partially.

Germany — sovereign enterprise AI strengthens, 
score held at 83.2
Cohere and Germany’s Aleph Alpha signed a definitive merger agreement to create a combined AI company previously valued at approximately $20 billion, with dual headquarters in Toronto and Berlin and Aleph Alpha’s Heidelberg base retained as a research centre. 
The industrial architecture surrounding the transaction is more consequential than the headline valuation.
Schwarz Group will invest €500 million in the merged company and provide computing capacity through StackIT. It is separately investing €11–13 billion in a German data-centre campus designed eventually to host as many as 100,000 AI chips. The strategy targets governments and regulated enterprises requiring models that can run inside sovereign or customer-controlled infrastructure. 
This directly strengthens Germany’s Enterprise Transformation thesis:
European enterprise demand → sovereign models → domestic cloud → German AI infrastructure → regulated-industry deployment.
No +0.1 is admitted today because the combination remains subject to regulatory approval, and significant parts of the infrastructure programme are future capital expenditure rather than operating capacity.
But Germany’s momentum improves materially.
The strategic significance is clear: Europe may not have to defeat US frontier-model companies on parameter count to build a defensible position. Secure, sovereign, industry-specific enterprise AI can be a different competitive game.

Spain — a potentially structural infrastructure upgrade, but 
no score yet
Spain announced that planned electricity-transmission investment through 2030 will rise by roughly 30% to more than €17 billion. The government explicitly cited rapidly increasing electricity demand from electrification and large users including data centres. 
It would be the largest transmission-grid investment programme in Spanish history. The proposal also includes an auction-based capacity mechanism allowing generation, storage and some demand-side participants to receive compensation for supporting the system during stress periods. 
This is exactly the type of architecture the Intelligence Infrastructure pillar is designed to capture:
renewables → transmission → storage → data centres → AI capacity → enterprise adoption.
However, the plan remains within the formal approval process rather than representing completed capex. The GVI therefore records a strong positive momentum change but zero numerical points today.
Spain remains 75.0.
The admission trigger is straightforward: final approval, committed transmission projects, procurement or observable construction.

United Kingdom — energy inflation confirms the constraint, 
no second downgrade
UK CPI accelerated to 3.1% in August, from 2.9%, driven largely by petrol, diesel and airfares. But core inflation remained at 2.6% for a fourth consecutive month, while services inflation stayed at 3.4%. 
The distinction matters.This is predominantly an external energy-price shock, not yet evidence of a domestic wage-price spiral.
Producer prices nevertheless provide a warning. Manufacturer input-price inflation accelerated to roughly 6.1% year on year and output-price inflation to approximately 3.7%, showing that the energy shock is beginning to enter industrial cost structures. 
Because yesterday’s −0.1 UK adjustment already incorporated weakening labour absorption, the model does not double-count the deterioration.
The UK remains 82.3.

China — the Agentic-AI divergence widens, 
score unchanged
Huawei now forecasts that autonomous agents could generate more than 90% of global AI-token traffic by 2035, with potentially hundreds of billions of agents continuously perceiving, reasoning, deciding and interacting with external systems. Huawei simultaneously lists autonomous-agent security among its ten core technology priorities. 
No numerical GVI weight is assigned to a corporate forecast.
But it reveals an important strategic divergence.
Parts of the US frontier-AI industry are debating whether capability growth should be slowed because of safety risks.
Huawei’s roadmap assumes dramatically faster deployment, accompanied by technical safeguards rather than a reduction in development speed. 
This may become one of the defining contrasts in the US-China race:
United States: frontier capability + increasingly explicit pacing debate.
China: capability catch-up + aggressive economic diffusion + security engineered around deployment.
The economic winner will not necessarily be determined simply by which philosophy proves safer or faster. It may depend on which one generates the strongest compounding loop between intelligence, adoption and productivity.

Institutional interpretation — 
The Cost of Capital Returns to the AI Equation
The fundamental question has shifted again:
What happens when intelligence continues to become cheaper technologically, but more expensive financially?
This distinction could define the next stage of the AI Supercycle.
Accelerated computing keeps improving.
Models keep improving.
Inference costs continue to fall.
But the infrastructure surrounding those models increasingly has to be financed at higher interest rates, higher equipment costs and higher energy costs.
That means two different cost curves are now moving in opposite directions:Technological cost of intelligence ↓
while
Fully loaded financial cost of industrialising intelligence ↑
The difference between them is becoming strategically decisive.
Main accelerator — 
AI becomes institutional infrastructure
The Crux AI transaction shows banks beginning to treat chips, customer contracts and AI capacity as collateral supporting tens of billions of dollars of financing. Germany is building sovereign enterprise AI around models, private cloud and industrial customers. Spain is redesigning its grid for a world containing far more large electricity users.
These are not isolated technology announcements.
They are evidence that AI is entering the architecture of banking, grids, corporate IT and national infrastructure. 
Main decelerator — 
the hurdle rate
The Fed has now transformed a market expectation into policy.
And it has signalled that one hike may not be enough.
The consequence is not that the AI investment cycle stops.
It is that the quality threshold for investment rises.
Projects whose economics only work with cheap money become vulnerable.
Projects possessing secured customers, secured energy, high utilisation, pricing power and rapid cash conversion become more valuable.
Implications for investors
The next Great Split within AI is likely to be between bankable and non-bankable intelligence infrastructure.
The premium should migrate toward:
permitted capacity + secured energy + contracted customers + high utilisation + resilient balance sheets.
The discount should widen for speculative gigawatts, weakly contracted capacity and business models dependent on permanently falling financing costs.
Implications for executives
The old question was:
How much AI should we deploy?
The better question is becoming:
What economic return does each incremental unit of intelligence generate above our rising cost of capital?
This requires companies to connect inference and agent deployment directly to revenue, margin, working-capital efficiency, asset productivity and free cash flow.
Implications for policymakers
The Spain-Germany-US evidence makes the new policy architecture increasingly visible.
National AI competitiveness requires not simply research grants or chip subsidies, but an integrated system:
capital markets → accelerated computing → electricity grids → enterprise deployment → skills → productivity → reinvestment.
The countries capable of connecting those layers will gain Velocity.
Those that optimise only one layer may create impressive technology without corresponding economic returns.
Bottom line
The FDK Global Velocity Index remains at 108.0, while the United States falls modestly to 94.3.
This is not the end of the AI Supercycle.
It is the beginning of a more demanding one.
The first phase rewarded access to intelligence.
The second rewarded access to scale.
The phase now emerging will reward the ability to produce returns above a rising hurdle rate.
And that creates a new operating equation for the Next Economy:
Velocity = Intelligence Productivity − Fully Loaded Cost of Capital and Physical Infrastructure.
The countries, companies and investors that solve that equation will increasingly determine where the next trillions of dollars of value are created.
