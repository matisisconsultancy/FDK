From the Manhattan Project to Nvidia: How Espionage, Technological Sovereignty and the U.S.–China AI Race Are Redefining Global Economic Power
When Corporate Secrets Become Strategic Weapons—and National Security Becomes a Financial-Market Variable
THE BIG TAKE
The most consequential revelation of the Nvidia espionage scandal is not the alleged targeting of Jensen Huang. It is that the geopolitical struggle for artificial intelligence has moved inside the corporations that control the technological foundations of global economic power.
For much of the twentieth century, the most sensitive intelligence operations targeted governments, military laboratories and defense contractors.
Today, extraordinary strategic knowledge resides inside private corporations.
Nvidia controls technologies central to accelerated computing. OpenAI and Anthropic develop frontier AI systems. Google, Microsoft and Amazon operate critical computing infrastructure. TSMC manufactures advanced semiconductors. ASML supplies equipment essential to their production.
These companies are no longer simply technology businesses.
They are strategic institutions whose capabilities increasingly influence national security, industrial competitiveness, military power and the future distribution of global wealth.
The controversy surrounding Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang illustrates this transformation.
American intelligence officials reportedly suspect that Alice Huang, a former executive associated with Singapore-based Megaspeed International, may have been acting on behalf of Chinese intelligence.
China denies the allegations. Nvidia has established an independent investigation and states that it has no reason to believe company secrets or American national security were compromised.
Neither espionage nor the compromise of Nvidia’s confidential information has been established.
But the wider concern is not hypothetical.
In January 2026, a former Google engineer was convicted of stealing AI trade secrets for the benefit of China. In October, an OpenAI employee was charged with allegedly installing a covert recording device in an executive conference room. The latter remains a criminal allegation, not a conviction or established foreign-government operation.
The incidents differ in their evidence and legal status. Together, they demonstrate why advanced AI companies require increasingly sophisticated protection of sensitive technology and strategic information.
The financial implications are profound.
Artificial intelligence is becoming a national-security asset before financial markets have fully developed the mechanisms to price the geopolitical risks attached to it.
The next phase of the AI race will therefore be fought over more than algorithms and processors.
It will involve intellectual property, industrial supply chains, executive access, secure computing, energy infrastructure and the financial capacity to sustain technological leadership.
The fundamental question is no longer simply whether America or China will develop the most advanced intelligence.
It is whether the pursuit of technological sovereignty will accelerate global productivity—or divide the world into competing technological and financial systems whose fragmentation raises the cost of innovation.
I. THE NVIDIA AFFAIR
WHEN CORPORATE LEADERSHIP BECOMES A STRATEGIC TARGET
Nvidia occupies an unusually sensitive position in the global economy.
Its processors and networking technologies underpin advanced AI systems. Its commercial relationships provide insight into future computing requirements, infrastructure investments and strategic technological developments.
Its CEO also maintains relationships with government leaders, major customers and industrial partners.
This concentration of information creates potential intelligence value.
Three categories are particularly important.
Technological intelligence.Product roadmaps, chip architectures, software developments and manufacturing partnerships may reveal competitive advantages before they become publicly visible.
Strategic intelligence. Executive conversations can provide insight into government policy, export restrictions, customer investment plans and the direction of technological competition.
Commercial influence. Access to decision-makers could potentially affect supply-chain relationships, procurement or strategic perceptions.
These are possible intelligence objectives, not findings about the Nvidia case.
The distinction is essential.
Investigators must establish what actually occurred, whether confidential information was accessed and whether any commercial decisions were improperly influenced.
According to reporting cited by Reuters, companies associated with Alice Huang allegedly facilitated approximately $7.5 billion in shipments involving Nvidia chips to Chinese customers. Reuters said it could not independently verify the underlying allegations.
The immediate financial consequence has already been significant.
Nvidia experienced sharp equity-market losses, while investors reassessed corporate governance, export-control exposure and leadership concentration.
But the more consequential question concerns the future.
When the leadership of a strategically indispensable company becomes a potential intelligence target, corporate governance becomes part of national-security infrastructure.
The resulting risks can influence valuations even before any technological compromise is proven.
II. THE HISTORICAL LESSONS
ESPIONAGE HAS ALWAYS FOLLOWED STRATEGIC TECHNOLOGY
The Nvidia controversy has important historical precedents. Their relevance lies not in identical circumstances, but in the mechanisms through which strategic knowledge becomes geopolitical power.
The Manhattan Project: Intelligence Can Accelerate Strategic Catch-Up
During the Second World War, Klaus Fuchs transmitted nuclear information to Soviet intelligence while participating in Allied atomic research.
His espionage contributed to Soviet knowledge of nuclear weapons development, although the Soviet program also depended on extensive domestic scientific and industrial capabilities.
The lesson is fundamental.
Espionage can reduce technological uncertainty and accelerate development without replacing the industrial capacity required to reproduce advanced technology.
The same principle applies to AI.
Information about advanced processors can assist technological substitution. It cannot automatically reproduce semiconductor fabrication, memory manufacturing, lithography, electricity infrastructure or a functioning software ecosystem.
The decisive advantage lies in converting acquired knowledge into productive capability.
The Profumo Affair: Personal Exposure Can Become Institutional Risk
Britain’s 1963 Profumo affair demonstrated how private relationships involving strategically important individuals can generate national-security concerns and institutional crises.
The episode did not establish that British military secrets were successfully transferred through those relationships.
Its relevance is therefore institutional rather than evidentiary.
The perceived vulnerability of a strategically important individual can undermine confidence even when an actual intelligence compromise remains unproven.
For Nvidia, the corresponding questions concern governance, disclosure, oversight and leadership continuity.
Boeing: The Industrial Process Is as Valuable as the Product
In 2009, former Boeing engineer Dongfan “Greg” Chung was convicted of economic espionage involving restricted aerospace technology.
The case included technical information connected to the Space Shuttle and Delta IV rocket programs.
The relevance to AI is direct.
Competitors may seek not only finished chip designs but also the manufacturing processes, testing methods, software systems and supply-chain knowledge required to reproduce advanced technologies.The next generation of industrial espionage will increasingly target the systems that make technological leadership reproducible.
Google: Corporate Espionage Can Become Foreign Policy
In January 2010, Google disclosed sophisticated cyberattacks originating from China that resulted in the theft of intellectual property.
The incident helped reshape Google’s relationship with Chinese authorities and its approach to operating in that market.
The lesson extends beyond cybersecurity.
An intelligence incident can trigger regulatory scrutiny, diplomatic confrontation and changes in corporate strategy.
The largest financial consequences may arise not from the information compromised, but from the institutional decisions taken afterward.
III. WHY THIS TIME IS DIFFERENT
STRATEGIC POWER HAS MOVED INTO PRIVATE TECHNOLOGY SYSTEMS
Three characteristics distinguish the contemporary AI race from earlier periods of technological espionage.
First, strategic innovation is increasingly corporate. Unlike the government-directed Manhattan Project, the AI frontier is largely advanced through commercial enterprises operating across national borders.
Second, AI is a general-purpose technology. Its applications extend simultaneously into manufacturing, defense, financial services, energy, transportation and scientific discovery. Technological advantages can therefore produce economic and strategic effects across multiple sectors.
Third, the infrastructure is global while political authority remains national. American-designed chips depend on manufacturing in Taiwan, memory from South Korea, equipment from several countries and internationally distributed supply chains.
The resulting system is simultaneously integrated and vulnerable.
Governments seek control over technologies that cannot easily be produced entirely within national borders.
Companies seek access to international markets that governments increasingly regard as security-sensitive.
And investors must assess the financial consequences of both objectives.
The AI race is transforming global corporate interdependence into a central question of national security.
That is its defining novelty.
IV. THE U.S.–CHINA AI RACE
FROM COMPUTING SUPREMACY TO TECHNOLOGICAL SOVEREIGNTY
The competition between the United States and China is moving beyond the performance of individual models and processors.
It increasingly concerns control over entire technological ecosystems.
The United States possesses major strengths in frontier AI development, semiconductor design, cloud computing and access to financial capital.
China possesses extensive manufacturing capabilities, industrial supply chains and growing domestic AI and robotics ecosystems.
Both economies face constraints.
America must reconcile technological leadership with infrastructure financing, secure supply chains and export-control enforcement.
China must reduce dependence on foreign-controlled bottlenecks while preserving the efficiency of its industrial and technological development.
The resulting competition has five dimensions.

The strategic dilemma is increasingly apparent.
Export restrictions may protect sensitive technology.
But they can also reduce revenues, increase compliance costs and encourage competitors to develop alternatives.
Technological autonomy may improve national resilience.
But duplicating industrial infrastructure can increase capital requirements and weaken economies of scale.The defining question is whether the security benefits of technological fragmentation will exceed its economic costs.
The answer will differ by technology, sector and national-security requirement.
There is no single financial outcome that applies to every restriction.
For investors, the challenge is to identify which companies benefit from changing supply chains—and which face higher costs, reduced market access or declining competitive advantages.
V. THE GREAT FINANCIAL REPRICING
NATIONAL SECURITY BECOMES A COST-OF-CAPITAL VARIABLE
The most consequential implication of the Nvidia scandal is the introduction of a more visible geopolitical dimension into corporate valuation.
Traditional technology analysis emphasizes revenue growth, margins, market share, capital expenditure and competitive positioning.
Strategically critical AI companies increasingly require another layer of analysis.
How much of their future economic value depends on institutional trust, secure technology and continued access to politically sensitive markets?
Five transmission mechanisms deserve particular attention.
1. The Governance Discount
A concentration of technological leadership and strategic relationships around individual executives can create substantial competitive advantages.
It can also create institutional vulnerabilities.
Nvidia’s investigation will test whether its governance and security arrangements are sufficiently robust.
A finding of no compromise would have different implications from evidence of material failures.
Investors must avoid treating allegations as established outcomes.
2. The Export-Control Discount
More restrictive controls could reduce addressable markets for advanced American semiconductors.
They could also stimulate investment in domestic alternatives and trusted supply chains.
The net valuation effect will depend on the relationship between lost sales, improved security and the long-term competitive response.
3. The Supplier-Diversification Premium
Customers may increasingly value alternative computing architectures, secure cloud platforms and systems that reduce dependence on a single supplier.
The opportunity extends to custom accelerators, networking, confidential computing and interoperable software.
But financial returns will depend on performance and total cost of ownership—not diversification alone.
4. The Counterintelligence Premium
Protecting strategic technology will require investment in cybersecurity, executive security, supply-chain verification and access controls.
These expenditures may become increasingly important for technology companies and critical infrastructure operators.
The financial value lies in strengthening operational reliability and reducing material risks without unnecessarily restricting legitimate collaboration.
5. The Sovereign-Capital Premium
Governments may increase investment in domestic semiconductors, AI infrastructure and secure computing.
This could create significant industrial opportunities.
But duplication of infrastructure and fragmented markets could raise aggregate capital requirements.
For fiscally constrained economies, technological sovereignty may therefore introduce a trade-off between strategic resilience and financial efficiency.
The next phase of the AI Supercycle will be increasingly shaped by the price investors place on geopolitical trust.
VI. THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS
WHAT THE NVIDIA AFFAIR COULD TRIGGER NEXT
The most significant consequences may emerge outside Nvidia.
The immediate question is whether the controversy remains company-specific or generates a broader institutional response.
Five developments deserve monitoring.
First, stronger export enforcement. Investigations could lead to more detailed scrutiny of semiconductor distribution channels, intermediaries and end customers.
Second, technological substitution. Customers and governments may accelerate investment in alternative processors and secure computing architectures.
Third, greater corporate-security expenditure.AI developers may reinforce controls over confidential research, executive communications and sensitive partnerships.
Fourth, supply-chain restructuring. Companies may adopt more geographically differentiated technology and distribution systems to comply with national regulations.
Fifth, a higher geopolitical discount rate. Investors may demand greater compensation for exposure to companies whose revenues depend materially on contested technologies or jurisdictions.
These developments are conditional.
They should not be confused with confirmed outcomes of the Nvidia investigation.
Nevertheless, the market has already begun assessing the risks.
Reuters reported that Nvidia faced a potential $470 billion reduction in market capitalization across November 9–10, reflecting the severity of investors’ immediate response. The figure is a change in equity market value, not a realized corporate cash loss.
The central analytical distinction remains:
A reputational crisis can change a company’s valuation. A sustained change in regulation, customer behavior or competitive position can change its economic value.
The latter is what investors must ultimately determine.
VII. EUROPE’S STRATEGIC OPENING
THE CASE FOR TRUSTED INDUSTRIAL INTELLIGENCE
The U.S.–China confrontation presents Europe with a fundamental strategic choice.
It can attempt to reproduce the extraordinary infrastructure investment of the American hyperscalers.
Or it can develop a differentiated competitive position grounded in the combination of secure computing, advanced industrial capabilities and productive physical assets.
These strategies are not mutually exclusive.
Europe requires access to frontier AI.
But its strongest comparative opportunities may lie in the application of intelligence to manufacturing, transportation, energy and critical infrastructure.
This creates the foundations of Trusted Industrial Intelligence.
The model combines technological capability with verified security, reliable infrastructure and measurable operating productivity.
Industrial AI can improve manufacturing throughput and maintenance efficiency.
Intelligent energy systems can improve grid utilization.
Railway and logistics platforms can increase asset availability and operating reliability.
Secure computing can protect sensitive industrial and public-sector information.
The financial objective is not simply to increase technology expenditure.
It is to raise the returns generated by capital already deployed.
Consider an illustrative European industrial company with €20 billion of invested capital.
A one-percentage-point increase in after-tax ROIC would generate approximately €200 million in additional annual operating profit, assuming invested capital remains unchanged.
The cost of deploying AI must also be considered.
But the underlying financial opportunity is substantial.
Europe’s next competitive advantage may emerge not from producing the greatest quantity of intelligence, but from becoming exceptionally effective at deploying intelligence across its physical economy.
This requires competitive energy, deeper capital markets, trusted technological systems and disciplined industrial execution.
Technological sovereignty becomes economically meaningful when it produces these outcomes.

For investors, the most important distinction will be between companies benefiting from strategic scarcity and companies capable of generating sustainable productivity.
Scarcity can create temporary pricing power.
Productivity determines whether that pricing power becomes durable economic value.
IX. THE NEXT 90 DAYS
THE EVOLUTIONARY PATH OF THE AI RACE
Three conditional paths deserve particular attention.
Controlled institutional response. Nvidia’s investigation establishes the relevant facts without identifying a material compromise. Export enforcement may still tighten, but broader AI investment continues. Valuations remain differentiated according to company fundamentals.
Accelerated strategic fragmentation. Governments introduce additional controls on sensitive technology, procurement and distribution. Companies increase investment in alternative architectures and regional supply chains. Security improves in selected areas while duplication and compliance costs rise.
Broader geopolitical transmission. Material findings or new incidents trigger substantial regulatory intervention, changes in market access or financing constraints. Investors reassess the value of globally integrated AI infrastructure.
These scenarios are not predictions of investigative findings.
Their purpose is to distinguish possible transmission mechanisms.
The most important indicators will be the outcome of Nvidia’s investigation, any confirmed security compromise, enforceable changes to export controls, changes in customer procurement and evidence of altered capital-investment plans.
The decisive question is whether security concerns begin changing the architecture of the AI industry rather than merely the valuation of individual companies.
CLOSING VIEW
THE NEW GEOGRAPHY OF ECONOMIC POWER
The Nvidia controversy has exposed a structural transformation in the global economy.
The most strategically important technologies are increasingly developed, financed and controlled by private corporations.
Yet their consequences extend directly into national security, international competition and the balance of geopolitical power.
This creates a new relationship between governments and companies.
Governments require access to advanced technology.
Companies require access to international markets, energy infrastructure and capital.
Both require secure supply chains and trustworthy institutions.
And both increasingly depend on technological systems extending beyond national boundaries.
The result is a more complex and potentially fragmented global economy.
The United States seeks to preserve its advantages in advanced computing and AI development.
China seeks to reduce dependence on foreign-controlled technologies and strengthen its industrial capabilities.
Europe must determine how to combine its industrial strengths with secure computing and greater financial competitiveness.
The consequences for investors are profound.
Technological leadership remains valuable.
But technological leadership without institutional security can become vulnerable.
National sovereignty can strengthen resilience.
But sovereignty pursued through excessive duplication can increase costs and reduce productivity.
And financial capital can support extraordinary innovation.
But innovation creates sustainable economic wealth only when its returns exceed the full cost of investment.
The AI race is therefore becoming a contest over the capacity to integrate technological capability, national security and economic productivity into a coherent competitive system.
That is the deeper significance of the Nvidia affair.
It is not simply a corporate scandal.
It is a warning that the global intelligence economy is entering a stage in which strategic information, institutional trust and the allocation of capital can no longer be separated.The most important competitive question is evolving.
Not merely who develops the most advanced intelligence.
But who can protect that intelligence, deploy it efficiently and transform it into durable economic power.
THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
THE NEXT LAW OF TECHNOLOGICAL SOVEREIGNTY
The industrial economy rewarded control over productive assets.
The digital economy rewarded control over platforms, networks and data.
The intelligence economy is creating a more demanding hierarchy.
Technology determines what can be developed.
Capital determines what can be financed.
Energy determines what can operate.
Security determines what can be protected.
Productivity determines what generates sustainable economic value.
The next generation of competitive advantage will belong to companies and economies capable of combining these dimensions without sacrificing innovation, financial discipline or institutional credibility.
THE FINAL VERDICT
The first phase of the AI Supercycle rewarded technological leadership.
The second mobilized unprecedented capital.
The third is revealing the geopolitical and institutional vulnerabilities of concentrated intelligence.
The fourth will reward the ability to transform trusted intelligence into superior industrial and financial productivity.
The greatest danger is not simply that strategic knowledge may be stolen.
It is that the response to technological insecurity could fragment the economic system required to make intelligence productive.
THE CLOSING LINE
The next global competition will not be decided solely by who controls the most advanced intelligence. It will be decided by who can protect it, finance it, deploy it and convert it into the greatest sustainable economic value.
In the Age of Velocity, technology creates power. Trust protects it. Productivity determines who compounds it.
