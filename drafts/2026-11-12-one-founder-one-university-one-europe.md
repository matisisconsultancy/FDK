Europe’s universities can give one-person AI companies what models alone cannot provide: a real problem, a place to prove the solution and a first customer.
The Big Take
China is asking individuals to build AI companies. Europe should ask its universities to make that possible for anyone with deep knowledge of a real problem.
A doctoral researcher who has spent three years studying battery degradation may understand a commercially valuable problem better than any generalist founder. A railway technician taking a continuing education course may know which recurring faults keep trains out of service and why existing diagnostic tools miss them. AI gives both the ability to build and test a product before they can afford to hire a software team.
The university can provide what neither can assemble alone. It can bring in a company willing to share data under controlled conditions, offer engineers and researchers who can challenge the product’s assumptions, arrange independent testing, and introduce a buyer with the authority to commission a paid deployment.
Europe’s universities should make founding a company a viable route for people with specialist knowledge. The strategic opportunity is to mobilize students, researchers, alumni and working professionals across the continent, including people who have never thought of themselves as technology entrepreneurs.
1. Redesign entrepreneurship around the individual
A one-person AI-native company is built from the outset around one accountable founder using AI to perform work that once required several functions. The founder can use agents to develop software, document it, prepare sales materials and support early customers. The founder remains responsible for the product, its performance and the decisions that carry operational consequences. Hiring can follow demand.
That changes what universities should teach. An engineer should learn to interview a customer, identify who owns the budget, build a prototype, test it against an existing process and price it against the value it delivers. A designer should learn to evaluate model outputs and calculate the cost of human review. A computer scientist should learn why a factory might reject a technically impressive product that requires too much integration. These skills belong in engineering, science, design and professional education as much as they belong in business schools.
Universities should offer a founder semester open to students, researchers, alumni and working professionals. An industrial partner brings a defined problem, names the person who can buy a solution and specifies the evidence needed to justify a paid trial. The founder builds and tests against that brief. Faculty assess the quality of the work and its evidence. The buyer makes a separate commercial decision. That division protects academic judgment and gives the founder a real customer to answer to.
2. Give one founder access to the capabilities of an institution
Universities can pool resources that would be expensive for a solo founder to buy early. A secure testing environment, specialist equipment, technical advisers and shared help with security, contracts and intellectual property can bring a prototype within reach of a serious buyer. Europe already has infrastructure to draw on. EuroHPC’s AI Factories connect supercomputing centers with universities, startups and industry, while the EU’s sectoral Testing and Experimentation Facilities support validation in real-world environments. Universities should give founders a single point of entry to those resources. 
The most valuable service may be independent proof. If an AI tool claims to reduce diagnostic time, the university can help design the test, specify the baseline and record failures, overrides and the cost of human review. A second buyer can then examine credible evidence before deciding what needs to be tested on its own equipment.That can shorten the sales cycle and give small suppliers access to customers that would otherwise favor established vendors.
Universities should also publish clear terms before projects begin. Founders need to know what they can commercialize, which data they may use and when university-owned research requires a license. A company built around an existing university invention can warrant different terms from one built by an alumnus using public tools and a teaching lab. Technology transfer offices should be judged in part by how quickly they resolve those questions and how readily a founder can explain the resulting rights to a customer or investor.
3. Organize the universities as a European network
A regional university can help a founder win a first customer. A network of universities can help the same founder reach a second market.
Imagine a founder working with a technical university in Turin on a system that detects faults in industrial machinery. A local manufacturer funds the first trial. A partner university in Germany reviews the evaluation and arranges a test with a buyer using different equipment. A third institution helps the founder adapt the product to another sector. Each institution contributes what it knows and gives the founder access to buyers they could scarcely reach alone.
The policy opportunity is to fund these partnerships around shared industrial problems and cross-border deployments. The European Institute of Innovation and Technology’s 2026 higher education call already commits €40 million to strengthening innovation and entrepreneurship capacity, with a focus on manufacturing and deep tech. It offers a starting point for consortia that bring universities, testing facilities and buyers together. An initial program could focus on machine maintenance, energy management and freight logistics, where customers can define the cost of the problem and verify the result. 
Funding should reward the steps that establish commercial viability. A paid trial shows a buyer values the problem. A production contract shows the solution can operate in the field. A second customer in another member state shows the company has a chance to scale. Universities should also track what happens to founders whose projects stop. An engineer who returns to industry able to evaluate and deploy AI can still carry considerable value into the economy.
4. What this means for capital markets
The university can improve the quality of the evidence available when capital first has to decide whether to back a founder. An independent evaluation establishes how the product performs. A paid deployment shows willingness to pay. A second installation reveals how much of the first deployment can be repeated. Together, those signals help investors judge whether they are looking at scalable software, a specialist services business or a research project that needs more time.
Who What to watch
Venture and growth investors The economics of the second installation. If deployment gets faster and requires less custom work, revenue can grow without a proportional increase in delivery costs.
Banks and other lenders Signed production orders, clear acceptance criteria and credible payment terms. These can support working-capital financing for eligible suppliers once delivery risk is understood.
Listed industrials and their investors Whether independently tested tools improve throughput, uptime or energy efficiency across multiple sites, after implementation and oversight costs. That is the evidence that can move earnings and returns on invested capital.
University boards and policymakers Time to the first paid trial, conversion to production and repeat sales across borders. These measures show whether academic support is creating companies that customers will sustain.
The Fundamental Question
Can a European university help one person turn what they know into something industry will buy across borders?
Europe’s universities have a role at the frontier of AI research.They also have a role in determining how widely the ability to build with AI is distributed. If they connect specialists to real problems, provide evidence that buyers can trust and use their European networks to open a second market, a founder’s prospects will depend less on where they study or whom they already know.
The first customer proves that one person can build a company. The second, across a border, proves that Europe can help it grow.
