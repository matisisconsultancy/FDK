South Korea — 
+0.1: AI finally crosses from semiconductor cycle into macro Velocity
This is today’s strongest admissible signal.
Official Korean customs data show exports in the first 20 days of September up 78.3% year on year to $71.4 billion, the highest 20-day performance on record, with semiconductor shipments up 259.4%. 
That measured evidence has now received powerful independent confirmation. The OECD raised South Korea’s 2026 growth forecast from 2.6% to 3.7%, while the Asian Development Bank separately increased its forecast from 2.6% to 3.2%, both citing strong exports, production and AI-semiconductor demand. 
The forecast itself does not earn GVI points.
The measured exports and production impulse do.
The causality chain has become visible:
global AI capex
→ HBM / memory demand
→ Korean semiconductor exports
→ industrial production
→ corporate earnings
→ investment
→ GDP acceleration.
This is exactly what productive technological diffusion should look like.
South Korea therefore rises from 88.4 to 88.5, primarily through Intelligence Infrastructure + Enterprise Transformation.
The constraint remains inflation: the OECD also raised Korea’s 2026 inflation projection to 3.0%. That prevents a larger adjustment.

United States — 
the economy is accelerating into a higher hurdle rate
The US Composite PMI surged from 56.0 to 58.4 in September, its highest level since July 2021. New orders reached their strongest reading since March 2022, work backlogs their highest since May 2022, and firms increased hiring. 
That is powerful measured Enterprise Transformation / absorption evidence.
But the same survey revealed the constraint: the input-price index jumped to 66.4, its highest since October 2022, with severe supply-chain pressure and insufficient operating capacity. 
Markets responded immediately.
The US 10-year Treasury yield jumped almost 14 basis points to 5.106%, its highest since 2007. The two-year reached 4.947% intraday, and markets moved the implied probability of another October Fed increase toward 70%. 
So the US signal is almost perfectly symmetrical:
economic Velocity ↑
inflation pressure ↑
interest rates ↑
cost of capital ↑.
The positive productive evidence and negative Smart Capital effect offset.
United States remains 94.3.

Germany — the strongest European operating signal in months
Germany’s Composite PMI rose from 51.8 to 53.8, the highest since October 2025 and far above expectations.
Services returned to expansion at 52.9, manufacturing remained at 53.8 and employment increased for a second consecutive month. 
That is meaningful.
But the GVI requires persistence before reversing Germany’s accumulated structural constraints. Input costs also rose at the fastest rate in four months, driven heavily by fuel and energy. 
The correct classification is therefore:
Enterprise Transformation: improving materially.
Smart Capital / energy friction: deteriorating.
Germany stays 83.2, but moves closer to an upgrade threshold.

France — recovery, but not yet escape velocity
France’s Composite PMI rose dramatically from 48.5 to 51.2, returning the private economy to expansion for the first time in months and marking the strongest pace in 25 months. 
But beneath the headline, the signal remains fragile.
Employment declined for a fifth consecutive month, new orders barely increased and business expectations weakened further. Input and selling prices also accelerated. 
That prevents any reversal of Saturday’s Smart Capital downgrade.
France remains 81.4.
The GVI interpretation is straightforward:
activity recovery = confirmed.
self-sustaining acceleration = not yet confirmed.

United Kingdom — digital finance advances as the macro mix worsens
Britain produced one genuinely important innovation signal overnight.
Lloyds, NatWest and Barclays completed the world’s first interbank transactions using tokenised commercial-bank deposits, including mortgage transactions; another test involving HSBC demonstrated programmable payments. The participating institutions now plan to move from pilot toward production and issue digital bonds settled through tokenised deposits in early 2027. 
This is meaningful Innovation Velocity + Enterprise Transformation evidence.
It receives no +0.1 because the transactions remain pilots.
Meanwhile Britain’s Composite PMI slipped from 52.5 to 51.7. Services weakened, inflationary pressure increased and S&P’s survey indicated quarterly growth of only around 0.1%. Manufacturing improved modestly to 52.0. 
The two signals offset.
UK remains 82.3.

Japan — 
capital is available, but increasingly expensive
SoftBank has now completed an $11.1 billion dollar/euro bond issuance to help finance its OpenAI investment.
The transaction is historic: Reuters describes it as the largest high-yield bond sale by an Asia-Pacific issuer. Dollar tranches carry coupons of 8.625%, 9.25% and 9.75%; SoftBank’s five-year CDS spread has climbed above 400 basis points, from around 280 in June. 
This is simultaneously positive and negative for Velocity.
Positive: global capital markets can still mobilise enormous amounts of capital for frontier AI.
Negative: the required return is becoming extremely high.
The sovereign signal reinforces the point. Japan’s 10-year government yield rose to 3.06%, a 30-year high, while the 30-year yield reached 4.12%. 
No downgrade is admitted because much of the monetary tightening was already reflected in Japan’s score.
But Smart Capital momentum deteriorates.
Japan remains 84.9.

Global macro validation — AI is now visible in GDP
The OECD’s new Interim Economic Outlook provides perhaps the strongest independent confirmation yet of the core GVI thesis.
It raised 2026 global growth from 2.8% to 2.9%, raised US growth to 2.2%, and explicitly identified investment in data centres and semiconductors as a major source of resilience, including through Japanese and Korean technology exports. 
But the OECD simultaneously warned that AI investment is becoming increasingly dependent on external financing, creating vulnerability if expected returns fail to materialise. It sees 2027 world growth at only 3.0% and G20 inflation at 3.6%, materially above its previous projection. 
That is extraordinarily close to the GVI’s emerging diagnosis:
AI is no longer merely a technology-sector phenomenon. It is becoming a macroeconomic variable.

Institutional interpretation — 
THE VELOCITY PARADOX
Today’s evidence exposes the most consequential paradox yet in the AI Supercycle.
AI investment is increasingly powerful enough to raise economic growth itself.
Korean semiconductor exports explode.
American business activity reaches a five-year high.
German activity accelerates.
The OECD revises world growth upward.
But precisely because the real economy is stronger than expected, inflation remains harder to suppress.
That keeps bond yields elevated.
Which raises the hurdle rate on the very AI infrastructure driving the growth.
The circular chain is becoming:
AI CAPEX → PRODUCTIVITY + DEMAND → GROWTH → CAPACITY PRESSURE → INFLATION → HIGHER RATES → HIGHER AI HURDLE RATE
That is The Velocity Paradox.
Main accelerator — productive diffusion
South Korea provides today’s cleanest case.
AI demand is no longer confined to Nvidia revenue or hyperscaler capex.
It is propagating through:
memory → exports → factories → earnings → national growth.
That is the transition from an AI trade to an AI economy.
Main decelerator — the price of acceleration
The 5.11% US 10-year yield is now arguably more consequential for the next phase of the AI cycle than another incremental model benchmark. 
At that hurdle rate, capital allocation becomes unforgiving.
Projects need:
secured power + contracted demand + high utilisation + superior productivity + credible refinancing.

Implications
For investors, the next Great Split should increasingly separate AI capacity that produces cash flow from AI capacity that merely consumes capital. Korea’s export data show what productive conversion looks like; SoftBank’s financing costs show the price of getting it wrong.
For executives, the decisive KPI is no longer AI adoption or even AI revenue. It is:
incremental operating cash flow generated per dollar of fully loaded AI capital employed.
For policymakers, the OECD evidence confirms that AI has become large enough to influence national growth trajectories. The policy challenge is therefore no longer simply fostering innovation. It is combining technology, energy, skills, capital markets and macroeconomic stability without allowing the resulting investment boom to destabilise inflation or public finances. 
Bottom line
The FDK Global Velocity Index remains at 108.0 on 24 September 2026. South Korea rises from 88.4 to 88.5; rankings remain unchanged.
But today marks an important conceptual inflection.
For the first time, the evidence is increasingly showing AI simultaneously on both sides of the macroeconomic equation:
AI is supporting growth.
And—
AI is contributing to the conditions keeping capital expensive.
That leads to the next refinement of the FDK framework:
VELOCITY = PRODUCTIVE INTELLIGENCE × ABSORPTION CAPACITY ÷ COST OF CAPITAL
The next winners will not simply deploy the most intelligence.
They will be those capable of making intelligence compound faster than its financing cost.
That may now be the defining competitive test of the Next Economy.
