Sánchez, Milei, the PP and Vox: How Artificial Intelligence, Political Spectacle and Cross-Border Confrontation Are Transforming the November 29 Election
Spain’s electoral campaign is entering a new communications environment. Political leaders are competing not only over economic policy, parliamentary majorities and institutional credibility, but also over the ability to dominate attention, define political narratives and influence the information reaching voters.
The significance extends beyond Spain.
Political communication practices associated with Donald Trump in the United States, Javier Milei in Argentina and Roberto Vannacci in Italy are increasingly part of an international debate over provocative messaging, digital mobilization and synthetic political content.
Spain now provides a particularly revealing case.
Within three days, the country has witnessed a public confrontation involving the Argentine president, a controversial AI-generated campaign video from the Partido Popular, a similar intervention by Vox and an unusually informal television appearance by Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez.
These events do not demonstrate that any one political strategy will succeed electorally.
They do reveal how rapidly the mechanisms of political competition are changing.
The fundamental question is whether the expansion of algorithmically distributed political communication will deepen democratic participation—or make institutional credibility increasingly vulnerable to synthetic content, fragmented attention and international political confrontation.
I. THE FIVE SIGNALS
What Happened Between October 21 and October 23
1. Sánchez Moves From Parliament to Entertainment Television
On October 22, Sánchez appeared on RTVE’s La Revuelta, hosted by David Broncano, becoming the first candidate of the current electoral cycle to accept the program’s invitation.
The discussion covered housing affordability, corruption controversies, the management of the Ceuta migration crisis, artificial intelligence and social-media algorithms.
Sánchez acknowledged disappointment over corruption cases involving former political associates and said aspects of the Ceuta crisis could have been better managed.
2. Milei Brings International Political Conflict Into the Spanish Campaign
During an October 21 appearance in Madrid, Argentine President Javier Milei used sharply hostile language toward Sánchez, calling him a dictator and corrupt and urging the removal of the Socialists from government.
Spain’s Foreign Ministry condemned the remarks, characterizing aspects of them as interference and incitement to violence.
The exchange has become an international diplomatic and campaign controversy.
3. The PP Introduces Synthetic Political Impersonation
The Partido Popular released a 51-second AI-generated video depicting simulated versions of politicians and figures connected with the PSOE, including former Prime Minister José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero and former minister José Luis Ábalos.
The video assigns fictional statements concerning alleged wrongdoing to these representations.
The PSOE has considered legal action. PP representatives argue that the content is recognizable as satire rather than authentic testimony.
4. Vox Expands the AI-Driven Digital Contest
Vox subsequently circulated synthetic political content targeting figures associated with the Socialist Party.
El País reported that Vox has approximately 1.2 million Instagram followers and 1.1 million followers on TikTok, illustrating its substantial presence on social platforms.
These figures measure account audiences, not voter support or persuasion.
5. The Italian Comparison: Vannacci and Synthetic Political Imagery
In July, controversy followed an AI-generated Italian political video depicting opposition figures being executed in an imagined Roman arena, with Roberto Vannacci represented as an emperor directing the scene.The episode illustrates the use of synthetic imagery to produce provocative political satire or spectacle.
It does not establish that the Italian and Spanish campaigns share the same organizers, objectives or effects.
II. THE HIDDEN PATTERN
Political Communication Is Moving From Persuasion Through Argument to Competition Over Attention
Historically, election campaigns relied heavily on party organizations, newspapers, television debates and speeches.
Digital platforms altered the distribution of political information.
Generative AI introduces an additional change: political actors can now produce convincing simulations of people, speech and events at relatively low cost.
Three processes are increasingly intertwined.
Personalization. Leaders use informal formats to create familiarity and communicate outside traditional parliamentary settings.
Provocation. Confrontational remarks and striking imagery can attract disproportionate attention.
Synthetic representation. AI-generated videos can depict public figures saying or doing things they never actually said or did.
These are distinct practices. An entertainment interview is not equivalent to a fabricated video, and provocative political speech is not necessarily synthetic misinformation.
Nevertheless, they compete in the same attention environment.
Sánchez’s interview represents one form of direct political communication. Milei’s intervention represents internationalized confrontation. The PP and Vox videos demonstrate the growing capacity to manufacture political scenes through AI.
The common mechanism is the increasingly rapid transformation of political communication into shareable digital content.

The model identifies plausible communication channels. It does not establish that exposure changes voting preferences.
The major uncertainty lies in the final stage.
Millions of views do not demonstrate millions of persuaded voters.
Highly visible content may mobilize supporters, provoke rejection, entertain politically disengaged audiences or have little lasting impact.
The electoral effects require representative surveys, reliable engagement analysis and subsequent behavioral evidence.
The new advantage may be the ability to shape the political agenda before voters begin evaluating detailed policy proposals. But agenda visibility and electoral persuasion remain different outcomes.

IV. THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS
Why This Could Change the Economics of Political Competition
1. Political Agenda Formation May Become Faster and Less Predictable
Generative AI reduces the time and cost required to create sophisticated political media.
A single video can generate multiple rounds of coverage, reactions and legal debate.
This could increase the speed at which political controversies dominate public attention.
It may also make campaign agendas more reactive.
However, whether this changes voting behavior remains an empirical question.
2. Authenticity Becomes an Institutional Asset
When artificial content convincingly imitates political figures, voters must distinguish real statements from simulated ones.
Even clearly satirical material can create disputes over whether an audience recognizes it as fictional.
Conversely, the widespread availability of synthetic content can make genuine recordings easier to dispute.
This is sometimes described as the liar’s dividend: the possibility that authentic evidence loses credibility because convincing fabrications also exist.
The institutional response should include prominent labeling, reliable provenance, independent verification and effective remedies for deceptive impersonation.
3. International Political Conflict Becomes Domestic Campaign Material
Milei’s remarks illustrate an emerging political transmission channel.
Statements by foreign leaders can enter a domestic campaign almost immediately.
Such interventions may provoke disputes over diplomatic norms, democratic sovereignty and political solidarity.
The actual effect on public opinion cannot be presumed.
But the episode demonstrates how international events can become part of the national political agenda without requiring formal party cooperation.
This becomes particularly important as Spain approaches its election ahead of the 2027 cycles in France and Italy.
4. Traditional Media Accountability Faces New Competition
Sánchez’s appearance on La Revuelta demonstrates how entertainment formats can provide access to audiences beyond conventional political interviews.
This can expand political participation and generate public discussion.
It can also raise questions about whether informal formats provide sufficient scrutiny of policy claims.
The answer depends on the questions asked, the follow-up and the availability of alternative journalistic examination.
RTVE’s own coverage confirms that Sánchez addressed corruption controversies, housing and Ceuta rather than limiting the appearance to entertainment.
The key issue is not whether politicians should appear on entertainment programs.
It is whether citizens retain access to substantive, verifiable information across the media environment.
5. Election-Related Uncertainty Can Affect Financial Markets Through Policy Credibility
Political spectacle does not automatically move bond yields or corporate valuations.
The financial transmission arises when uncertainty affects expectations about government formation, taxation, regulation, fiscal execution and investment policy.
A prolonged period of contradictory economic proposals or unclear governing agreements can complicate investment decisions.But market participants must distinguish temporary political noise from changes in expected cash flows and funding conditions.
The relevant financial risk is not that campaigns become more confrontational. It is that uncertainty over future policy becomes sufficiently persistent to alter the cost or timing of productive investment.

VII. FIVE TAKEAWAYS
1. The campaign has become an international information event. Milei’s intervention, the Spanish party videos and comparisons with Italian political communication demonstrate how developments can cross borders rapidly.
2. Synthetic political content creates a new credibility problem. The central concerns involve authenticity, clearly identified satire, the impersonation of real people and the availability of reliable information.
3. Media visibility is not electoral influence. Viral video counts and televised appearances provide evidence of exposure, not proof of voter persuasion or shifts in party support.
4. The next five weeks require separating political communication from policy commitments. Financial markets will ultimately respond more durably to changes in fiscal, regulatory and investment expectations than to isolated campaign controversies.
5. Spain offers an important test for Europe’s 2027 electoral environment. Developments in Spanish campaigning and parliamentary government formation may become reference points in France and Italy, but any causal effects will require careful observation.
CLOSING VIEW
WHEN POLITICS BECOMES SYNTHETIC, CREDIBILITY BECOMES MORE VALUABLEThe events of October 21–23 reveal a change in the infrastructure of democratic competition.
A prime minister communicates through an entertainment program.
A foreign president intervenes in another country’s political debate.
Political parties create simulations of their opponents through generative AI.
And campaign controversies circulate through digital networks capable of reaching large audiences almost immediately.
None of these developments, taken alone, determines an election.
Together, they increase the importance of verifying what is real, identifying what is manufactured and preserving the distinction between political communication and evidence.
The implications extend beyond electoral politics.
The same technological capabilities transforming production, finance and business are changing the institutions through which societies form political judgments.
Artificial intelligence can make information more accessible.
It can also make fabrication more convincing.
Social platforms can expand participation.
They can also reward emotionally intense material without establishing its accuracy.
Informal communication can humanize political figures.
It cannot substitute for accountable government.
For Spain, the November 29 election will be a test of democratic choice within this changing information environment.
For Europe, it will provide another opportunity to examine how technological change interacts with political legitimacy and institutional credibility.
And for investors, it reinforces the importance of separating campaign narratives from policy outcomes and financial fundamentals.
The deeper challenge is not to eliminate provocation, satire or disagreement from political life.
It is to preserve the conditions under which citizens can assess competing claims accurately and make their own decisions.
The next generation of democracy will not be defined solely by who controls the most powerful algorithms. It will also depend on whether public institutions, political parties and independent media can sustain a shared basis of verifiable facts.
THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
In the industrial age, political power depended heavily on control over institutions. In the digital age, it increasingly depended on control over attention. In the Age of Pervasive Intelligence, the enduring question will be who can earn trust when appearances themselves can be manufactured.
Technology can amplify a political message. Only credibility can give it lasting legitimacy.
