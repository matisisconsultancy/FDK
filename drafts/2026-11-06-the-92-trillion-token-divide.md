China is accelerating the consumption of intelligence while access to advanced computing remains constrained. America is discovering that technological leadership must also withstand financial-market discipline.

MATERIAL INFLECTION ALERT — HIGH SIGNIFICANCE

THE BIG TAKE

Three developments reported on November 5–6 materially strengthen our central thesis: the United States retains important advantages at the AI frontier, while China is increasingly challenging the economics, distribution and industrial scalability of intelligence.

The most consequential development is not a new benchmark victory. It is evidence of accelerating Chinese demand for operational AI.

DeepSeek reports that its agent platform now processes approximately 92 trillion tokens daily, while 600,000 Nvidia H200 processors reportedly remain in Hong Kong awaiting Chinese import approval.

Meanwhile, China’s largest NAND flash-memory manufacturer is preparing to increase its reliance on domestic semiconductor-production equipment.

Together, these developments reveal an increasingly important distinction:

China’s capacity to consume intelligence is expanding faster than its ability to secure complete independence from foreign computing infrastructure.

The next stage of competition will depend on how quickly that imbalance can be reduced.

1. DEEPSEEK — FROM LOW-COST MODELS TO INDUSTRIAL-SCALE CONSUMPTION

On November 5, DeepSeek disclosed that its Harness agent platform had reached approximately 1.54 million daily active users, up from 271,000 on September 30.

The company also reported approximately 92 trillion tokens processed daily and 35,000 third-party plugins.

These figures are company-reported, cover users accessing Harness through DeepSeek’s official API and have not been independently audited. Token volume should not be confused with paid consumption, unique human activity or completed economic tasks. 

Nevertheless, the scale and direction are significant.

The competitive challenge is moving beyond inexpensive models toward the formation of an agent ecosystem capable of generating substantial recurring computing demand.

An additional development reinforces this trajectory. On November 5, Ant Group released Ling-3.2-flash, a native multimodal model with 560 billion total parameters, approximately 25 billion activated parameters and a one-million-token context window.

The model supports text, image and video inputs and targets complex agentic workflows. Its commercial economics and comparative performance still require independent evaluation. 

Why it matters: China’s challenge is evolving from producing intelligence cheaply to distributing increasingly sophisticated intelligence across an expanding application ecosystem.

2. THE COMPUTING CONTRADICTION — 600,000 NVIDIA CHIPS AWAIT APPROVAL

Reuters reported on November 5 that approximately 600,000 Nvidia H200 processors were being held in Hong Kong pending approval for large-scale importation into mainland China.

Although Washington has authorized certain H200 sales, Beijing continues to restrict imports as it seeks to reduce dependence on American technology.

Chinese technology companies still need these processors for demanding AI workloads. 

This exposes a critical contradiction.

China is encouraging domestic semiconductor substitution while its leading AI developers continue to value access to American accelerated computing.

The consequences could extend beyond hardware availability.

Restrictions create incentives to optimize Chinese models for domestic accelerators, develop alternative software stacks and improve inference efficiency.

But they may also increase costs and slow frontier-model training.

The strategic test is whether China can turn computing scarcity into a durable efficiency advantage rather than a persistent competitive handicap.

3. SEMICONDUCTOR SOVEREIGNTY — THE NEXT INDUSTRIAL BOTTLENECKOn November 6, Reuters reported that Yangtze Memory Technologies intends to source at least 60%, potentially 70%, of the semiconductor-manufacturing equipment for its planned fourth Wuhan factory from Chinese suppliers.

The factory is expected to manufacture advanced NAND memory for data centers and AI applications.

Construction is planned for 2027, but supplier negotiations remain preliminary and no formal orders have been placed.

China continues to depend on foreign equipment for certain sophisticated manufacturing processes. Crucially, NAND storage is distinct from the high-bandwidth memory used alongside advanced AI accelerators. 

The development therefore represents an important industrial-localization ambition, not proof that China has overcome its most consequential AI semiconductor dependencies.

Its significance lies in the direction of capital investment: China is attempting to convert geopolitical restrictions into domestic manufacturing demand.

THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT — TWO DIFFERENT AI CAPITAL CYCLES

The emerging divergence has important financial consequences.

In China, DeepSeek’s expanding agent ecosystem suggests that demand for intelligence can accelerate through inexpensive models, developer adoption and commercial integration.

In the United States, the financial burden of sustaining frontier leadership is becoming increasingly visible.

On November 6, SoftBank shares declined 10.8%, while its five-year credit-default-swap spread reached approximately 466 basis points, a four-year high.

Investors are questioning the leverage and financing commitments supporting major AI investments, including SoftBank’s reported $64.6 billion investment in OpenAI. 

The comparison does not demonstrate that China’s AI industry is more profitable.

It reveals two different risks.

America must prove that its enormous investment in frontier intelligence can generate sustainable returns. China must prove that widespread adoption of relatively inexpensive intelligence can generate sustainable profits despite computing constraints.

Both systems face the same ultimate financial test: the relationship between productive output and the capital required to generate it.

WHAT TO EXPECT NEXT

Five indicators deserve particular attention.

1. DeepSeek’s agent economics: Whether rising token consumption produces recurring revenues, attractive margins and measurable productivity gains.
2. Chinese computing independence: Whether domestic accelerators achieve competitive performance and system-level costs in commercial deployment.
3. Nvidia’s China exposure: Whether Beijing relaxes H200 import restrictions or increasingly redirects demand toward domestic alternatives.
4. Frontier-model convergence: Whether DeepSeek, Alibaba, Tencent or Ant Group demonstrate independently verified improvements that materially narrow America’s remaining technological advantages.
5. AI capital-market discipline: Whether infrastructure developers can sustain investment as financing spreads widen and investors demand stronger free-cash-flow visibility.

THE INVESTMENT VERDICT

Today’s developments strengthen our thesis without establishing a reversal of American technological leadership.

China’s expanding agent activity is an increasingly important demand signal. Its semiconductor-localization plans reflect a determined effort to reduce strategic dependencies.

But neither rising token consumption nor announced manufacturing capacity guarantees economic returns.

For investors, the opportunity increasingly lies in identifying where growing demand translates into durable profitability.

For boards, the relevant measure is shifting from model capability alone toward the cost of successfully completing an economically valuable task.

For policymakers, the challenge is to preserve technological advantages without allowing restrictive policies to accelerate the formation of competitive alternative ecosystems.

THE FINAL TAKE

The AI Supercycle is entering a more demanding stage.Technological leadership remains essential, but it is no longer sufficient.

The United States continues to command significant advantages in frontier intelligence and advanced computing. China is increasingly challenging the economics of deploying that intelligence at scale.

The distinction between innovation leadership and economic-value capture will become progressively more important.

America is competing to extend the frontier of intelligence. China is competing to make intelligence an increasingly ubiquitous economic input.

The next decisive advantage will belong to the system that converts intelligence into productivity at the lowest sustainable cost of capital.
