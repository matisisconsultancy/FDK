THE CAPITAL THAT DOES NOT FOLLOW CONTROL
The EBA’s New Ruling on MPS, Mediobanca and Generali Exposes a Critical Constraint on European Banking Consolidation
Why Regulatory Capital, Institutional History and the Economics of Integration Are Becoming Decisive Drivers of Financial Valuation
Where Continental Scale, Capital Allocation and Financial Sovereignty Collide


⸻


THE BIG TAKE
The most consequential development in European banking this weekend is not another acquisition proposal, another earnings record or another political intervention. It is a regulatory clarification exposing a fundamental limitation in the economics of financial consolidation: acquiring control of an institution does not necessarily mean acquiring the regulatory capital advantages embedded in its history.
On November 7, Monte dei Paschi di Siena disclosed the European Banking Authority’s response concerning the prudential treatment of Mediobanca’s investment in Assicurazioni Generali.
The EBA confirmed that the historical exemption available to Mediobanca under Article 495a(3) of the Capital Requirements Regulation cannot automatically be extended to the consolidated MPS group or transferred to another group entity.
The clarification has an important qualification.
MPS explicitly stated that the decision has no impact on its current consolidated capital ratios because the group has never applied that exemption.
The issue is therefore not an immediate deterioration in MPS’s reported capital position.
It is the limitation imposed on potential future organizational structures and regulatory-capital benefits. 
This distinction becomes particularly relevant as Intesa Sanpaolo advances toward its acquisition of MPS, Mediobanca remains a strategically valuable financial-services franchise, and Generali occupies a central position in the prospective ownership architecture.
The EBA decision also reveals a broader asymmetry.
UniCredit received ECB authorization in September to apply favorable Danish Compromise treatment to its qualifying insurance holdings, with an estimated improvement of approximately 52 basis points in its CET1 ratio.
MPS, by contrast, cannot automatically inherit Mediobanca’s historically established exemption at consolidated-group level.
These are different regulatory applications, not directly comparable permissions. Yet together they demonstrate that the treatment of financial conglomerates can materially influence capital efficiency. 
Europe’s banking consolidation is increasingly becoming a contest over three different forms of capital: financial capital that can be acquired, regulatory capital whose treatment depends on legal conditions, and institutional capital embedded in historical ownership structures.
The ability to combine all three will determine which institutions can translate Continental Scale into sustainable shareholder returns.
THE FIVE SIGNALS THAT MATTER
1. The EBA has clarified a consequential regulatory boundary. Historical capital exemptions cannot automatically migrate across institutions or consolidation perimeters.
2. MPS faces no immediate reported capital-ratio impact. Its November 7 announcement explicitly confirms that the grandfathering exemption was never applied to the group on a consolidated basis.
3. Future restructuring flexibility becomes more important. The treatment of Mediobanca’s Generali participation requires careful assessment under alternative corporate structures.
4. UniCredit enters its November 9 earnings announcement with a potentially material capital-efficiency advantage already authorized by the ECB. The approximately 52-basis-point Danish Compromise benefit reinforces the importance of distinguishing recurring operating performance from regulatory effects.
5. The next valuation premium will depend on the capital consequences of integration. Investors must evaluate not simply the earnings acquired, but the regulatory capital required to retain and deploy those earnings.The emerging thesis: Europe is discovering that ownership can be transferred more easily than the regulatory advantages associated with ownership.

1. WHAT CHANGED IN THE LAST 24 HOURS
1.1 The EBA ruling becomes the weekend’s decisive development
On November 7 at 17:08, MPS published a statement concerning the treatment of its indirect investment in Generali.
The announcement followed the publication on November 6 of EBA Q&A 2026_7945, addressing the application of Article 495a(3) CRR.
The EBA examined whether an exemption available to a bank holding an insurance participation could extend to its new parent group following an acquisition.
It also considered whether the exemption could transfer to another institution receiving the relevant participation through an internal restructuring.
The answer establishes a clear principle.
Eligibility must be assessed independently for each institution and each applicable consolidated supervisory perimeter.
The historical exemption available to an acquired subsidiary does not automatically become available to the acquiring group.
Similarly, transferring an eligible insurance participation to another legal entity does not automatically transfer the associated exemption.
The ruling identifies historical ownership conditions as a central constraint. 
For the Italian banking contest, this is an important clarification because Mediobanca’s substantial Generali participation forms part of the financial architecture that Intesa seeks to acquire indirectly through MPS.
What is confirmed
Mediobanca’s existing treatment at individual-entity level does not automatically extend to MPS.
The EBA requires an independent assessment at consolidated level.
The EBA also rejects automatic transferability of the historical exemption when the participation moves to another group entity.
MPS confirms that its existing consolidated ratios remain unaffected.
What remains unresolved
The final economic implications for a future Intesa–MPS–Mediobanca structure have not been quantified.
Nor does the EBA decision establish that Intesa must dispose of the Generali participation or that the acquisition’s projected synergies have been reduced by a specific amount.
The appropriate conclusion is regulatory clarification, not an announced capital shortfall.
This is the distinction investors must preserve.
1.2 MPS’s earnings reinforce the importance of the ruling
The regulatory development follows MPS’s November 6 financial results.
The group reported €1.657 billion in nine-month net income, an increase of 21.2%.
Its fully loaded CET1 ratio reached 16.4%, with a buffer of approximately 680 basis points above its stated regulatory requirement.
MPS also raised its 2026 pre-tax profit guidance above €3.7 billion.
Management attributed approximately €150 million of the improvement to costs no longer expected following shareholders’ rejection of the proposed Mediobanca merger.
However, the bank must issue additional senior debt before year-end to satisfy regulatory funding requirements under its revised organizational structure. 
These two developments belong in the same investment framework.
MPS is demonstrating stronger profitability and substantial capital buffers.
At the same time, regulatory funding and capital-treatment requirements are influencing its organizational choices.
The earnings capacity of a financial institution and the regulatory efficiency of its corporate structure are separate sources of economic value.
1.3 No new equity-market session
Sunday, November 8, is not a regular trading day in Milan.
The latest available closing prices are therefore from Friday, November 6.
Importantly, MPS published its EBA clarification on Saturday evening, after the Friday trading session.
Consequently, Friday’s share-price movements cannot be interpreted as a market reaction to the November 7 corporate announcement.
The first regular trading opportunity for investors to reassess that disclosure is Monday, November 9.
That timing makes tomorrow’s market opening particularly relevant.

2.THE DECISIVE ISSUE — REGULATORY CAPITAL IS NOT AUTOMATICALLY TRANSFERABLE
The EBA’s ruling exposes a distinction that is frequently underestimated in banking acquisitions.
Financial ownership, regulatory eligibility and capital efficiency are not interchangeable.
Consider the institutional structure.
Mediobanca historically held a substantial participation in Generali.
Certain legacy holdings can benefit from preferential prudential treatment where the applicable historical and regulatory conditions are satisfied.
MPS subsequently acquired control of Mediobanca.
The question was whether the historical treatment could also apply to MPS at consolidated-group level.
The EBA’s answer was negative in the circumstances described.
The exemption cannot be inherited simply because a parent company acquires control of an eligible subsidiary.
The three dimensions of capital
First: Accounting Capital.
This represents equity recognized under the applicable accounting framework.
Acquisitions can change consolidated accounting equity through goodwill, purchase-price allocation and other adjustments.
Second: Regulatory Capital.
This represents the capital eligible to satisfy prudential requirements.
Its measurement depends on deductions, risk weights, regulatory permissions and the composition of the consolidated group.
Third: Deployable Capital.
This represents the resources an institution can actually distribute, reinvest or allocate without breaching capital, funding and supervisory constraints.
These three measures can evolve differently.
A transaction can increase consolidated earnings without increasing deployable capital proportionately.
An acquisition can improve distribution capabilities while simultaneously increasing risk-weighted assets.
And a restructuring can preserve the economic ownership of an investment while changing the regulatory treatment available to the institution holding it.
Why this matters for Intesa–MPS
Intesa’s strategic rationale includes acquiring Mediobanca’s financial-services capabilities and retaining significant exposure to Generali.
The economic attractiveness of that structure depends on more than the profitability of the businesses involved.
It also depends on how those investments are treated for regulatory-capital purposes after the acquisition and any subsequent reorganization.
The November 6 EBA clarification does not determine the entire future capital treatment of an Intesa-controlled structure.
But it establishes that a historical exemption cannot simply be assumed to survive a change in the relevant legal entity or consolidated supervisory perimeter.
That restriction can influence the choice between maintaining subsidiaries, transferring assets and undertaking corporate mergers.
The investment implication
The correct acquisition model should distinguish:
Operating Synergies → Accounting Earnings → Prudential Capital Adjustments → Distributable Economic Value.
The final stage is the one that matters most for shareholders.
A banking acquisition creates sustainable value only when incremental earnings exceed the full economic cost of the capital required to generate them.

UniCredit–Commerzbank: An identifiable capital-efficiency advantage
UniCredit’s September 8 announcement confirmed ECB authorization to apply Danish Compromise treatment to qualifying consolidated insurance participations.
The bank estimated a CET1 benefit of approximately 52 basis points based on its second-quarter position.
This is a regulatory benefit relating to specific qualifying insurance investments.
It must not be interpreted as a general exemption from capital requirements or as applying automatically to all financial holdings. 
The distinction becomes important as UniCredit pursues Commerzbank.
Its strategic opportunity is to create a larger financial platform serving European corporations and households.
Its principal challenge remains the conversion of ownership influence into operational integration.
Commerzbank’s November 5 results showed third-quarter earnings of €859 million, up approximately 45%.
CEO Bettina Orlopp acknowledged potential value creation from combining the banks’ capabilities.
Nevertheless, disagreements remain over governance, employment, consideration and the implementation process. 
UniCredit’s opportunity is increasingly about combining Continental Scale with capital efficiency. Its unresolved risk is the cost of institutional integration.
Intesa Sanpaolo: The capital-allocation benchmark
Intesa’s €8.199 billion nine-month profit provides substantial evidence of operating efficiency.
The MPS acquisition could extend the institution’s capabilities in wealth management, corporate financing and investment banking.
However, the November 7 EBA clarification reinforces the need to distinguish financial integration from regulatory-capital optimization.
Intesa must also complete the outstanding competition and securities-regulatory processes.
Reports published November 6 suggest that Italy’s competition authority is broadly satisfied with proposed remedies but may require additional branch disposals.
That remains market reporting rather than formal clearance. 
Intesa’s investment case remains strong. The decisive question is how much incremental capital productivity the final integrated structure can deliver.
MPS–Mediobanca: The institutional-history premium
MPS’s latest results demonstrate substantial operating momentum.
Mediobanca contributes differentiated capabilities and a strategically important Generali investment.
But the EBA decision introduces an important analytical distinction.
Historical ownership can have economic consequences under prudential regulation.
The relevant exemption cannot simply be recreated through the acquisition of a financial institution.
This does not imply that maintaining separate legal entities is necessarily optimal.
It means that alternative restructuring plans must be evaluated on their own regulatory and economic merits.
Mediobanca’s value includes specialized capabilities and institutional relationships. The prudential treatment of those relationships depends on the legal structure in which they are maintained.
Crédit Agricole–Banco BPM: Strategic optionality remains intact
Banco BPM CEO Giuseppe Castagna indicated on November 5 that the bank would examine a potential transaction involving Crédit Agricole.
No binding proposal has been announced.
Crédit Agricole remains Banco BPM’s largest shareholder, with approximately 29.6%.
Banco BPM’s strong earnings support its ability to negotiate from a position of financial strength. 
The relevant acquisition economics extend beyond banking branches.
Banco BPM’s ownership of Anima creates important exposure to asset management and financial distribution.
This is particularly relevant because the EBA has previously clarified limits on extending favorable insurance-related prudential treatment to asset-management businesses held through insurers.
That earlier issue is distinct from the November 6 ruling affecting MPS and Mediobanca. 
The strategic lesson is consistent.
Complex financial conglomerates require capital-allocation models that distinguish banking, insurance and asset-management activities rather than treating their earnings as prudentially interchangeable.
Generali and Banca Generali: The value of financial specialization
Generali remains strategically important because of its insurance operations, investment capabilities and ownership connections to Mediobanca and Delfin.
The latest EBA clarification concerns the treatment of an insurance participation within a banking group.
It does not question Generali’s solvency position or establish a deterioration in its standalone insurance operations.
Banca Generali presents a different investment proposition.
Following the rejection of MPS’s acquisition plan, its valuation should rest principally on customer assets, net inflows, recurring commissions and distribution productivity.
Its November 10 results will be important in establishing the strength of that standalone investment case.
BPER–Unipol: Integration remains the principal risk
BPER reported approximately €2.01 billion in adjusted nine-month earnings.
Unipol has completed financing intended to support the prospective acquisition of the MPS banking perimeter following a successful Intesa transaction.
The proposed transfer involves approximately 635 branches.
The opportunity is considerable, but the institution must also manage its existing integration commitments.
For investors, the decisive question remains the incremental capital required to absorb the transferred businesses and the operating returns generated afterward.

7. POLITICAL AND REGULATORY IMPLICATIONS
Frankfurt — Prudential rules become strategic variables
The November 6 EBA decision illustrates the extent to which prudential regulation can influence acquisition economics.
The ECB’s supervisory responsibilities concern the resilience and capital adequacy of banking institutions.
But its decisions and the interpretation of European prudential rules can also affect the economic attractiveness of different corporate structures.
The critical distinction is between supervisory authorization and shareholder value.
An institution can satisfy the applicable capital requirements while generating a lower return on the capital employed than alternative organizational structures might deliver.

This is a relevant forward-looking consideration.
Financial consolidation can change the systemic importance of an institution and therefore the capital framework within which it operates.
Brussels — The Banking Union Discount becomes more tangible
The Banking Union is intended to strengthen financial stability and facilitate the operation of financial institutions across the euro area.
Yet important differences remain between prudential integration and the broader legal, political and fiscal conditions under which banks operate.
The UniCredit–Banco BPM Golden Power controversy remains an important precedent.
In June 2025, the European Commission had approved UniCredit’s proposed Banco BPM acquisition subject to competition commitments.
The subsequent failure of the transaction illustrates that competition clearance and national political acceptance are separate dimensions of European consolidation. 
The wider Golden Power dispute remains relevant to Brussels’ concerns about the use of national intervention powers in banking transactions.
No new Golden Power decision directly affecting the Intesa–MPS acquisition was verified over the weekend.
Rome — The fiscal contradiction persists
Italy’s proposed 2027 budget includes additional taxation of banks and insurers.
The government approved a €26 billion fiscal package on October 28, with more than €5 billion expected from financial-sector measures.
The proposals include higher corporate tax rates for financial institutions and changes affecting financial transactions.
The legislation remains subject to parliamentary approval. 
The economic implications deserve careful treatment.The government’s expected fiscal revenue is not equivalent to an equal reduction in the banking sector’s accounting net income.
Different tax measures affect cash flows, retained earnings and transactions differently.
Nevertheless, higher taxation can reduce the incremental after-tax return available from domestic banking capital.
This creates an important policy tension.
Italy seeks larger and more competitive financial institutions while potentially reducing the financial returns available to support their expansion.
Europe cannot evaluate Banking Union independently of the fiscal and political conditions under which capital is allocated.

8. THE HIDDEN PATTERN — THE NON-TRANSFERABILITY OF FINANCIAL ADVANTAGE
The EBA ruling reveals a deeper characteristic of European banking.
Not every competitive advantage owned by a financial institution can be transferred through an acquisition.
This proposition has implications well beyond Article 495a(3).
First — Institutional history can have an economic value
Historical ownership conditions can determine eligibility for specific regulatory provisions.
This creates a form of institutional path dependence.
Two banks holding economically similar assets may face different prudential treatment because their ownership histories and legal structures differ.
That does not necessarily mean the regulation is inconsistent.
It means the economic consequences of historical conditions must be incorporated into investment analysis.
Second — The optimal ownership structure may differ from the optimal operating structure
A banking group may seek to simplify its organization through mergers and the elimination of legal entities.
Such restructuring can reduce operating costs.
But it may also change regulatory eligibility, funding requirements or the treatment of particular investments.
The economically optimal solution therefore requires comparing the operating savings from simplification with any capital or regulatory consequences.
Legal simplification is not automatically capital optimization.
Third — Regulatory-capital efficiency can influence the competitive balance
Banks with more favorable treatment for specific qualifying investments may have greater flexibility to allocate eligible capital elsewhere.
This can influence acquisitions, distributions and organic investment.
However, such advantages are valuable only to the extent that they are sustainable, usable and not offset by additional capital requirements.
The November 6 EBA ruling and UniCredit’s September authorization demonstrate different applications of this principle.
The second-order effect — The repricing of organizational architecture
The most interesting implication concerns future acquisitions.
As European banks become larger and more diversified, investors may increasingly evaluate the regulatory efficiency of different corporate structures.
That could affect the valuation of insurance participations, asset-management businesses, banking subsidiaries and holding companies.
A financial group with strong earnings but an inefficient regulatory structure may command a lower valuation than an otherwise comparable institution capable of deploying capital more flexibly.
Conversely, organizational advantages can support sustainable valuation premiums where they translate into measurable improvements in distributable earnings.
The next generation of European banking competitive advantage may depend as much on the architecture of capital as on the scale of the balance sheet.
This is the emerging dimension of the Banking Union Discount.

10. VELOCITY INSIGHT — THE NEW ECONOMICS OF CONTINENTAL SCALE
European banking is approaching a more sophisticated stage of consolidation.
The relevant investment question is no longer simply whether institutions can acquire larger banking franchises.
It is whether they can combine assets, earnings, capital and organizational structures in a way that produces superior economic returns.
Three capabilities remain fundamental.
Continental Scale expands the addressable economic opportunity.
Capital Productivity determines the return generated from that opportunity.
Institutional Flexibility determines the ability to operate effectively across Europe’s fragmented legal, regulatory and political environment.
The November 6–7 EBA developments reveal a fourth dimension.Regulatory Capital Efficiency: The ability to structure financial activities so that the institution can satisfy prudential requirements while deploying capital productively.
These capabilities are complementary.
But they are not interchangeable.
A bank can possess substantial Continental Scale without superior returns.
It can generate substantial accounting profits without equivalent distributable capital.
And it can acquire financial assets without automatically inheriting all the regulatory advantages associated with their previous ownership.
KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS AND BOARDS
1. Treat the EBA decision as a structural clarification, not an immediate capital shock. MPS has explicitly stated that its existing consolidated ratios are unaffected.
2. Evaluate acquisitions through incremental regulatory-capital requirements. Earnings accretion alone is an insufficient measure of shareholder value.
3. Distinguish historical exemptions from generally available prudential treatments. The EBA ruling concerning Mediobanca and UniCredit’s separate ECB authorization address different regulatory circumstances.
4. Monitor UniCredit’s November 9 results for the interaction between earnings and regulatory capital. The quality of capital generation matters more than a single headline ratio.
5. Recognize organizational architecture as an emerging competitive variable. The future European banking premium will depend increasingly on how efficiently institutions convert acquired earnings into deployable capital.

THE FINAL TAKE
Europe is discovering that financial integration is more complicated than financial consolidation.
Intesa is advancing toward its acquisition of MPS.
Mediobanca remains central to the economics of that transaction.
Generali continues to occupy an important position in the institutional architecture of Italian finance.
UniCredit is pursuing a continental banking platform.
Crédit Agricole retains substantial influence over Banco BPM.
BPER and Unipol are preparing for a significant restructuring of domestic banking distribution.
But this weekend’s regulatory developments expose a deeper financial reality.
Ownership rights can change hands.
Assets can be transferred.
Branches can be sold.
Corporate structures can be reorganized.
Yet certain regulatory advantages remain conditional on institutional history, legal structure and independently satisfied prudential requirements.
For investors, this raises the importance of examining the capital consequences of acquisitions rather than relying exclusively on earnings projections.
For boards of directors, it reinforces the need to distinguish financial engineering from genuine capital productivity.
For European policymakers, it exposes the continuing challenge of developing a financial architecture in which capital can be allocated efficiently while maintaining the safeguards required for financial stability.
The implications extend well beyond Italian banking.
As European financial institutions become larger and increasingly diversified, the economic value of consolidation will depend on the interaction between operating efficiency, supervisory requirements and institutional flexibility.
The next European banking champions will not be defined simply by the capital they acquire. They will be defined by the capital they can deploy—and the returns they can generate from it.
