THE GREAT BIFURCATION
AI is no longer just responding to monetary policy. It is beginning to influence monetary policy itself.
The most consequential development since our original Central Banks & AI Supercycle thesis is that the transmission mechanism is now visible in the data.
On September 16, the Fed raised the federal-funds target range by 25 bps to 3.75%–4.00%. More importantly, Chair Kevin Warsh explicitly identified surging capital expenditure and hyperscaler funding as forces increasing the competition for capital and contributing to higher bond yields. The Fed’s new projections put 2026 PCE inflation at 3.7%, core PCE at 3.4%, while simultaneously upgrading real GDP growth to 2.3%. 
That combination matters.
AI is strengthening investment and productivity while simultaneously increasing demand for capital.
This is precisely where the AI Supercycle becomes a central-bank problem.
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
What if AI is inflationary before it becomes deflationary?
The conventional AI narrative assumes:
AI → productivity → lower costs → disinflation → lower rates.
That may ultimately prove correct.
But the transition increasingly looks different:
AI → capex → debt issuance → electricity demand → infrastructure scarcity → higher wages for scarce skills → higher yields → tighter financial conditions.
Only later may we reach:
AI deployment → automation → productivity → lower unit costs → expanding supply → structural disinflation.
The distinction between those two phases is the core of The Great Bifurcation.

1 | FED — AI HAS ENTERED THE REACTION FUNCTION
Warsh’s September 16 remarks may prove more consequential than the 25-basis-point increase itself.
He argued that rising bond yields partly reflect economic strength and the surge in capital expenditure, noting that hyperscalers are raising funding and therefore increasing competition for capital. He also identified geopolitical instability as another source of higher long-term borrowing costs. 
This creates a remarkable circularity:
AI investment ↑
→ demand for capital ↑
→ corporate bond issuance ↑
→ long-term yields ↑
→ WACC ↑
→ required AI returns ↑.
The Fed is therefore confronting an unusual investment boom.
AI capex can increase productive capacity tomorrow while increasing aggregate demand and financing pressure today.
That means the same investment can initially be inflationary and ultimately deflationary.
The timing mismatch is the monetary-policy problem.
And the Fed’s projections reinforce the point. Despite stronger productivity and investment, policymakers now project a median federal-funds rate of 4.1% at end-2026 and 4.1% at end-2027, higher than the corresponding June projections. 
The AI productivity dividend is arriving.
But it has not yet earned lower rates.


⸻


2 | ECB — EUROPE FACES THE MOST DIFFICULT VERSION OF THE EQUATION
The ECB raised all three policy rates by 25 bps on September 10, taking the deposit facility to 2.50%. Its baseline now sees headline inflation averaging 3.0% in 2026, 2.5% in 2027 and 2.1% in 2028, with Middle East-driven energy pressures central to the outlook. 
Yet something structurally different is happening underneath.
Euro-area companies are expected to devote roughly 10% of total investment to AI in 2026, while ECB analysis estimates AI-related borrowing accounted for approximately one-quarter of the increase in corporate credit in Q1. More than half of euro-area workers now use AI at work. 
This produces Europe’s paradox:
Europe must invest much more in AI to raise productivity.
But:
the global AI investment boom itself is contributing to higher financing costs.
The ECB notes that large U.S. hyperscalers now account for close to one-tenth of new euro-denominated bond issuance by non-financial companies. Because European long-term yields are linked to U.S. yields, Europe can therefore import part of the financing cost of America’s AI boom—even if Europe captures less of its productivity upside. 
That is extraordinarily important.Europe risks paying the interest-rate cost of the AI Supercycle without capturing enough of the productivity dividend.
This is why Industrial AI is no longer merely a technology policy.
It is becoming a macroeconomic imperative.


⸻


3 | BANK OF JAPAN — THE LAST LOW-RATE ANCHOR IS MOVING
The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to 1.25% on September 18, the highest level in 31 years, and signalled greater willingness to act pre-emptively against inflation. 
Yet the yen weakened rather than strengthened. Today USD/JPY is around 157.17, and markets are again watching for possible Japanese intervention. 
That matters globally.
For decades Japan represented one of the world’s deepest reservoirs of inexpensive capital.
If Japanese rates normalize while U.S. and European yields remain elevated, another source of global financial repression disappears.
The equation becomes:
**Fed tightening
ECB tightening
BOJ normalization
= higher global hurdle rate.**
And that arrives precisely as AI requires perhaps the largest coordinated private infrastructure build-out in decades.

5 | THE CENTRAL BANK PARADOX
Central banks face two versions of AI simultaneously.
AI TODAY — POTENTIALLY INFLATIONARY
Capex ↑

construction ↑
scarce skilled labor ↑
credit demand ↑

AI TOMORROW — POTENTIALLY DEFLATIONARY
automation ↑

The policy mistake could therefore occur in either direction.
Cut too early, and central banks could amplify the infrastructure boom and inflation.
Remain restrictive too long, and they could suppress precisely the capital formation needed to deliver the future productivity shock.
Central banks are being forced to price tomorrow’s productivity with today’s inflation.
That is the core dilemma.

INVESTOR PERSPECTIVE
The implication is sharper than simply “higher rates hurt technology.”
The new regime should separate AI assets according to time-to-cash-flow and capital productivity.
The strongest exposures are those that monetize bottlenecks immediately: accelerated computing, memory, photonics, networking, power equipment, grids, nuclear, cooling and industrial automation.
The more vulnerable exposures combine high capital intensity + repeated external financing + distant cash flows + uncertain utilization.
The relevant investment metric is therefore moving from:
AI Revenue Growth
to:
AI Return Velocity = Incremental Cash Flow / Incremental Capital / Time
That could become one of the central metrics of The Velocity Edge.
VELOCITY INSIGHT
The first phase of the AI Supercycle was about the scarcity of intelligence.
The second is increasingly about the scarcity of capital required to produce intelligence.
The third will be decided by whether intelligence itself can eliminate scarcity through productivity.
The central-bank question of the AI age is therefore no longer whether AI will lower inflation. It is whether the world can finance the road to AI deflation without first creating an AI inflation problem.
