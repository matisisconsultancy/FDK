The U.S. 10-year Treasury has touched 5%. Brent is back near $107. At the same time, the five largest U.S. AI hyperscalers are still expected to spend about $795 billion this year and more than $1 trillion in 2027. Europe’s answer is becoming physical: the EIB has made its first investment in small modular nuclear reactors, while Christine Lagarde warns that Europe’s data-centre deficit could widen sixfold within a decade. The AI Supercycle has reached a new phase: capital will no longer reward acceleration alone. It will reward acceleration that can be UNDERWRITTEN.

The Fundamental Question
When money approaches 5%, oil trades above $100 and AI itself acquires new safety constraints, which forms of Velocity still generate returns high enough to deserve capital?
The Next Economy enters today with three simultaneous repricings.
The price of capital has risen. The U.S. 10-year Treasury briefly crossed 5% on Monday for the first time since 2023; Germany’s 10-year yield moved above 3.51%, its highest since 2009; Japan’s 10-year yield has reached 3% ahead of a widely expected Bank of Japan increase on Friday. 
The price of physical scarcity has risen. Brent is around $107, while Saudi Arabia’s East–West pipeline remains offline. The pipeline has recently been rerouting roughly 4 million barrels per day—about 4% of global supply— around the badly constrained Strait of Hormuz. 
And the price of technological uncertainty has risen. Calls from leading AI executives to slow frontier development triggered a 5.9% fall in the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index on Monday, with Nvidia down 3.4%, Micron more than 5%, and Broadcom and AMD more than 4%. 
Yet the capital cycle has not stopped.
BofA Global Research estimates Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle will spend roughly $795 billion on capex in 2026 and $1.08 trillion in 2027. 
Europe is beginning to finance the power architecture around that build-out.
And AI is simultaneously moving deeper into banking, industrial systems and physical mobility.
This morning therefore marks a crucial transition:
**FROM CAPITAL FORMATION
TO CAPITAL DISCIPLINE.**

Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy
1.Five-percent money is becoming the first real underwriting test of the AI Supercycle
Verified facts. The U.S. 10-year yield touched 5% Monday and is trading around 4.99%. Markets assign around a 90–93% probability to a Fed increase on Wednesday. In a Reuters poll, 86 of 101 economists expect a quarter-point increase to 3.75–4.00%, and a majority of respondents who gave a March forecast expect at least one additional increase by then. 
FDK interpretation.
The first two years of the AI Supercycle asked:
Can capital be raised?
The next phase asks:
CAN CAPITAL EARN MORE THAN IT COSTS?
That is a different problem.
At a 5% risk-free benchmark, an AI factory cannot be justified simply by:
capacity,
market share,
strategic relevance
or long-term optionality.
It increasingly needs:
utilisation,
pricing power,
cash conversion
and productivity evidence.
The new denominator is unforgiving.
Every project now competes simultaneously with:
sovereign bonds,
energy assets,
infrastructure yields
and alternative corporate uses of capital.
The AI Supercycle remains powerful.
But financial gravity has returned.


⸻


2. The AI capex boom has not broken—but its margin for error has
Verified facts. BofA estimates hyperscaler capex at about $795 billion this year and more than $1 trillion next year. Despite Monday’s semiconductor selloff, there is not yet evidence of cancelled data-centre construction or material order withdrawals. Reuters quotes investors explicitly waiting for such evidence before declaring that the investment cycle has changed. 
FDK interpretation.
This distinction is crucial.
The market is currently repricing the probability of uninterrupted acceleration.
It is not yet repricing a collapse in actual infrastructure demand.
That creates a very different investment framework.
Yesterday’s semiconductor valuation implicitly assumed:
AI capability growth
→ endless capex acceleration.
Today capital begins asking:
Which infrastructure remains valuable even if frontier progress pauses?
The strongest answer may be infrastructure serving:
inference, enterprise deployment, sovereign AI, physical AI and regulated workloads.
That makes the Great Split inside AI more granular.
CAPEX SUPPORTED BY DURABLE UTILISATION
versus
CAPEX SUPPORTED BY PERPETUAL FRONTIER ACCELERATION.


⸻


3. Europe is finally treating AI sovereignty as an infrastructure financing problem
Verified facts. ECB President Christine Lagarde said Monday that Europe risks becoming dangerously dependent on foreign AI technology. Europe already lacks sufficient data-centre capacity, she argued, and the shortfall could become more than six times larger within ten years. Faster AI adoption could raise European productivity by as much as 4% over a decade. 
This morning, the European Investment Bank announced its first SMR investment, a €40 million loan to Finnish startup Steady Energy for a 50-MW underground reactor design. The EIB says this is the first of a pipeline of SMR investments. 
FDK interpretation.
These two developments belong together.
Europe’s AI challenge is often described as a model problem.
It is actually a capital architecture problem.
Europe needs to connect:
energy
→ accelerated computing
→ data centres
→ models
→ industrial applications
→ capital markets.
The EIB entering SMRs is therefore much more consequential than the €40 million headline.
Institutional capital has crossed a boundary.
Nuclear is becoming part of Europe’s AI financing stack.
That creates a new European strategic chain:
**NUCLEAR
→ FIRM POWER
→ DATA-CENTRE BANKABILITY
→ AI CAPACITY
→ INDUSTRIAL AI
→ PRODUCTIVITY**
That may be the most credible route toward European technological autonomy.

4. China’s Great Split is now visible in the macro data
Verified facts. China’s August industrial production rose 5.2%, beating expectations and accelerating from July. But retail sales increased just 0.4%, while fixed-asset investment declined 7.2% in the first eight months of the year. Property investment fell 19.9%. High-tech-sector investment, however, rose 5.2%. 
FDK interpretation.This is a nearly perfect macroeconomic expression of the Great Split.
On one side:
manufacturing
high tech
exports
AI
robotics
strategic industries.
On the other:
property
household demand
traditional investment
domestic consumption.
China is not simply slowing.
It is reallocating economic velocity.
The most consequential variable therefore becomes not aggregate GDP alone, but the composition of capital formation.
China’s economic architecture is increasingly:
**STRONG PRODUCTION CAPACITY
WEAK DOMESTIC DEMAND.**
That can sustain export strength.
But it also increases pressure on global markets as Chinese industrial capacity seeks demand abroad.
For Europe, that is both an inflation benefit and a competitive threat.

5. AI is moving from models into infrastructure and physical mobility simultaneously
Verified facts. SoftBank-backed data-centre developer SB Energy is preparing a U.S. listing and will place up to $500 million of shares with Japanese investors. Nvidia has committed $1.5 billion at the IPO price; OpenAI has received warrants worth roughly $5.5 billion. Proceeds will help finance data centres, power generation and associated infrastructure. 
Waymo, GO and Nihon Kotsu also announced today that they aim to launch Japan’s first fully autonomous commercial taxi service in Tokyo in 2027, initially scaling toward about 100 vehicles subject to regulatory approval. 
FDK interpretation.
These developments reveal where the Supercycle is moving.
From:
model training
toward:
power generation
data centres
inference
mobility
physical deployment.
That is exactly the transition from digital AI toward Industrial AI and Intelligent Motion.
The critical investment question therefore shifts from:
Who owns the best model?
toward:
WHO CAN TURN INTELLIGENCE INTO PHYSICAL ECONOMIC OUTPUT?

Hidden Patterns Beyond the Headlines
1. Guardrails may reduce AI volatility rather than reduce AI value
Markets initially interpreted calls for slower frontier development as negative for infrastructure demand.
But there is a second-order interpretation.
If governance creates more predictable:
release cycles,
model behaviour,
enterprise liability
and regulatory treatment,
then capital may become easier to underwrite, not harder.
As one investor told Reuters, a credible safety framework could make long-duration AI capex more financeable. 
The real enemy of capital is not slower acceleration.
It is unquantifiable risk.


⸻


2. Trust is becoming an enterprise-AI control point
Palantir, Nvidia and Booz Allen are reportedly restricting use of frontier models over concerns about proprietary information and data retention. Palantir has demanded irrevocable zero-data-retention guarantees before making some Anthropic models available; Nvidia is limiting certain uses to less-sensitive work and relying on its own models internally. 
This reveals the next enterprise bottleneck.
Not inference cost.
Not GPU availability.
DATA SOVEREIGNTY.
Enterprise AI adoption increasingly depends on:
who sees the data,
where it resides,
who retains it,
and whether the model provider can become a competitor.
Secure AI architecture may therefore become a premium product category.

3. European Banking Union is becoming industrial policy
Germany has now shifted from blocking UniCredit’s approach to Commerzbank toward negotiating conditions.
Berlin wants Commerzbank to:
remain listed,
remain headquartered in Frankfurt,
and continue financing German Mittelstand companies.
A combined UniCredit–Commerzbank group would have more than €1.3 trillion of assets. 
That shift matters.
Europe increasingly accepts that continental scale may be necessary.
The negotiation is becoming about how scale is governed, not whether scale should exist.
And a continent trying to finance:
AI gigafactories,
SMRs,
grids,
defence,
rail
and industrial transformation
cannot separate its banking architecture from its industrial architecture.

Likely Market and Competitive Implications Today
The immediate market backdrop is fragile rather than disorderly.
Asian equities are mixed, with MSCI Asia-Pacific ex-Japan down modestly and the Nikkei slightly positive after reversing early losses. Oil remains around $107, and bond yields remain near critical thresholds. 
The most likely capital preference is therefore toward productive scarcity plus visible cash flow.
Potential relative beneficiaries include:
nuclear and firm power, grids, secure data centres, cybersecurity, AI governance, enterprise AI, energy infrastructure, selected banks and physical-AI platforms.
Semiconductors remain structurally central—but near-term valuation sensitivity has increased dramatically.
The pressure points are increasingly:
long-duration technology, speculative AI suppliers, transport exposed to fuel inflation, highly leveraged industrial assets, and companies unable to translate AI spending into measurable cash returns.
China remains a special case.
Its high-tech economy is accelerating while domestic demand deteriorates. That keeps Chinese industrial exporters highly competitive—and increases pressure on European companies operating without equivalent capital intensity or state-backed scale.
And Europe has acquired an important new opportunity.
The combination of:
EIB capital, nuclear technology, Nordic power, continental banking scale and growing political awareness of AI dependency
could finally become the beginnings of a coherent European Next Economy architecture.
