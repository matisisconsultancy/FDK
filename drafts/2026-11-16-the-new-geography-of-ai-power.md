Washington consolidates the infrastructure of intelligence. Beijing accelerates the economics of intelligence.
Material thesis reinforcement
THE BIG TAKE
Two developments on November 15–16 strengthen our central thesis: the United States is organizing its technological advantage into an allied infrastructure system, while China is pushing aggressively to lower the economic cost of advanced AI.
The developments are complementary rather than contradictory.
1. THE AMERICAN INFLECTION — AI LEADERSHIP BECOMES AN ALLIANCE STRATEGY
On November 15, the United States and nine partners signed the San Francisco Declaration under the Pax Silica initiative. Signatories include Japan, South Korea, India, Singapore, Australia, the United Kingdom, Israel, Qatar and the UAE.
The agreement creates a framework for coordinating protection of advanced technologies, semiconductor supply chains, critical minerals and AI infrastructure. Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and the European Commission are expected to join subsequently. Reuters +1
Why it matters: Washington is moving beyond protecting individual semiconductor technologies toward organizing the entire industrial system required for AI leadership.
The declaration is an actual diplomatic commitment, although its investment projects and coordinated export-control measures remain to be implemented.

Why it matters: China is increasingly competing on the economics of useful AI output, not simply on model capability.
The critical metric is shifting from the cost of computing capacity toward the cost of completing valuable commercial and industrial tasks.
This is consequential for the AI Supercycle because lower unit costs can accelerate adoption even as they intensify pressure on model-provider pricing.

A further development reinforces the capital-allocation challenge. Reuters reported on November 16 that Nvidia’s $500 billion AI infrastructure financing initiative faces uncertainty following Jensen Huang’s departure, potentially shifting future financing toward established hyperscalers rather than riskier specialized cloud operators. No suspension has been announced. Reuters
This creates a potentially significant second-order dynamic: capital could become more concentrated among financially resilient infrastructure operators precisely as lower-cost Chinese models expand the range of AI applications.
4. WHAT TO WATCH NEXT
The November 19 Trump–Xi summit in Shenzhen may clarify the direction of advanced-chip export restrictions and bilateral AI cooperation. Discussions about an AI incident-communication mechanism are underway, but no operational agreement has been confirmed. ReutersThree decisive validation points remain: binding implementation of allied semiconductor controls, independent verification of Qwen4-Max’s cost-per-task improvements, and evidence that cheaper models are translating into sustainable industrial productivity and commercial returns.
THE FINAL TAKE
The latest developments strengthen rather than overturn the governing thesis.
America’s advantage rests increasingly on control of frontier computing capabilities, trusted technology networks and infrastructure investment.
China’s challenge lies in reducing the cost of deploying advanced intelligence and expanding its commercial reach.
For investors, the next great capital rotation may not be determined by who builds the most powerful intelligence, but by who controls its infrastructure—and who captures the economic value created when intelligence becomes dramatically cheaper.
