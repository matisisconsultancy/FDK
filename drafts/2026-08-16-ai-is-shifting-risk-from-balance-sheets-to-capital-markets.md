The Fundamental Question
Can the AI infrastructure cycle preserve equity returns as financing risk migrates from hyperscaler balance sheets into guarantees, infrastructure equity, IPOs, private credit and long-duration public markets—while the real cost of capital is already close to two-decade highs?
The most important AI development this weekend is not another processor.
It is a change in who carries the financial risk of the processor economy.
Nvidia is reportedly discussing an investment of up to $3 billion in SB Energy, the SoftBank subsidiary developing OpenAI’s enormous Ohio data-centre campus. Roughly half could be invested when the project agreement is signed and half in a planned SB Energy IPO that could raise at least $5 billion. The discussions sit alongside approximately $100 billion of credit support being considered for the project.
But this follows Friday’s report that Nvidia expects to provide an initial guarantee of less than $120 billion, compared with as much as $250 billion previously discussed, after investors raised concerns about the chipmaker’s exposure. The proposed Ohio campus is a 10-gigawatt project.
This is not retreat.
It is risk redistribution.
The financing structure is moving from:
one corporate balance sheet
toward
vendor support + infrastructure equity + IPO capital + institutional credit + project finance + long-duration leases.
That transition matters because it tells us where the AI Supercycle is heading.
The next phase of AI will be financed less like software and increasingly like electricity, telecom networks, airports and other infrastructure.
And once that happens, capital structure becomes part of competitive advantage.

Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy
AI Financing Is Moving From Concentrated Guarantees to Distributed Risk
Verified facts
Nvidia has reportedly reduced the initial guarantee contemplated for OpenAI’s Ohio campus to less than $120 billion from as much as $250 billion, while separately considering a $3 billion investment in SB Energy. SB Energy could seek at least $5 billion through an IPO, and the parties are discussing around $100 billion of credit support. Nvidia has also created financing platforms with six major institutional investors targeting more than $500 billion of third-party AI infrastructure capital.
FDK interpretation
This is one of the most consequential developments of the AI Supercycle.
The capital requirement has become too large to remain concentrated on technology-company balance sheets.
Risk is being unbundled.
The emerging structure resembles:
equity sponsor → project company → vendor support → customer lease → private credit → infrastructure debt → public equity → institutional refinancing.
The significance is profound.
AI infrastructure is becoming a capital market, not merely a capex programme.
That broadens the available capital pool.
But it also spreads AI risk through the financial system.
The investment question therefore shifts from:
Does AI demand exist?
to:
Who owns the residual risk if utilization, pricing or technological value falls short?
That question will determine returns across private equity, credit, utilities, data-centre operators and semiconductor vendors.

AI Valuation Is Moving Further Into the Future
Verified facts
Anthropic is preparing investors for a potential IPO using a projected $190–200 billion of 2028 revenue, compared with a revenue run rate above $47 billion reported in May. Bankers are reportedly considering revenue multiples based on forward forecasts rather than current earnings. Anthropic has projected at least $10.9 billion of Q2 revenue and its first quarterly operating profit.
FDK interpretation
This is a textbook duration trade.
Investors are being asked to capitalize cash flows that do not yet exist at scale.
That is not intrinsically irrational.
High-growth companies have always been valued partly on future revenues.
What is unusual is the combination of:
extraordinary future growth assumptions
with
extraordinary present capital intensity
and
historically expensive long-duration money.
The valuation chain becomes:
2028 revenue forecast → assumed future margin → assumed infrastructure efficiency → assumed capital requirement → discount rate → present equity value.
Every assumption compounds the previous one.
The further valuation moves into the future, the more powerful the long bond becomes.
This creates an uncomfortable contrast.
AI revenue expectations are accelerating.
Real yields are also accelerating.
One increases terminal value.
The other discounts it.
The Great Split is now inside the valuation formula itself.

AI Capital Formation Is Starting to Raise Its Own Hurdle Rate
Verified facts
Alphabet, Amazon and Meta have issued almost $220 billion of bonds in 2026, already more than double their total issuance during 2025. U.S. 30-year real yields are near 3%, while German and UK real yields are also near decade highs. Reuters reports that investors increasingly identify heavy AI borrowing alongside government issuance as a driver of competition for long-duration capital.
FDK interpretation
Until recently, we treated the cost of capital as an external variable affecting AI.
That framework is becoming incomplete.
AI itself is now large enough to affect demand for capital.
The feedback loop becomes:AI investment → bond issuance → competition for savings → higher real yields → higher WACC → higher required AI ROIC.
This is the first endogenous financial constraint of the AI Supercycle.
The more successful the capital-formation phase becomes, the higher the return threshold imposed on the next dollar.
That means capex can eventually become self-limiting even if technological demand remains extremely strong.
AI may not encounter capital scarcity because investors stop believing in AI. It may encounter capital scarcity because AI requires too much capital simultaneously with governments.
That distinction matters enormously.

Capital Is Moving Toward Europe While Industrial Capital Becomes More Geopolitically Selective
Verified facts
European equity funds attracted $13.52 billion in the latest week, their strongest inflow since early July and far above U.S. inflows of $2.58 billion. Meanwhile German companies invested only €4.3 billion directly in the United States during the first half of 2026, nearly two-thirds less than a year earlier and almost 80% below the comparable 2024 level.
The STOXX 600 nevertheless remains within roughly 1% of its record high, with Q2 earnings expected to increase 23.4%, the strongest growth in almost four years.
FDK interpretation
This is a fascinating reversal.
Financial capital is moving into Europe.
European industrial capital is becoming more cautious about moving into America.
That is The Great Split applied to cross-border capital.
The implication goes beyond tariffs.
Capital allocation increasingly prices:
policy durability;
trade access;
energy security;
currency risk;
regulatory stability;
and strategic autonomy.
Europe therefore has an unusual opening.
It can attract global financial capital while retaining more of its industrial capital at home.
But only if it converts that opportunity into:
accelerated computing;
energy infrastructure;
advanced manufacturing;
defence;
mobility;
and Industrial AI.
Europe’s competitive question is no longer simply whether it can mobilize money.
It is whether it can turn the current capital-flow advantage into a capital-formation advantage.

Energy Security Is Becoming Explicit Sovereign Industrial Policy
Verified facts
India today ordered domestic refiners to target a maximum 63,810 tonnes of LPG production per day, while maintaining the storage and transport infrastructure needed to build supply buffers. Before the Iran war disrupted the Strait of Hormuz, India sourced roughly 90% of its LPG imports from the Middle East.
The United States is simultaneously preparing additional economic pressure on Iran, potentially targeting oil shippers, Chinese refiners, financial intermediaries and other entities involved in Iranian commerce. China purchased more than 80% of Iran’s shipped oil according to 2025 Kpler data cited by Reuters.
Brent settled Friday at $88.52, up 6% for the week, while traffic through Hormuz remained severely constrained.
FDK interpretation
Energy security is no longer being managed as procurement.
It is being managed as national resilience architecture.
India’s response is revealing:
**domestic production targets
inventories
transport capacity
diversified sourcing.**
That logic will increasingly spread.
And it has direct implications for AI.
Every country trying to build sovereign accelerated-computing capacity must ultimately answer the same question:
Can intelligence continue operating when energy supply is politically disrupted?
The sovereign AI stack therefore becomes:
chips + data + grids + generation + storage + logistics + capital.
Without the energy layer, technological sovereignty is incomplete.

Hidden Patterns Beyond the Headlines
The AI Value Chain Is Becoming a Risk-Transfer Chain
The old chain was technological:
model → processor → data centre → application.
The emerging chain is financial:
manufacturer → vendor finance → developer → infrastructure fund → bank → bond market → sovereign investor.
Every link transfers part of the project risk.
The most important analytical question will increasingly be:where does the residual value sit—and where does the residual risk end up?
If accelerated-computing hardware increasingly becomes collateral for loans, investors must price the future resale and utilization value of a rapidly depreciating technological asset.
That is fundamentally different from lending against railways, airports or pipelines.
⸻
The Great Split Is Now Earnings Versus Consumption
S&P 500 earnings grew approximately 52% in Q2, while July U.S. retail sales fell 0.6%, consumer sentiment declined to 51.0 and July payrolls had already contracted.
Those numbers can coexist.
But they cannot diverge indefinitely.
AI raises productivity and margins.
The consumer ultimately supplies final demand.
The system therefore depends on propagation:
productivity → profits → investment → wages/prices/new activity → consumption.
If the propagation mechanism weakens, the corporate economy can outperform the household economy for a considerable period—but eventually revenue growth meets the demand constraint.
Profit acceleration without income propagation is powerful financially, but unstable economically.
⸻
The Long Bond Is Becoming the Real AI Benchmark
The Federal Reserve can pause in September.
That does not make AI capital inexpensive.
Thirty-year real U.S. yields remain around 3%, while the Treasury’s latest long-bond auction cleared at 5.22%.
The relevant financial equation is therefore no longer simply:
Fed funds → technology multiple.
It is:
real long yield + credit spread + project risk + technological depreciation = AI hurdle rate.
That formula will increasingly separate profitable AI infrastructure from capital destruction.
⸻
Central Banks Are Building Their Own Barbell
Central banks bought a net 289 tonnes of gold in Q2, five times Q1 volumes and a record for a second quarter. The purchases were worth an estimated $45 billion, while China added another 20 tonnes in July. A World Gold Council survey found 45% of central banks expect to increase gold holdings over the next twelve months.
This is the sovereign counterpart to the AI trade.
Private markets are buying future productivity.
Central banks are buying monetary resilience.
The common denominator is scarcity.
One portfolio buys intelligence. The other buys optionality against instability.
The Next Economy increasingly requires both.

Europe’s Physical Economy Is Acquiring a New Risk Premium
Europe’s fifth heatwave of the year is also revealing a widening insurance gap. Moody’s estimates cited by Reuters put last summer’s heat-related economic losses at about €43 billion, versus only around €500 million of insured payouts. In a Padua survey, more than 80% of hospitality operators reported revenue declines of roughly 20% during recent heat.
This matters beyond climate policy.
Heat affects:
worker productivity;
electricity demand;
power generation;
water availability;
transport;
insurance costs;
and therefore industrial ROIC.
Europe’s competitiveness challenge now includes the rising cost of making physical infrastructure resilient.
That is particularly important for Industrial AI, because AI’s supposedly digital economy is becoming extraordinarily dependent on physical reliability.
