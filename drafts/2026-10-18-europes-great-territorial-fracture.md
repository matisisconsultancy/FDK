The New Geography of Capital, Competitiveness and Political Risk
A Strategic Agenda for Investors, EU Policymakers and National Governments
The central thesis
Europe is entering a new phase of economic divergence. The defining divide may no longer be between Northern and Southern Europe, or between fiscally disciplined and highly indebted countries. It may increasingly run between territories capable of attracting productive capital, deploying artificial intelligence and sustaining industrial competitiveness—and territories progressively excluded from these processes.
The research highlighted by Andrés Rodríguez-Pose introduces a critical challenge to European industrial policy: concentrating investment in established centers of excellence may raise aggregate productivity while deepening territorial inequality.
This is particularly consequential when three forces are converging:
The AI Supercycle: concentrating initial infrastructure investment in locations with sufficient energy, computing capacity, skills and financing.
The Sovereign Risk Cycle: raising the cost of productive capital, particularly for bank-dependent enterprises and financially vulnerable territories.
The Territorial Divergence Cycle: reinforcing differences in productivity, employment, fiscal capacity and political stability.
These forces could interact to produce a self-reinforcing process of industrial concentration and political fragmentation.
Yet the outcome is not predetermined. AI can also lower barriers to industrial modernization, while integrated capital markets and infrastructure networks can extend productivity gains beyond existing technology hubs.
The strategic question is no longer simply how Europe can invest more. It is how Europe can make capital more productive, more geographically scalable and more resilient to political and financial shocks.
This requires three distinct but coordinated agendas.
I. INVESTORS — Repricing Europe’s Economic Geography
From sovereign risk to territorial capital productivity
The investment thesis
Traditional European asset allocation relies heavily on country exposure, sector composition, sovereign spreads and corporate fundamentals.
These variables remain essential, but they may increasingly fail to capture the geographical distribution of productive capacity.
A company headquartered in a financially stable country can face deteriorating competitive conditions if its production assets depend on congested electricity networks, declining industrial districts or increasingly scarce technical labor.
Conversely, companies operating in countries with higher sovereign debt may remain attractive if they occupy technologically advanced industrial ecosystems with strong cash generation and internationally diversified markets.
The implication is a potential shift from national risk classification toward regional productive-capacity analysis.
Five investment decisions
1. Introduce territorial exposure into investment underwriting.
Investors should evaluate regional productivity trends, electricity costs, grid availability, labor availability, industrial specialization and financing conditions alongside traditional corporate metrics.
A sovereign spread is not a sufficient proxy for the investment risk of an industrial region.
2. Distinguish AI infrastructure spending from productive AI deployment.
The strongest long-term opportunities may not necessarily be found in companies announcing the largest AI capital expenditure.
More durable returns may arise among enterprises demonstrating measurable productivity improvements, higher asset utilization, operating-margin expansion and free-cash-flow conversion.
The investment test is whether AI produces incremental returns on invested capital—not simply greater computing capacity.
3. Identify the beneficiaries of industrial diffusion.Potential beneficiaries include industrial automation, energy storage, electricity grids, cybersecurity, engineering software and firms enabling SMEs to deploy AI at scale.
Their attractiveness depends on valuations, competitive positioning and the ability to capture economic value, not simply exposure to favorable themes.
4. Reassess European banks through regional credit exposure.
National banking indicators may conceal concentrations in economically vulnerable territories.
Investors should monitor regional SME lending, sectoral credit concentration, refinancing needs and collateral values.
A deterioration in local industrial activity could affect loan quality before it becomes visible in aggregate national indicators.
5. Prepare for a new market rotation.
The next European rotation may favor enterprises that combine financial resilience with measurable capital productivity.
A forward-looking investment framework
Potential relative beneficiaries Grid modernization, industrial AI adoption, automation, energy efficiency, resilient infrastructure
Potential vulnerabilities Highly leveraged manufacturers, energy-intensive firms without pricing power, capital-intensive AI projects with uncertain utilization
Key monitoring indicators ROIC, free cash flow, regional industrial orders, SME credit spreads, electricity availability and AI-driven productivity
Critical market signal Simultaneous deterioration in regional investment, bank credit and sovereign financing conditions
What investors should do in the next 90 days
Commission a regional exposure review of European equity, private-credit, infrastructure and banking portfolios. Identify companies whose profitability depends on structurally vulnerable industrial territories.
Introduce a Territorial Capital Productivity Score combining financial resilience, energy exposure, AI adoption, labor availability and regional industrial momentum.
Prioritize investment opportunities with identifiable productivity improvements and contractual or cash-flow evidence.
The investor’s decisive question: Where will the next euro of capital generate the highest sustainable productivity-adjusted return?
II. EU POLICYMAKERS IN BRUSSELS — Redesigning Europe’s Capital Formation Architecture
From redistribution and sectoral subsidies to productive European integration
The policy thesis
The central challenge for Brussels is not simply to increase the volume of European investment.
It is to design an institutional architecture that reconciles three objectives:
Global technological and industrial competitiveness.
Economically productive territorial convergence.
Fiscal sustainability and political legitimacy.
The evidence from regional economic research suggests that these objectives do not automatically reinforce one another.
Mission-oriented investment may generate strong aggregate productivity returns while concentrating economic benefits geographically. Place-based investment can improve employment and territorial convergence, but its effectiveness depends on local capabilities, governance and project selection.
The European Union must therefore move beyond the false choice between industrial excellence and cohesion.
Europe needs a capital allocation framework that finances excellence while enabling the diffusion of its productivity benefits.
Five decisions for Brussels
1. Integrate competitiveness and cohesion into a single investment architecture.
The next Multiannual Financial Framework should explicitly assess both aggregate productivity returns and territorial effects.
Funding decisions should be evaluated against a common framework incorporating productivity, employment, private capital mobilization, industrial resilience and measurable spillovers.
This does not mean allocating equal resources to every region. It means identifying where complementary investments can unlock the greatest economic value.
2. Accelerate the Savings and Investments Union.
Fragmented European capital markets remain a structural obstacle to financing innovation and industrial transformation.Brussels should prioritize cross-border investment vehicles, deeper risk-capital markets, simplified financing rules and stronger channels for mobilizing institutional savings.
The objective is to reduce the dependence of productive European SMEs on geographically concentrated banking systems.
3. Build a European AI Diffusion Infrastructure.
Europe should not evaluate its AI strategy primarily through the number or scale of AI gigafactories.
A more meaningful objective is the diffusion of productive AI throughout European industry.
This requires shared computing access, interoperable industrial data frameworks, advanced technical training and financing for enterprise transformation.
Large computing investments should be assessed partly on their capacity to generate measurable downstream industrial benefits.
4. Make energy integration a central instrument of industrial policy.
Electricity availability and reliability are increasingly decisive determinants of industrial location.
Brussels should accelerate cross-border interconnections, grid modernization, storage investment and permitting coordination.
Without more integrated electricity infrastructure, the benefits of European AI and industrial investment may remain concentrated in a limited number of territories.
5. Create a European Territorial Competitiveness Observatory.
The Commission should develop a forward-looking system measuring regional productivity, private investment, AI adoption, industrial restructuring, energy constraints and access to capital.
The objective would be to identify emerging vulnerabilities before they become entrenched economic or political problems.
A proposed Territorial Velocity Index could complement existing European regional competitiveness indicators, provided its methodology is independently validated.
The Brussels implementation agenda

The institutional consequence
Brussels should increasingly act as the architect of interconnected European industrial ecosystems rather than solely as a distributor of funding.
Its comparative advantage lies in addressing cross-border coordination failures that national governments cannot efficiently resolve independently.
The Brussels question: Can Europe transform fragmented national savings, energy systems and industrial capabilities into a single productive investment ecosystem?
III. NATIONAL GOVERNMENTS — From Fiscal Management to Industrial Transformation
The national imperative: turn regional capabilities into productivity growth
The government thesis
National governments face a different challenge from Brussels.
The EU can improve financing architecture and cross-border coordination. But national authorities remain central to education, industrial implementation, permitting, infrastructure delivery and many dimensions of territorial development.
The critical mistake would be to respond to industrial divergence exclusively through compensatory fiscal transfers.
Transfers can protect households and maintain essential services. They cannot, by themselves, create sustainable productivity growth.
Governments must distinguish between income stabilization and productive transformation.
Six national strategic priorities
1. Identify the country’s productive territories and structural bottlenecks.
Develop regional industrial maps covering productivity, specialization, energy, workforce skills, investment and infrastructure constraints.Distinguish regions with credible growth potential from those requiring more fundamental economic restructuring.
2. Prioritize AI deployment in existing industrial systems.
National AI strategies should move beyond technology promotion toward measurable enterprise transformation.
SMEs need access to financing, technical expertise, interoperable data and organizational capabilities.
3. Redirect industrial incentives toward productivity outcomes.
Public support should be linked to verifiable improvements in investment, productivity, employment quality and private capital mobilization.
Avoid indefinite subsidies to activities without credible paths to competitiveness.
4. Reduce the cost and uncertainty of industrial investment.
Accelerate permitting, grid connections, infrastructure execution and workforce development.
For many enterprises, implementation delays and unreliable infrastructure can be as damaging as financing costs.
5. Protect fiscal credibility while preserving productive investment.
Governments facing higher sovereign-risk premiums should differentiate between recurring expenditure and investments with credible long-term economic returns.
Fiscal credibility remains indispensable, but indiscriminate reductions in productive investment can worsen future debt sustainability.
6. Establish national regional-productivity delivery units.
Each government should identify a limited number of priority industrial transformation programs, assign accountable institutions and publish measurable results.
The objective is execution rather than the proliferation of strategies.
IV. Six national priorities: Where governments must act
France — Restore fiscal credibility without sacrificing industrial renewal
Protect productive investment from political and fiscal volatility. Prioritize energy affordability, industrial restructuring and investment certainty in vulnerable manufacturing regions.
Key test: Can France stabilize financing conditions while maintaining industrial capital formation?
Italy — Modernize the industrial districts
Create scalable financing and technical support for AI adoption among SMEs. Link regional industrial specialization to automation, energy efficiency and international supply chains.
Key test: Can Italy convert industrial specialization into measurable productivity acceleration?
Spain — Convert energy and digital investment into industrial productivity
Use renewable-energy potential and digital investment to attract advanced manufacturing, while ensuring the benefits extend beyond established metropolitan and technology hubs.
Key test: Can Spain transform infrastructure investment into a geographically broader industrial expansion?
Germany — Rebuild the productivity advantage of its industrial base
Accelerate industrial automation, energy-system modernization and supplier transformation. Address the vulnerabilities of traditional manufacturing regions.
Key test: Can German industry regain competitiveness without prolonged supplier-network contraction?
Netherlands — Remove infrastructure constraints on technological leadership
Expand electricity-grid capacity, improve industrial site availability and strengthen the diffusion of advanced technology beyond established innovation clusters.
Key test: Can infrastructure investment prevent bottlenecks from limiting high-value industrial growth?
Belgium — Overcome institutional fragmentation
Improve coordination across regional governments on industrial investment, energy infrastructure and workforce transformation while preserving fiscal discipline.
Key test: Can Belgium turn its logistics and industrial assets into a coherent productivity strategy?
V. The three-level European operating model
The three audiences must operate through a coordinated system, rather than through independent initiatives.
BRUSSELS — ENABLE
Integrated capital markets, energy interconnections, industrial standards and cross-border investment
NATIONAL GOVERNMENTS — EXECUTE
Industrial transformation, regional specialization, skills, permitting and project deliveryINVESTORS — ALLOCATE AND DISCIPLINE
Productive capital, risk assessment, measurable returns and investment selection
THE PRODUCTIVITY FEEDBACK LOOP
Private investment → industrial productivity → employment and fiscal capacity → stronger investment conditions
The framework is conditional. It works only when incentives are aligned, projects are economically viable and the three levels share comparable performance indicators.

THE BIG TAKE — EUROPE MUST FINANCE ITS FUTURE, NOT ITS FRAGMENTATION
Europe’s next crisis may not begin with the collapse of a sovereign bond market or the failure of a major financial institution.
It may develop more gradually, through the cumulative consequences of investment that never reaches productive enterprises, technologies that fail to diffuse and industrial regions that lose their capacity to compete.
This would be a different form of European fragmentation: less visible in monetary institutions, but potentially more damaging to long-term prosperity.
The strategic response must therefore differ for each decision-maker.
For investors: stop treating Europe as a collection of national sovereign risks. Identify the territories and enterprises capable of converting energy, computing, skills and capital into sustainable economic returns.
For Brussels: stop evaluating industrial competitiveness and territorial cohesion as separate policy objectives. Build an integrated architecture that combines technological excellence with economically productive diffusion.
For national governments: stop measuring industrial-policy success primarily through public spending commitments. Measure productivity, private investment, execution quality and the ability of regional enterprises to compete internationally.
The decisive distinction is not between countries that spend more and countries that spend less.
It is between economies that transform capital into productive capacity and economies that increasingly use capital to compensate for the erosion of that capacity.
Europe has spent two decades defending its monetary integration. The next decade will determine whether it can achieve productive economic integration.
