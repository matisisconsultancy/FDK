THE GREAT CONVERSION TEST
AI is sustaining global growth. Capital markets are becoming more selective. The next competitive divide will be determined by who transforms technological investment into economic productivity.
IN FOCUS | THE BIG TAKE
The global economy is entering a decisive phase of the AI supercycle: technological investment is supporting growth, but the financial system is becoming increasingly selective about who can finance it.
Three developments this morning expose the emerging contradiction.
First, the IMF has identified AI infrastructure investment as a critical source of global economic resilience. Speaking in Bangkok today, Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva said AI investment is helping maintain global growth around 3%, despite the energy shock. She estimated that AI-related activity could add 0.5–1 percentage point to growth in advanced Asian economies and 0.2–0.8 points in emerging Asian economies.
Second, financial markets are beginning to distinguish AI infrastructure winners from capital-intensive aspirants. TSMC is expected to report another record quarterly profit, while Nvidia-backed Australian data-centre operator Firmus has abandoned a proposed $5 billion IPO amid investor concerns over valuation, leverage and execution.
Third, global capital is becoming more defensive. Investors directed $153.8 billion into money-market funds during the week ended October 7—the largest weekly inflow since May—even as technology and industrial investment opportunities continued expanding. 
The evidence reveals something fundamental.
The AI supercycle is generating an investment boom. But investment-led growth is not yet equivalent to productivity-led growth.
That distinction will increasingly determine the next great rotation of global capital.
THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION
Will AI generate a broad-based productivity revolution—or concentrate extraordinary profits among the few companies controlling computing capacity, electricity and capital, leaving the rest of the economy to absorb the financing costs?
This is the next stage of The Great Split.
The division is no longer simply between technological leaders and laggards.
It is between those capable of converting AI investment into sustainable economic returns and those whose growth depends on the continued availability of increasingly expensive capital.

US$153.8 billion moving into money-market funds.

That distinction will increasingly separate the winners and losers of the Next Economy.

II. FIVE SIGNALS SHAPING THE NEXT ECONOMY
1 | AI — FROM AN INVESTMENT SHOCK TO A PRODUCTIVITY TEST
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
At the opening of the IMF–World Bank meetings in Bangkok, Georgieva described AI infrastructure investment as a force supporting global demand.
But the IMF also highlighted an extraordinary employment challenge.
Approximately 40% of jobs worldwide could eventually be augmented, downgraded or displaced by AI.
Singapore’s President Tharman Shanmugaratnam warned that AI is already reducing opportunities for some new graduates and could widen inequalities between advanced and developing economies. 
FDK INTERPRETATION
This reveals the central paradox of the AI supercycle.
AI investment is already supporting economic growth. Its long-term distributional and productivity effects remain unresolved.
Today, the strongest measurable benefits are concentrated in infrastructure construction, semiconductor production, data centres and electricity demand.
The next phase must demonstrate that AI can raise output per employee, reduce operating costs, accelerate innovation and create new sources of revenue.
Otherwise, the investment boom risks creating an unusually concentrated economic structure.
Infrastructure owners capture the first wave of revenues.
Financiers capture interest and fees.
Technology suppliers capture scarcity premiums.
But the wider economy may experience slower productivity diffusion than investment projections anticipate.
SECOND-ORDER EFFECT
The next capital rotation may increasingly favour companies demonstrating measurable productivity improvements rather than merely announcing AI adoption.
Enterprise software, industrial automation, robotics, healthcare technology and intelligent mobility become more important as investors seek evidence of economic transformation beyond infrastructure expenditure.
THE NEXT AI BREAKTHROUGH WILL BE FINANCIAL: PROVING THAT INTELLIGENCE GENERATES RETURNS ABOVE ITS COST.

2 | SEMICONDUCTORS — THE MOST PROFITABLE BOTTLENECK IS BECOMING MORE VALUABLE
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
TSMC is expected to report third-quarter net profit of approximately NT$746.3 billion, equivalent to US$23.4 billion, an increase of 65% year on year.
The company has already reported quarterly revenue growth of 50%.
Its advanced semiconductor manufacturing and packaging capacity remain critical to Nvidia, Apple and the broader accelerated-computing ecosystem.
TSMC has raised its 2026 capital expenditure forecast to US$60–64 billion.
Meanwhile, Nvidia-backed Firmus withdrew its proposed US$5 billion Australian IPO after investors questioned the economics of its expansion programme. 
FDK INTERPRETATION
Two different AI economies are emerging.
The first owns scarce technological capacity and generates substantial current earnings.
The second must finance large-scale infrastructure before its expected revenues materialize.
That creates an important asymmetry.
Advanced semiconductor manufacturers can benefit from customers’ capital spending even when those customers face uncertain long-term returns.
Data-centre operators, by contrast, must manage construction risk, financing costs, utilization rates, electricity exposure and equipment depreciation.
The distinction becomes increasingly consequential as AI borrowing expands.
SECOND-ORDER EFFECT
Capital could concentrate in advanced chip manufacturing, semiconductor equipment, memory, optical networking and infrastructure suppliers with demonstrable pricing power.
Highly leveraged computing platforms may face greater valuation pressure.
THE NEXT MARKET ROTATION WILL NOT NECESSARILY BE OUT OF AI. IT WILL BE TOWARD THE MOST PRODUCTIVE PARTS OF ITS VALUE CHAIN.

3 | ENERGY AND SOVEREIGN CAPITAL — THE GLOBAL COST OF GROWTH IS RISING
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
Brent retreated toward US$103.38 on Monday morning following announcements concerning Russian diesel supplies and a possible Russia–Ukraine energy ceasefire.
However, the proposed ceasefire remains unconfirmed by the parties, while attacks on shipping and infrastructure continue in the Middle East.
The International Energy Agency has urged countries to consider further diesel releases.
Meanwhile, World Bank President Ajay Banga disclosed discussions with 30–40 countries seeking potential assistance in managing energy and price shocks.
Developing countries face approximately US$400 billion in external debt payments in 2026, with interest accounting for roughly one-third.
The World Bank reported mobilizing a record US$112 billion in private capital during its latest fiscal year. 
FDK INTERPRETATION
The global financial system is confronting three simultaneous demands for capital:
AI infrastructure expansion.
Sovereign debt refinancing.
Industrial and energy-system modernization.
These investment requirements interact through borrowing costs, credit spreads and the availability of long-duration financing.
The transmission mechanism is increasingly important:
Higher energy costs → persistent inflation → higher financing costs → more selective investment → slower deployment of marginal projects.
This is particularly significant for emerging economies exposed to imported energy and foreign-currency borrowing.
India provides an immediate example.
The Reserve Bank of India’s measures to reduce dollar demand from oil companies helped the rupee recover approximately 1% on Monday morning.
But exchange-rate stabilization does not eliminate the underlying energy-financing vulnerability. 
SECOND-ORDER EFFECT
Energy efficiency, storage, electricity transmission, nuclear upgrades and resilient transport infrastructure gain strategic importance.
Investors should increasingly distinguish between infrastructure with contracted cash flows and projects dependent on optimistic future demand.
ENERGY RESILIENCE IS BECOMING CREDIT RESILIENCE.

4 | EUROPE — FINANCIAL CONSOLIDATION MEETS THE INDUSTRIAL RECOVERY TEST
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
Europe enters the week with two developments of particular consequence.
First, sovereign bond markets remain under pressure.
French 10-year yields recently reached 4.994%, while the U.S. 10-year yield touched 5.3645%.
Second, the Italian banking consolidation process is moving toward a decisive stage.
Monte dei Paschi is expected to hold a board meeting late today to review its strategic options following Intesa Sanpaolo’s improved takeover proposal.
The October 29 shareholder vote remains central to the outcome.
Caltagirone has announced opposition to MPS’s proposed acquisitions of Banco BPM and Banca Generali and to its Mediobanca merger plan.
Crédit Agricole has separately rejected the attractiveness of MPS’s Banco BPM offer. 
FDK INTERPRETATION
Europe’s financial transformation is becoming inseparable from its industrial future.
The immediate Italian contest concerns control of banking assets.
The larger European opportunity concerns the mobilization of savings into productive investment.
Two different strategic models are emerging.
Intesa represents the potential consolidation of domestic financial depth.
UniCredit represents the development of continental financial reach.
Banco BPM remains a strategically important independent banking asset, while Generali illustrates the growing significance of long-duration institutional savings.
But banking consolidation alone does not create productive investment.
Europe must connect larger financial institutions with deeper capital markets, infrastructure finance and credible industrial projects.
That is the real opportunity.
SECOND-ORDER EFFECT
A successful combination of financial scale and industrial execution could accelerate investment in AI infrastructure, electricity networks, railways, advanced manufacturing and intelligent mobility.
The beneficiaries would extend far beyond banks.Engineering, automation, energy infrastructure and industrial technology could experience a new investment cycle.
EUROPE’S NEXT GREAT OPPORTUNITY IS NOT SIMPLY BANKING CONSOLIDATION. IT IS THE CREATION OF A CONTINENTAL CAPITAL-FORMATION SYSTEM.

5 | CHINA–EUROPE — THE NEW INDUSTRIAL CONTEST MOVES FROM EXPORTS TO LOCAL PRODUCTION
VERIFIED EVIDENCE
China’s domestic passenger-car sales fell 28.1% year on year in September, to 1.63 million units.
Exports increased 73.2%, reaching 923,000 vehicles.
The divergence illustrates how Chinese automotive companies are relying increasingly on external markets.
Meanwhile, the EU and China have reached a preliminary understanding that could substantially reduce Chinese hybrid-vehicle exports to Europe.
The arrangement reportedly envisages an initial annual ceiling of approximately 350,000 vehicles, increasing over subsequent years. The agreement’s implementation details remain unsettled.
Major Chinese manufacturers have already begun establishing production capabilities inside Europe, including investments in Hungary and Spain. 
FDK INTERPRETATION
The agreement could accelerate an important structural transformation.
Competition may shift from exporting finished products into Europe toward building industrial capacity inside Europe.
That would change the financial and industrial consequences of Chinese competition.
European production could benefit from additional investment, employment, supplier development and technological transfer.
But it could also intensify competitive pressure on established European manufacturers.
The critical distinction will be between countries capable of attracting technologically advanced production and countries relying principally on import restrictions.
SECOND-ORDER EFFECT
Industrial investment may increasingly favour European locations combining competitive electricity, skilled labour, predictable regulation and efficient logistics.
Spain, Central Europe and selected German industrial regions could compete for production mandates.
Rail freight, ports, urban mobility systems and industrial logistics would benefit where new manufacturing investment generates durable transport demand.
THE NEXT INDUSTRIAL WINNERS WILL NOT NECESSARILY BE THE COUNTRIES THAT PROTECT THEIR MARKETS MOST AGGRESSIVELY. THEY WILL BE THOSE THAT ATTRACT THE MOST PRODUCTIVE INVESTMENT.

III. THE HIDDEN PATTERN — THE CAPITAL PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDE
The five signals reveal a deeper evolution.
The global economy is moving through three distinct stages of the AI supercycle.
STAGE I — TECHNOLOGICAL DISCOVERY
Capital rewarded innovation, frontier models and access to computing capacity.
STAGE II — INFRASTRUCTURE EXPANSION
Investment accelerated across semiconductors, data centres, electricity and networking.
STAGE III — ECONOMIC CONVERSION
The emerging stage will reward businesses and economies capable of translating technological infrastructure into higher productivity, stronger cash flows and sustainable returns.
This third stage is the most consequential.
It determines whether the AI supercycle becomes a broad-based industrial transformation or remains concentrated around a relatively small number of infrastructure owners.
Three hidden patterns deserve particular attention.
First, the financial return on AI investment will be distributed unevenly. Semiconductor manufacturers, infrastructure owners, model developers and enterprise users have fundamentally different revenue models, capital requirements and risk profiles.
Second, financial conditions will influence the speed of technological diffusion. Companies with strong balance sheets can invest through periods of expensive capital. Financially constrained competitors may be forced to postpone deployment.
Third, national competitiveness will increasingly depend on the ability to combine technology, energy and finance. Countries that can assemble these three components into functioning industrial systems will attract more investment and capture a larger share of the value created.THE GREAT SPLIT IS MOVING FROM TECHNOLOGICAL CAPABILITY TO CAPITAL PRODUCTIVITY.

VII. THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS — WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?
The coming months could produce three consequential changes in global financial dynamics.
1. A NEW HIERARCHY WITHIN AI
Capital may become more concentrated in companies controlling scarce technology and generating current profits.
Developers whose business models depend on repeated financing rounds could face tougher terms.
2. A BROADER INDUSTRIAL INVESTMENT CYCLE
The demand for electricity, computing infrastructure and industrial automation could spread investment opportunities beyond technology companies.
Europe could benefit if it accelerates execution and financing integration.
3. A REPRICING OF SOVEREIGN COMPETITIVENESS
Countries capable of mobilizing domestic savings and private investment while maintaining fiscal credibility could acquire greater strategic flexibility.
Countries with expensive energy, weak financing structures and limited investment capacity could fall further behind.
These developments would reinforce one another.
Technological acceleration creates investment opportunities.
Capital selection determines which opportunities are financed.
Productivity growth determines which investments ultimately succeed.
That is the emerging circular causality of the Next Economy.

IX. THE VELOCITY INSIGHT
The global economy is undergoing a transformation of extraordinary scale.
But the decisive variable is changing.
The first phase of the AI supercycle was about technological invention.
The second was about infrastructure expansion.
The third will be about economic conversion.
This is where the next great concentration of financial and industrial power will emerge.
For the United States, the challenge is converting technological leadership into sustainable returns without excessive financial leverage.
For China, it is translating industrial scale into profitable domestic and international growth.
For Europe, it is mobilizing its savings and technological capabilities into a new cycle of productive investment.
For emerging economies, it is balancing industrial ambition with financing and energy resilience.
The opportunity is considerable.
But the next phase will be less forgiving.
The companies able to convert AI into measurable productivity will accelerate.
Those unable to generate adequate returns will face increasing financial pressure.
The countries capable of connecting capital, technology and infrastructure will attract investment.
Those unable to do so risk falling permanently behind.
The Great Split is therefore no longer simply about who possesses the most advanced technology.
It is about who can convert that technology into sustainable economic power.
THE NEXT ECONOMY WILL NOT BE WON BY THOSE WHO INVEST THE MOST.IT WILL BE WON BY THOSE WHO TRANSFORM INVESTMENT INTO PRODUCTIVITY THE FASTEST.
In the next industrial age, competitive advantage will be measured not by the velocity of capital expenditure, but by the velocity of economic returns.
