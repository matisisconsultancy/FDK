THE GREAT VALUE-CAPTURE DIVIDE
AI Is Accelerating Global Trade. The Distribution of Economic Power Is Becoming More Unequal.

THE BIG TAKE
The AI Supercycle is accelerating technological production faster than it is distributing economic prosperity. The next Great Split will be determined by who captures the value—not simply who produces it.
Three developments provide particularly consequential evidence.
First, the United Nations has quantified the concentration of AI’s economic rewards.
UNCTAD’s Trade and Development Report 2026, published October 9, forecasts global economic growth of just 2.6%, down from 2.9% in 2025, despite expected real trade growth of approximately 4%.
More revealingly, its examination of an advanced AI server rack finds that post-tax profits represent 68% of traced value added, while workers capture less than 15%.
These figures describe a particular production-chain case study, not the distribution of income throughout the global AI economy. Nevertheless, they provide striking evidence of how concentrated the rewards of AI infrastructure can become. 
Second, Europe is confronting a strategic contradiction.
On October 10, the Irish presidency of the European Union proposed an 8% reduction in the Commission’s 2028–2034 budget proposal, including a 13% reduction in proposed competitiveness and security expenditure.
The overall budget would still be approximately 30% larger than the current framework. But the allocation of resources raises a critical question: can Europe accelerate industrial transformation while reducing the proposed funding for some of its future-oriented priorities? 
Third, the AI industry is moving toward deeper vertical integration.
Nvidia is reportedly exploring an acquisition of, or additional investment in, Reflection AI, a developer of open-weight AI models.
The discussions are preliminary and may not result in a transaction. Nevertheless, the strategic significance is clear: a leading supplier of accelerated computing is considering stronger participation in the model layer itself. 
Together, these developments expose a broader evolutionary pattern.
TECHNOLOGICAL DIFFUSION IS ACCELERATING. ECONOMIC CAPTURE IS CONCENTRATING.
The defining question of the next AI phase is therefore changing.
It is no longer simply: Who can build intelligence?
It is:
Who will own the productive assets, financial returns and competitive advantages generated by intelligence?

II. FIVE SIGNALS DEFINING GLOBAL VELOCITY
1 — GLOBAL ECONOMY: MORE TRADE, LESS BROAD-BASED PROSPERITY
UNCTAD’s latest projections reveal a widening distinction between expanding international commerce and the distribution of its benefits.
Its report forecasts:
Indicator UNCTAD 2026 assessment
Global GDP growth 2.6%
Global GDP growth in 2025 2.9%
Real global trade growth Approximately 4%
Developing-economy growth 4.0%
China’s projected growth 4.5%
India’s projected growth 7.3%
Asia’s contribution to global growth 59%
These are estimates and forecasts, not realised full-year results. 
The underlying message is consequential.
AI-related trade can accelerate while global growth slows.
Higher technological output does not automatically generate proportionate increases in aggregate productivity, household income or economic convergence.
The next competitive distinction will be between countries participating in AI supply chains and countries capturing their highest-value activities.
2 — AI ECONOMICS: THE PROFIT CONCENTRATION SIGNAL
UNCTAD’s server-rack case study provides a particularly revealing illustration of value distribution.
In the examined supply chain, post-tax profits account for 68% of traced value added, while labour receives less than 15%.
Separately, UNCTAD reports that developed economies accounted for approximately 70% of announced greenfield investment value in high-value strategic sectors during 2020–2025. 
The figures concern different measures and populations and should not be combined into a single concentration ratio.
Their strategic implication is nevertheless important.
Physical manufacturing, technological ownership, intellectual property and financial returns are not necessarily located in the same economy.
Countries can supply minerals, components or industrial labour without capturing a proportionate share of the final profits.
This creates a new challenge for industrial policy:
Moving into the AI supply chain is not enough. Economies must move upward within it.
3 — UNITED STATES: VERTICAL INTEGRATION BECOMES THE NEXT COMPETITIVE FRONTIER
The reported Nvidia–Reflection AI discussions offer a potential glimpse of the next phase of technological competition.
Nvidia’s established advantage lies in accelerated computing, supporting software and the wider AI development ecosystem.
Reflection AI specialises in open-weight models and software-development automation.
Deeper integration could potentially connect computing infrastructure more closely with model development, enterprise applications and distribution.
But no agreement has been confirmed, and no acquisition-related economic benefit is measurable today. 
The second-order implication is more important than the individual transaction.
Infrastructure suppliers may increasingly seek to capture value higher in the AI stack.
If successful, this could reinforce scale advantages and increase the importance of competition policy, interoperability and open technological standards.
The US GVI remains unchanged.
4 — EUROPE: THE BUDGET DEBATE BECOMES A COMPETITIVENESS TEST
The Irish presidency’s October 10 proposal seeks to reconcile competing demands from European governments.
The compromise would reduce the Commission’s proposed seven-year budget by approximately €141 billion in constant-price terms.
Crucially, it proposes a 13% reduction relative to the Commission’s planned allocation for competitiveness and security, compared with a 3% reduction for agriculture and regional expenditure.
Germany, Sweden and the Netherlands argue for further fiscal restraint. The European Parliament opposes the proposed reductions.EU leaders will discuss the proposal on October 15–16. 
This is a negotiating proposal, not an adopted budget.
But it reveals a significant tension.
Europe is attempting to accelerate technological transformation while confronting political disagreements over the fiscal resources necessary to support it.
The risk is that Europe preserves established expenditure priorities while moving too slowly to finance emerging industrial capabilities.
The next European competitiveness challenge may be determined less by technological ambition than by the speed and quality of collective capital allocation.
5 — EUROPE AND CHINA: MANAGED INTERDEPENDENCE TAKES SHAPE
The European Union and China reached a preliminary understanding following trade consultations on October 8–9.
The reported elements include improved access for selected European exports, cooperation on rare-earth supply chains and potential changes affecting Chinese electric-vehicle exports to Europe.
Implementation requires further political approval. The agreement is therefore not yet evidence of realised improvements in trade flows or industrial competitiveness. 
The strategic significance is substantial.
Europe is attempting to reconcile two objectives:
REDUCING STRATEGIC DEPENDENCIES WHILE PRESERVING ECONOMIC INTERDEPENDENCE.
This may increasingly define European trade and industrial policy.

IV. SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS — THE NEXT GREAT SPLIT
I — The productivity dividend may be captured before it is distributed
The first-order benefit of AI is lower production costs and greater output potential.
The second-order question concerns ownership.
When intellectual property, infrastructure, distribution and financial capital are concentrated, the initial productivity dividend may accrue disproportionately to asset owners.
Broader benefits depend on competition, adoption, wage bargaining, prices and reinvestment.
This creates an important analytical distinction:
PRODUCTIVITY CREATION ≠ PRODUCTIVITY DISTRIBUTION.
The economic and political consequences of AI will increasingly depend on both.
II — Global value chains may become more hierarchical
UNCTAD’s evidence suggests that advanced economies retain significant advantages in attracting investment into strategic high-value activities.
That raises the possibility that AI strengthens existing international hierarchies rather than automatically accelerating convergence.
Countries lacking domestic computing capacity, intellectual property or adequate financial resources may participate in production while capturing only limited returns.
The potential outcome is a new form of technological dependence.
Access to intelligence becomes widespread. Ownership of its economics remains concentrated.
III — Europe faces a capital-allocation paradox
Europe’s most immediate danger may not be insufficient capital in absolute terms.It may be the inability to redirect sufficient capital toward technologies, infrastructure and industries capable of generating future growth.
The proposed EU budget revision illustrates the political difficulty of shifting expenditure priorities.
At the same time, Europe’s industrial recovery remains uneven.
The Netherlands and Sweden have demonstrated improving industrial momentum, while Italy faces a more difficult manufacturing environment.
If European financing decisions reinforce existing structures rather than productive transformation, these divergences could deepen.
A common market without sufficiently effective capital allocation risks becoming a collection of increasingly unequal industrial economies.
IV — Vertical integration could redefine competition
Nvidia’s reported interest in Reflection AI illustrates the potential convergence of computing infrastructure and model development.
The larger evolutionary movement could connect:
CHIPS → COMPUTE → MODELS → SOFTWARE → INDUSTRIAL APPLICATIONS → CASH FLOW
The companies controlling several stages may possess advantages in integration, distribution and financing.
However, vertical integration can also increase capital intensity and create regulatory scrutiny.
Its financial value must ultimately be demonstrated through incremental margins, customer retention and returns on invested capital.
V — The market may increasingly distinguish economic capture from technological leadership
The next AI investment rotation is unlikely to be determined solely by technological performance.
Investors will increasingly need to distinguish businesses that produce critical inputs from businesses that capture the final economic benefits.
This changes the hierarchy of analysis.
AI CAPABILITY → MARKET ACCESS → PRICING POWER → CASH GENERATION → CAPITAL COMPOUNDING
Technological excellence creates opportunity.
Economic capture determines the return.

V. FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS
1 — Investors: follow the ownership of the productivity dividend.
Evaluate intellectual-property control, distribution, pricing power, free cash flow and capital intensity. Participation in an expanding AI market is not equivalent to capturing its profits.
2 — Boards: measure economic capture, not technological adoption.
AI investment should be evaluated through incremental productivity, operating margins, asset turnover and returns on capital.
3 — CEOs: compete for higher positions in the value chain.
The objective should be to move beyond supplying capacity or implementing technology toward owning differentiated processes, customer relationships and recurring economic value.
4 — Policymakers: make broad-based productivity diffusion a strategic objective.
Industrial policy should encourage domestic value creation, competition, workforce adaptation and access to productive capital—not merely infrastructure announcements.
5 — European leaders: treat the October 15–16 budget discussions as an industrial competitiveness test.
The crucial issue is not simply how much Europe spends.
It is whether resources are directed toward activities capable of expanding the continent’s future productive capacity.
