III. INFLATION — THE DEFLATIONARY DIVIDEND IS STILL AHEAD

New US producer-price data released November 13 reinforce the immediate problem.

The Producer Price Index rose 0.3% in October and 6.5% year on year. Electronic components and accessories increased 31.7% year on year, while computer-equipment prices rose 21.4%.

These particular technology-price movements are consistent with the supply pressures associated with AI capital formation. They do not establish that AI caused the broader 6.5% producer inflation rate. Bureau of Labor Statistics +1

Meanwhile, San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly signalled that additional tightening was likely to be needed, citing persistent services inflation and companies’ increasing willingness to pass input costs to customers. Reuters

The monetary consequence is that central banks cannot rely on future Industrial AI deflation to offset present inflation unless the gains become measurable.

IV. BANK OF ENGLAND — THE HIGHER-FOR-LONGER RISK EXTENDS INTO 2027

NOVEMBER 14 | HUW PILL INTERVIEW

In an interview published Saturday, Bank of England Chief Economist Huw Pill indicated that interest rates would probably need to rise further in 2027, beyond a potential December increase.

He expressed concern about persistent energy inflation feeding through into wages and business pricing. UK Bank Rate currently stands at 4%, and the Bank expects inflation to approach 5% around mid-2027.

Pill considered market expectations implying rates near 5% in 2027 not unreasonable. These remarks represent his policy assessment, not a collective Monetary Policy Committee commitment. Reuters

The implications for AI and industrial investment are indirect but significant: financing conditions may tighten further even as infrastructure investment needs remain large.

V. THE GREAT SPLIT — THE NEXT DIFFERENTIATION IN CAPITAL MARKETS

Three divisions now deserve particular attention.

First, the divide between companies able to finance AI investment through operating cash flow and those dependent on increasingly complex external borrowing.

Second, the divide between sectors already paying higher prices for AI-related inputs and industries beginning to realise measurable productivity savings.

Third, the divide between economies capable of sustaining investment under higher interest rates and those constrained by expensive energy, weak productivity and fiscal vulnerability.

These divisions will increasingly shape credit spreads, corporate valuations, investment returns and sovereign competitiveness.

The Great Split is therefore becoming not merely technological, but financial.

VI. THE IMPLICATIONS FOR INVESTORS AND EU POLICYMAKERS

For institutional investors, the next analytical priorities are interconnected exposure across lenders and borrowers, future lease and guarantee commitments, cash-flow conversion from AI expenditure, and sensitivity to persistently higher real rates.For European policymakers, the Fed’s new analysis strengthens the case for more rigorous monitoring of AI-related exposures across banks, insurers, private-credit funds and special-purpose financing structures.

The ECB’s November 12 research had already identified favourable financing conditions for European AI-exposed companies. The new Fed report supplies the corresponding systemic-risk perspective: financing advantages can reverse if expected productivity and cash flows disappoint.

Europe should therefore prioritise productive industrial adoption, competitive energy supply and transparent financing, rather than maximising AI investment volume alone.

No comparably consequential new AI-specific decision was verified from the ECB, MAS, SNB or BIS in the November 13–14 review. Nor has a new internationally coordinated AI monetary-policy framework been announced.

THE FINAL TAKE — THE NEXT TEST IS FINANCIAL, NOT TECHNOLOGICAL

The Federal Reserve has made an important development visible: the financing architecture behind AI is becoming a financial-stability concern of systemic relevance.

The fundamental question is no longer whether AI can transform production. It is whether the capital committed to that transformation can generate sufficient returns under a structurally more demanding monetary regime.

For central banks, the challenge is distinguishing productive investment from financial excess without unnecessarily suppressing the technology’s long-term potential.

For investors, the distinction is between economic value creation and financing-dependent expectations.

For Europe, it is between financing an industrial productivity revolution and becoming dependent on the financial returns generated elsewhere.

THE BIG TAKE

A consequential turning point occurred on November 13. The Federal Reserve’s new Financial Stability Report establishes AI as the most frequently cited risk among surveyed financial-market contacts, while revealing the extraordinary scale and complexity of financing behind the data-centre investment boom.

On the same day, Fed Vice Chair Philip Jefferson explicitly examined how AI could raise the neutral interest rate, fuel near-term inflation and displace workers before delivering broad productivity improvements.

On November 14, Bank of England Chief Economist Huw Pill reinforced the prospect of continued monetary tightening into 2027.

The emerging conclusion is consequential:

The AI Supercycle is no longer simply reshaping equity valuations. It is becoming an important determinant of credit creation, financial stability and the equilibrium cost of capital.

The Fed also identifies more than $1 trillion in future leases not yet commenced and over $2 trillion in other contractual commitments and guarantees among major technology companies.

These commitments are not directly comparable with outstanding debt: some are contingent, undiscounted or economically interconnected. They must not be treated as $3 trillion in additional bank debt. federalreserve.gov

The critical revelation concerns circular financing. Hyperscalers, semiconductor suppliers, infrastructure developers and financing vehicles are increasingly connected through long-term leases, guarantees, equity investments and credit agreements.

Those relationships may provide resilience by distributing or absorbing losses. But they can also obscure the full economic exposure of individual companies.

The Federal Reserve explicitly compares aspects of this investment cycle with the late-1990s telecommunications buildout, while stopping short of predicting a comparable collapse.

The investment implication: understanding AI balance-sheet exposure increasingly requires examining the entire financing network, not merely reported corporate debt. federalreserve.gov

II. THE MONETARY INFLECTION — JEFFERSON CONNECTS AI TO THE NEUTRAL RATE

NOVEMBER 13 | FED VICE CHAIR PHILIP JEFFERSON

Jefferson’s speech, New Technology, Old Questions, supplies the monetary counterpart to the stability report.

His evidence draws a sharp distinction between adoption and realised productivity.

Approximately 25% of US firms report AI usage in at least one business function, with materially higher adoption in knowledge-intensive sectors. Yet one survey cited in his speech estimates that AI raised sales per employee by only about 0.5% cumulatively over three years.

Jefferson expects productivity benefits to increase substantially but gradually.
