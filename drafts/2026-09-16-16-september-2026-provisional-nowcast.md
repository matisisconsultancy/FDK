United Kingdom — 
−0.1: the conversion problem moves into labour
The UK’s August labour report provides unusually consistent evidence across several measures. Vacancies fell to 702,000, their lowest level since 2014 outside the pandemic. Preliminary payroll employment declined by 26,000 in August, while July was revised to a 19,000 decline. Regular earnings growth slowed to 3.5%, and private-sector regular pay increased only 2.9%, the weakest rate since late 2020. 
This matters because the UK had recently received an upgrade as digital services, computer hardware investment and business investment accelerated. Today’s evidence reveals the missing transmission mechanism:
AI and digital investment ↑ → productivity capacity ↑ → GDP ↑
but:
vacancies ↓ → hiring ↓ → payroll employment ↓.
The GVI therefore reduces the UK by only 0.1, primarily through Enterprise Transformation and Discovery Capacity. Smart Capital provides a partial offset because softer wage growth makes an immediate wage-price spiral less likely. 
This is not evidence that AI is causing the employment slowdown. It is evidence that productive investment and labour absorption are moving in different directions.
Japan — no score change, but today’s most important macro signal
Japan’s August exports increased 19.3% year on year, exceeding expectations and marking a twelfth consecutive monthly increase. Semiconductor-related shipments were a principal contributor; exports to the United States rose 24.9% and exports to China 20.6%. 
Yet imports surged 28%, their largest increase in nearly four years. Crude-oil import volumes increased only 3.6%, but their value jumped 58.7%. Japan consequently posted a ¥1.106 trillion trade deficit. 
That creates two simultaneous chains of causality:
AI demand → semiconductors → exports → industrial investment → corporate Velocity
and:
geopolitical disruption → oil >$100 → import bill → inflation → BOJ tightening → higher capital cost.
No numerical adjustment is admitted because the effects offset each other almost exactly. Japan stays at 84.9.
But the strategic implication is powerful: energy intensity is becoming a tax on Intelligence Velocity for import-dependent economies.
United States — the grid responds, but the hurdle rate rises faster
Hitachi Energy announced a $528 million transformer factory in Mississippi, the company’s largest single investment in its US manufacturing expansion. Transformers are becoming one of the critical physical bottlenecks in the expansion of electricity-intensive data centres, industrial electrification and grid capacity. 
This is high-quality Intelligence Infrastructure evidence, but $528 million remains below the national materiality threshold required for another US score increase.
The financial offset is considerably larger. The US 10-year Treasury yield breached 5% and reached its highest level since 2007, while markets assigned a 94.5% probability to a 25-basis-point Federal Reserve increase later today. Wall Street declined for a second consecutive session as oil, rates and renewed anxiety about the pace of AI development hit technology valuations. 
The Fed decision itself is not incorporated in this update because it will occur after today’s evidence cutoff.
The United States therefore remains 94.4.
Netherlands — yesterday’s lithography upgrade begins to propagate downstream
A second Dutch semiconductor signal appeared after yesterday’s cutoff.
Eindhoven-based Axelera AI launched its second-generation Europa inference chip and disclosed multiple supply agreements with AI factories. Its processors will be incorporated into selected Dell and Supermicro products. Axelera says more than 600 customers already use its technology and that signed contracts are worth tens of millions of dollars; the company is pursuing a potential sales pipeline of as much as $1.5 billion, although Reuters correctly notes that this is not yet equivalent to orders or revenue. 
This is strategically important because it extends the Dutch semiconductor position:
ASML lithography → advanced-node manufacturing → European chip design → inference → AI factories.
But no additional +0.1 is awarded one day after the Netherlands’ +0.2 High-NA upgrade. The additionality is real; the realised economic magnitude remains below threshold.
The Netherlands stays 80.4, with momentum strengthening.
France — domestic demand weakens, score held
The Banque de France lowered its 2026 GDP growth projection to 0.4% from 0.5%, citing weak consumer spending, cautious corporate investment, expensive financing and energy uncertainty. It expects household purchasing power to contract in 2026 and unemployment to reach 8.4% by year-end. Its survey of 8,500 executives nevertheless points to approximately 0.1% quarter-on-quarter growth in Q3 after a stagnant first half. 
The evidence is negative but does not clear the GVI numerical threshold because the principal observation is a forecast revision rather than a realised deterioration in productive capacity.
France remains 81.5, with momentum moving from “selective recovery” toward softening domestic absorption.
Germany and Spain — important, but no recalculation
Germany’s ZEW current-conditions measure improved materially from −61.1 to −47.1, while expectations edged up to 34.7. But the expectations reading undershot consensus and ZEW explicitly warned that elevated energy costs remain a significant economic risk. The net signal is positive but insufficiently directional for a score change. Germany remains 83.2.Spain’s harmonised inflation rose sharply to 4.6% in August from 3.9%, although core inflation actually eased to 2.9%. Because the final reading largely confirms the earlier flash estimate and the acceleration is heavily energy-related rather than broad-based, it fails the additionality threshold. Spain remains 75.0. 
Smart Capital — the oil shock persists
Brent closed Tuesday at $108.75 per barrel, up another 2.9%, as Saudi Arabia suspended Yanbu loadings and cancelled some crude deliveries to Europe. Reuters reports that the damaged East-West pipeline could affect flows equivalent to as much as 4% of global supply, while commodity-vessel traffic through Hormuz had fallen to only four vessels on Monday. 
Early Wednesday Brent eased toward $107.82 after US industry data pointed to a large inventory build. That does not reverse the GVI shock because the Saudi infrastructure constraint, Hormuz disruption and record diesel-market pressure remain unresolved. 
The distinction is crucial:
Volatile oil is a market variable. Persistent energy insecurity is a production variable.
The latter is what enters the GVI.

Institutional interpretation — 
The Energy Tax on Intelligence
The hidden pattern today is increasingly clear.
The AI Supercycle is no longer suffering from a shortage of technological demand.
Japan’s semiconductor exports are surging. Dutch inference chips are entering AI factories. America is expanding transformer manufacturing. AI capital formation remains extraordinarily strong. 
The constraint is appearing somewhere else:
the physical economy is beginning to impose an Energy Tax and a Capital Tax on intelligence.
Oil above $100 raises transport, materials and backup-generation costs. Electricity infrastructure requires transformers and new grid investment. Higher inflation pushes central banks toward tighter policy. Treasury yields above 5% raise the discount rate applied to data centres whose returns may arrive years in the future. 
This produces a new chain of causality:
AI demand ↑
→ electricity demand ↑
→ physical infrastructure demand ↑
→ commodity and equipment scarcity ↑
→ inflation ↑
→ policy rates ↑
→ cost of capital ↑
→ required AI productivity ↑
That final arrow is the crucial one.
For investors
The AI trade is entering a more discriminating phase. The premium should move toward businesses able to generate high utilisation, pricing power and free cash flow quickly enough to outrun a higher cost of capital.
The scarce assets are increasingly not only GPUs. They include transformers, grid connections, power generation, advanced lithography, memory, optical networking and secured energy supply.
For executives
The relevant measure of AI economics should no longer be simply cost per token.
It should become:
economic value created per fully loaded unit of intelligence.
That denominator must include accelerated computing, electricity, financing, connectivity, cooling, security and redundancy.
For policymakers
The UK and Japan reveal two different absorption failures.
Britain shows that investment can rise while hiring weakens.
Japan shows that advanced manufacturing can accelerate while imported energy destroys terms of trade.
The next generation of national AI strategy must therefore connect:
Intelligence Infrastructure → Enterprise Transformation → energy security → labour mobility → productivity → wages → domestic demand.
Without that chain, technological acceleration can coexist with economic frustration.
Bottom line
The FDK Global Velocity Index remains at 108.0. The United Kingdom falls to 82.3.
The deeper change is far more consequential than the one-decimal headline.
The first phase of the AI Supercycle was governed by the cost of accelerated computing.
The next phase will increasingly be governed by the fully loaded cost of industrialising intelligence.That means the next competitive advantage will not belong simply to the countries that can generate the most tokens or install the most accelerators.
It will belong to those capable of producing the greatest economic value per unit of energy, capital and intelligence deployed.
That may become the defining productivity equation of the Next Economy.
