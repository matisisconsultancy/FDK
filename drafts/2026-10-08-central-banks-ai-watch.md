THE FED HAS REWRITTEN THE AI MONETARY EQUATION

In Focus | 8 October 2026

The AI Supercycle has crossed a decisive institutional threshold: the Federal Reserve now explicitly identifies AI-driven investment, inflation, corporate borrowing and changes in the neutral interest rate within its monetary-policy deliberations.

The minutes of the September 15–16 FOMC meeting, released on October 7, provide the strongest evidence yet that the Great Split is becoming a central-bank policy problem.

Three findings are particularly consequential.

First, Fed officials identified accelerating AI investment as a contributor to persistent inflation, including rising core-goods prices.

Second, the meeting’s market analysis linked substantial AI-related corporate borrowing to competition for capital and upward pressure on Treasury yields.

Third, some policymakers reported raising their estimates of the neutral federal funds rate, although the minutes do not establish that AI alone caused those revisions.

Together, these developments establish a more demanding monetary framework:

AI CAPITAL FORMATION → INFLATION PRESSURE → HIGHER FINANCING COSTS → HIGHER POLICY-RATE EXPECTATIONS → PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND STILL UNCERTAIN. 

1. THE FED’S REACTION FUNCTION IS CHANGING

The September minutes reveal a significant distinction between AI’s immediate inflationary effects and its prospective productivity benefits.

Participants observed that the AI infrastructure expansion was contributing to higher business costs and core-goods inflation. Some warned that investment demand could exceed available productive capacity over the medium term.

The implications extend beyond technology.

AI investment competes with other sectors for electricity, semiconductors, construction capacity and specialised labour. If those constraints persist, sectoral price increases can spread through the broader economy.

The Fed therefore faces a difficult sequencing problem.

It cannot offset today’s inflation with productivity gains that may not materialise for several years.

The Committee unanimously raised the federal funds target range by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00% in September. Most participants subsequently judged that another increase would likely be appropriate before year-end, conditional on incoming data.

This is a meaningful development in monetary policy, although it does not establish that AI was the decisive cause of the September increase. 

2. AI CAPITAL FORMATION IS REPRICING GLOBAL CAPITAL

The more consequential finding concerns the bond market.

The FOMC minutes report that nominal Treasury yields increased by approximately 35 basis points across the two- to ten-year segment during the intermeeting period.

The Fed’s market review identified AI infrastructure financing among the factors contributing to higher term premiums and Treasury yields, alongside geopolitical developments, stronger economic data and monetary-policy expectations.

This is a material change in the financial transmission of the Supercycle.

Until recently, extraordinary AI investment was primarily interpreted as a driver of corporate earnings and equity valuations.

It is increasingly becoming a determinant of the global demand for capital.

The second-order consequences are substantial:

AI DEBT ISSUANCE → GREATER COMPETITION FOR CAPITAL → HIGHER LONG-TERM YIELDS → HIGHER INVESTMENT HURDLE RATES → GREATER CORPORATE AND SOVEREIGN FINANCING PRESSURE.

Importantly, the Fed also observed that equity valuations and relatively narrow corporate credit spreads continued to support financial conditions.

The tightening transmitted through Treasury yields was therefore being partially offset by buoyant risk assets.

The AI Supercycle is simultaneously raising the price of debt and supporting the value of equity.

Which force dominates will increasingly determine monetary restrictiveness. 

3. THE NEUTRAL-RATE QUESTION BECOMES CONSEQUENTIAL

The minutes introduce a further complication.Some Fed participants reported upward revisions to their neutral-rate estimates.

That does not demonstrate an AI-driven structural increase in r*. Nevertheless, it reinforces the policy relevance of a central question:

Could extraordinary AI capital formation sustain a higher equilibrium real interest rate before Industrial-AI deflation becomes visible?

The distinction matters.

Higher investment demand and expected returns on capital can put upward pressure on equilibrium real rates.

Productivity diffusion, meanwhile, can expand productive capacity and moderate inflation.

These effects are neither necessarily simultaneous nor mutually exclusive.

The Fed staff’s September projection anticipated inflation returning to 2% in 2029, while acknowledging considerable uncertainty about AI’s contribution to potential output and inflation.

Central banks therefore face an uncomfortable possibility: the investment boom can change financial conditions much sooner than its productivity benefits change measured economic performance. 

4. JAPAN PROVIDES THE INDUSTRIAL COUNTERPOINT

The Bank of Japan’s October 8 regional assessment adds an important dimension.

Strong global AI demand is supporting Japanese investment in electronics, industrial machinery, telecommunications infrastructure and data centres.

At the same time, Japanese businesses face broader inflationary pressures from imported materials, energy, exchange-rate developments and labour costs.

The significance is not that AI explains all these pressures. It does not.

Rather, Japan demonstrates how AI infrastructure demand can strengthen selected industrial sectors while an economy simultaneously confronts more general cost inflation.

For the Bank of Japan, the challenge is to distinguish sustainable productivity-driven growth from demand conditions that could reinforce inflation persistence. 

5. THE GREAT SPLIT ENTERS THE LABOUR MARKET

The Fed minutes contain another important signal.

Participants observed strong wage gains among specialised workers supporting AI infrastructure investment, even as aggregate wage growth remained relatively moderate.

That divergence matters.

The Great Split is not simply between companies investing in AI and those falling behind.

It is increasingly emerging between workers, occupations and regions benefiting immediately from capital formation and those awaiting productivity diffusion.

The monetary consequence is potentially significant.

AI can generate localised wage inflation without producing equivalent economy-wide wage growth.

A single unemployment rate may therefore provide an increasingly incomplete picture of labour-market conditions during the technological transition.

WHAT’S NEXT: THE FINANCIAL CONSEQUENCES

For investors, the policy message is becoming more demanding.

The next stage of the AI Supercycle will be judged increasingly by the relationship between capital intensity, financing costs and realised productivity.

Five implications follow.

1. US Treasuries: Monitor whether extraordinary AI-related borrowing continues contributing to term premiums independently of policy-rate expectations.
2. AI equities: Prioritise free cash flow, capital productivity and the timing of returns over headline capital-expenditure growth.
3. Corporate credit: Distinguish internally financed AI expansion from business models requiring continuous access to increasingly expensive external capital.
4. European markets: Watch whether US-driven increases in global long-term yields translate into disproportionate financing pressure on European investment.
5. Central-bank policy: Treat changes in neutral-rate estimates, productivity assumptions and the transmission of long-term yields as consequential signals, not secondary forecasting adjustments.

THE EUROPEAN CONSEQUENCE

For European policymakers, the Federal Reserve’s findings expose an increasingly important asymmetry.

The United States is generating extraordinary AI capital formation.Europe participates in the resulting global repricing of capital but captures a smaller proportion of the associated infrastructure investment.

This creates the risk of an adverse transmission mechanism:

**HIGHER GLOBAL FINANCING COSTS

• INSUFFICIENT EUROPEAN AI INVESTMENT
    = WEAKER PRODUCTIVITY DIFFUSION AND GREATER COMPETITIVE DIVERGENCE.**

The European response must therefore extend beyond monetary policy.

Capital-markets integration, energy infrastructure, investment in compute capacity, industrial adoption and workforce adaptation are becoming parts of the same economic challenge.

The ECB cannot independently deliver the productivity gains needed to offset the inflationary costs of the transition.

That requires a coordinated European capital-formation strategy.

FIVE KEY TAKEAWAYS

I. Monetary policy: The Fed is explicitly incorporating AI-related demand pressures into its assessment of inflation persistence.

II. Capital formation: AI infrastructure financing is becoming relevant to the pricing of sovereign and corporate debt.

III. Neutral rates: The possibility of structurally higher equilibrium rates deserves greater attention, although its relationship with AI remains uncertain.

IV. The Great Split: The divergence between capital formation and productivity diffusion increasingly extends into labour markets and monetary transmission.

V. Europe: The strategic challenge is to convert European savings into productive AI investment before imported financing pressures further constrain competitiveness.

THE NEXT MONETARY REGIME WILL BE DETERMINED BY THE RETURN ON AI CAPITAL

The central question is no longer whether artificial intelligence will transform productivity.

It is whether the resulting increase in productive capacity will arrive quickly enough to justify the extraordinary capital committed to building it.

The Federal Reserve’s latest minutes make the stakes clearer.

The AI Supercycle is already influencing inflation, financing costs and monetary-policy deliberations.

Industrial-AI deflation remains a plausible long-term outcome, not an established counterweight to current price pressures.

The Great Split has reached the central banks. The next economic regime will be determined by whether productivity can outrun the rising cost of capital.
