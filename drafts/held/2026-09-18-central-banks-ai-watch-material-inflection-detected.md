The most consequential new signal comes from the Bank of England's 17 September meeting and the Reserve Bank of Australia on 18 September. In both cases, AI is no longer being discussed primarily as a future productivity shock. Policymakers are now explicitly treating the AI build-out itself as a source of current inflation pressure.

That is an important escalation of the thesis we have been tracking. A year ago, central banks were asking whether AI would raise productivity. They are now beginning to ask a fundamentally different question: **can the world physically build AI fast enough without creating inflation first?**

::signals The Reaction Function Turns | how the mechanism is being named
# The BoE has put AI inside a rate vote
The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% by 6–3, but the three dissenters — Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill — wanted an immediate 25bp increase to 4%. The minutes state that AI supply constraints were among the global factors adding inflation pressure. Greene cited AI-related supply constraints as a source of persistent inflation; Pill said the broader AI build-out was magnifying energy and other global inflation risks. Even members voting to hold, including Sarah Breeden and Dave Ramsden, referred to AI-related global supply shocks as part of the upside inflation distribution.

The emerging chain runs: AI infrastructure demand → semiconductors, electricity, construction and capital demand → supply constraints → input-price inflation → second-round wage/price risk → tighter monetary policy. This is exactly the opposite of the simplistic assumption that AI automatically produces lower inflation through productivity.
signal: AI is now part of the upside inflation distribution in an MPC decision.

# The BoE separates the short rate from the long end
The same meeting adopted a multi-year QT plan to unwind the remaining £368 billion of monetary-policy gilts at an average pace of £46 billion per year through 2034, with only £20 billion of annual active sales. The Bank estimates QT itself explains only about 20–30bp of the roughly 200bp rise in UK long-term term premia since 2022, and said it wants Bank Rate to remain the active policy instrument while making QT more predictable.

This points to a two-instrument architecture: the short rate to control inflation, and balance-sheet policy to avoid unnecessary amplification of long-duration financing stress. That matters enormously for an AI build-out increasingly dependent on long-dated debt, infrastructure finance and private credit — tight enough at the front end to prevent AI-capital inflation from becoming generalized, but not so destabilizing at the long end that infrastructure capital formation collapses.
signal: A more sophisticated reaction function than "AI raises productivity, therefore rates fall."

# Australia confirms the same mechanism
On 18 September, RBA Governor Michele Bullock told lawmakers that upside inflation risks were materialising and explicitly identified the AI boom as one of them. Her formulation: the global AI boom is producing stronger growth in economies central to the AI supply chain while simultaneously driving higher prices for supply-constrained AI technologies. Australia's own data-centre investment boom is adding to domestic demand as oil prices remain elevated.

Markets now assign roughly a 93% probability to another RBA hike at the 28–29 September meeting, from 4.35% to 4.60%.
signal: Two independent central banks, one mechanism — AI as a live inflation risk.

::section The Great Split Becomes a Monetary-Policy Split | one stance, two economies
The increasingly visible regime splits the economy in two. AI-capable economies and sectors see capital expenditure, employment demand in constrained skills, electricity and semiconductor demand, higher investment and higher productivity. Meanwhile non-AI, capital-sensitive sectors face higher interest rates, weaker investment, weaker hiring and margin pressure.

The same monetary stance can therefore be too loose for the AI economy and too tight for the Old Economy. This is the Great Split moving directly into monetary transmission — and it explains why employment data may become progressively harder to interpret. Aggregate employment could appear stable while AI infrastructure, energy and engineering employment rises and routine services, administrative and lower-productivity employment falls. Central banks will increasingly have to distinguish structural labour reallocation from cyclical weakness.

::section The ECB Now Has an Even Harder Problem | importing shocks before dividends
Euro-area consumer inflation expectations released on 18 September moved higher: one-year expectations rose to 3.0%, three-year to 2.9%, and five-year to 2.5%. The immediate cause is primarily the energy shock, not AI. But the timing matters because AI infrastructure is adding a second source of structural resource demand precisely as inflation expectations become less comfortable.

The European risk loop runs: energy shock plus AI infrastructure demand plus higher global capital costs → inflation expectations up → ECB tightening → European cost of capital up — while Europe still captures less AI productivity than the United States.

::pull THE DANGEROUS ASYMMETRY
Europe can import both the energy shock and the AI cost-of-capital shock before capturing the productivity dividend.

::patterns Investor & Policy Implications | where the hurdle rate becomes physical
# The Hurdle Rate Is Becoming Physical
The critical metric for AI investments is no longer simply revenue growth. It is AI return on invested capital greater than the AI-adjusted cost of capital. The best-positioned companies convert AI investment into cash flow rapidly enough to absorb higher yields, rising electricity costs, supply-constrained chips, grid connection costs and greater debt requirements.

# The Dangerous Category
High AI capex, weak free cash flow, long duration, external financing dependence and constrained power access. That combination increasingly sits directly in the path of tighter central-bank reaction functions.

# Energy Is Now Monetary Policy
Europe cannot treat AI, energy, capital-markets and monetary policy as separate silos. Insufficient power → AI infrastructure scarcity → higher marginal AI cost → inflation → higher rates → higher WACC → slower AI diffusion → weaker productivity.

# The Positive Loop
Energy abundance, grids, European capital markets and AI infrastructure → adoption → productivity → falling unit costs → lower structural inflation → greater monetary-policy space. This is why generation, grids, storage and the Savings and Investments Union are becoming one strategy.

::takeaways Key takeaways | the reaction function has changed
- The decisive change this week is not a 25bp move — it is the language of the reaction function.
- The Fed has linked AI capital formation to stronger investment; the BoE now names AI supply constraints as inflation risk inside a rate decision; the RBA says the AI boom is raising prices in supply-constrained technologies.
- The general equation: AI capital formation → compute, power, infrastructure and capital scarcity → inflation / higher R* → monetary tightening → selection by cost of capital → productivity diffusion → industrial AI deflation.
- The world is still predominantly in the upper half of that chain.
