The AI Supercycle has crossed a decisive institutional threshold: the Federal Reserve now explicitly identifies AI-driven investment, inflation, corporate borrowing and changes in the neutral interest rate within its monetary-policy deliberations.

The minutes of the September 15–16 FOMC meeting, released on October 7, provide the strongest evidence yet that the **Great Split** is becoming a central-bank policy problem. Three findings are particularly consequential: accelerating AI investment as a contributor to persistent inflation; substantial AI-related corporate borrowing linked to competition for capital and upward pressure on Treasury yields; and upward revisions by some policymakers to their estimates of the neutral federal funds rate.

::signals The policy machinery is shifting | five readings from the minutes

# The Fed's reaction function is changing
The September minutes reveal a significant distinction between AI's immediate inflationary effects and its prospective productivity benefits. Participants observed that AI infrastructure expansion was contributing to higher business costs and core-goods inflation, with some warning that investment demand could exceed productive capacity over the medium term. AI investment competes with other sectors for electricity, semiconductors, construction capacity and specialised labour; if those constraints persist, sectoral price increases can spread through the broader economy.

The Committee unanimously raised the target range by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00%, and most participants judged another increase likely appropriate before year-end, conditional on data. This does not establish that AI was the decisive cause of the September increase.
signal: The Fed cannot offset today's inflation with productivity gains that may not materialise for years.

# AI capital formation is repricing global capital
Nominal Treasury yields rose approximately 35 basis points across the two- to ten-year segment during the intermeeting period. The Fed's market review identified AI infrastructure financing among the factors contributing to higher term premiums and yields, alongside geopolitical developments, stronger data and policy expectations. Extraordinary AI investment is increasingly a determinant of the global demand for capital, not merely a driver of equity valuations.

The Fed also observed that equity valuations and relatively narrow credit spreads continued to support financial conditions — so the tightening transmitted through Treasury yields was partially offset by buoyant risk assets.
signal: The Supercycle is simultaneously raising the price of debt and supporting the value of equity.

# The neutral-rate question becomes consequential
Some participants reported upward revisions to their neutral-rate estimates. That does not demonstrate an AI-driven structural increase in r*, but it reinforces a central question: could extraordinary AI capital formation sustain a higher equilibrium real rate before Industrial-AI deflation becomes visible? Higher investment demand can lift equilibrium real rates, while productivity diffusion can expand capacity and moderate inflation — effects that are neither necessarily simultaneous nor mutually exclusive.
signal: The investment boom can change financial conditions long before its productivity benefits change measured performance.

# Japan provides the industrial counterpoint
The Bank of Japan's October 8 regional assessment notes strong global AI demand supporting Japanese investment in electronics, industrial machinery, telecommunications infrastructure and data centres. Yet Japanese businesses simultaneously face broader inflation from imported materials, energy, exchange-rate developments and labour costs. Japan shows how AI infrastructure demand can strengthen selected sectors while an economy confronts more general cost inflation.
signal: The challenge is distinguishing sustainable productivity-driven growth from demand that reinforces inflation persistence.

# The Great Split enters the labour market
Participants observed strong wage gains among specialised workers supporting AI infrastructure investment, even as aggregate wage growth remained relatively moderate. The Great Split is increasingly emerging between workers, occupations and regions benefiting immediately from capital formation and those awaiting productivity diffusion.
signal: A single unemployment rate may offer an increasingly incomplete picture of labour-market conditions during the transition.

::section What's next: the financial consequences | five implications for investors

The next stage of the AI Supercycle will be judged increasingly by the relationship between capital intensity, financing costs and realised productivity.

::patterns Five implications | position for a repriced world

# US Treasuries
Monitor whether extraordinary AI-related borrowing continues contributing to term premiums independently of policy-rate expectations.

# AI equities
Prioritise free cash flow, capital productivity and the timing of returns over headline capital-expenditure growth.

# Corporate credit
Distinguish internally financed AI expansion from business models requiring continuous access to increasingly expensive external capital.

# European markets
Watch whether US-driven increases in global long-term yields translate into disproportionate financing pressure on European investment.

# Central-bank policy
Treat changes in neutral-rate estimates, productivity assumptions and the transmission of long-term yields as consequential signals, not secondary adjustments.

::section The European consequence | an asymmetry exposed

For European policymakers, the Fed's findings expose an increasingly important asymmetry. The United States is generating extraordinary AI capital formation; Europe participates in the resulting global repricing of capital but captures a smaller proportion of the associated infrastructure investment. This risks an adverse transmission mechanism: higher global financing costs plus insufficient European AI investment equal weaker productivity diffusion and greater competitive divergence.

The response must extend beyond monetary policy. Capital-markets integration, energy infrastructure, investment in compute capacity, industrial adoption and workforce adaptation are becoming parts of the same challenge. The ECB cannot independently deliver the productivity gains needed to offset the inflationary costs of the transition — that requires a ==coordinated European capital-formation strategy==.

::takeaways Five key takeaways | where the regime is heading
- **Monetary policy:** The Fed is explicitly incorporating AI-related demand pressures into its assessment of inflation persistence.
- **Capital formation:** AI infrastructure financing is becoming relevant to the pricing of sovereign and corporate debt.
- **Neutral rates:** The possibility of structurally higher equilibrium rates deserves greater attention, though its relationship with AI remains uncertain.
- **The Great Split:** The divergence between capital formation and productivity diffusion increasingly extends into labour markets and monetary transmission.
- **Europe:** The strategic challenge is to convert European savings into productive AI investment before imported financing pressures further constrain competitiveness.

::close The Great Split has reached the central banks. The next economic regime will be determined by whether ==productivity can outrun the rising cost of capital==.
