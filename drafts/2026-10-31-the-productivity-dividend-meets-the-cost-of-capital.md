The Great Split Enters a New Phase: AI Adoption Is Accelerating, but Monetary Transmission, Financing Structures and Labour Markets Are Moving at Different Speeds
THE BIG TAKE — The most consequential development is not another interest-rate decision. It is new evidence that artificial intelligence is beginning to reshape monetary transmission itself.
On October 30, ECB Executive Board member Philip Lane presented an extensive assessment of AI adoption, financing and productivity at the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco.
His findings reveal something fundamental: AI-related activity is now contributing materially to European credit growth, but AI-intensive companies are also more sensitive to monetary tightening than comparable businesses.
Meanwhile, new Bank of England evidence shows that AI is already altering recruitment, professional-services pricing and corporate cost structures. At the Federal Reserve, Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan has explicitly called for at least two additional interest-rate increases, despite uncertainty about AI investment and its eventual returns.
Together, these developments expose a central contradiction.
The economy is becoming more productive in selected activities, but the capital required to distribute those gains remains expensive, unevenly allocated and increasingly sensitive to monetary policy.
This is a material new development in the Great Split thesis. 
FIVE SIGNALS THAT MATTER
0.9 percentage points: The ECB estimates that AI-related activity contributed approximately this amount to annual euro-area corporate credit growth in the first quarter of 2026—roughly one-quarter of aggregate growth at that time.
52%: The proportion of surveyed euro-area workers reporting AI use at work, up from 26% in 2024. This is self-reported adoption, not a measure of realised aggregate productivity.
Greater monetary sensitivity: ECB research finds that patenting among AI firms declines approximately 1% more following monetary tightening than among comparable non-AI firms.
A changing employment model: The Bank of England reports that AI-related adjustments are appearing particularly through lower replacement hiring, reduced trainee recruitment and changing professional-services pricing—not widespread layoffs.
At least 50 basis points: The additional Federal Reserve tightening advocated by Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan on October 30. This is her individual policy assessment, not a Federal Open Market Committee decision.
The evidence comes from publications and speeches dated October 30, 2026.

1. THE ECB: AI HAS BECOME A MEASURABLE MONETARY-TRANSMISSION VARIABLE
Philip Lane’s October 30 speech, AI Adoption in Europe, provides the strongest new empirical evidence.
Euro-area digital investment has increased by 73% in real terms since 2014, rising from approximately 2.3% to an estimated 3.3% of GDP.
Businesses participating in the ECB’s Corporate Telephone Survey report that AI has increased their capital-expenditure plans by approximately 10% this year.
The ECB also estimates that AI-related borrowing accounted for approximately one-quarter of annual aggregate corporate credit growth in the first quarter of 2026.
These findings establish that AI is no longer merely a prospective productivity shock.
It is already influencing investment demand, borrowing requirements and the transmission of monetary conditions to the real economy. 
THE HIDDEN PATTERN: MONETARY TIGHTENING CAN PENALISE THE COMPANIES BUILDING FUTURE PRODUCTIVITY
The ECB’s research identifies greater sensitivity of AI-related innovation to monetary tightening.
This finding matters because AI investment often requires substantial upfront expenditure before productive returns materialise.
If borrowing costs remain elevated, businesses may postpone investments that could eventually reduce unit production costs.
The resulting transmission mechanism is:HIGHER INTEREST RATES → HIGHER AI FINANCING COSTS → LOWER INNOVATION INVESTMENT → DELAYED PRODUCTIVITY GAINS → WEAKER FUTURE DISINFLATIONARY CAPACITY.
This is a potential second-order consequence of monetary restraint, not a reason for central banks to abandon inflation targets.
It nevertheless changes how policymakers should assess the longer-term costs of tightening.
Restrictive monetary policy can reduce inflationary demand today while also delaying the productive capacity required to contain inflation tomorrow.
The new ECB evidence makes that trade-off more measurable.
2. EUROPE’S CAPITAL PARADOX: FINANCING AMERICAN AI WHILE CONSTRAINING EUROPEAN ADOPTION
A second finding deserves particular attention from investors and EU policymakers.
Lane confirms that European investors are significant purchasers of American technology equities and euro-denominated bonds issued by US companies.
Purchases of these corporate bonds have increased alongside declining European purchases of US Treasury securities.
This does not prove that investors are directly selling Treasuries to purchase hyperscaler bonds. But it demonstrates a meaningful change in the composition of European international investment flows.
The ECB also finds that financing remains particularly difficult for intangible investments, including training and organisational capabilities that cannot readily serve as loan collateral.
European banks are generally better equipped to finance tangible infrastructure than the less collateral-intensive activities necessary for widespread AI adoption. 
THE SECOND-ORDER EFFECT: CAPITAL FORMATION WITHOUT DOMESTIC PRODUCTIVITY FORMATION
The mechanism is potentially self-reinforcing.
European savings help finance American technological infrastructure.
American technology providers expand their platforms and computing capacity.
European businesses purchase access to those capabilities.
Yet smaller European companies may lack sufficient funding for the organisational transformation required to use them productively.
Europe can become an increasingly sophisticated consumer and financier of intelligence without capturing a proportionate share of the economic returns generated by producing it.
The ECB currently finds only limited evidence of direct crowding-out in European corporate financing.
That qualification is important.
Nevertheless, the emerging pattern creates a strategic capital-allocation question.
For Europe, the principal risk is not international diversification itself.
It is insufficient domestic investment in the capabilities needed to transform imported intelligence into productivity, intellectual property, competitive differentiation and sustainable earnings.
3. THE BANK OF ENGLAND: INDUSTRIAL-AI DEFLATION IS MORE COMPLICATED THAN LOWER LABOUR COSTS
The Bank of England’s October 30 Agents’ Summary provides an important counterpoint.
Businesses increasingly report measurable productivity improvements from AI, particularly in professional services.
But the transmission to prices is not straightforward.
Some firms are responding to efficiency improvements by changing their pricing models rather than automatically reducing prices.
Value-based pricing is becoming more common, while customers increasingly demand a share of productivity savings.
Meanwhile, labour-cost savings are being partially offset by higher expenditure on cloud computing, software, cybersecurity, data infrastructure and governance.
The employment effects are emerging predominantly through reduced recruitment and trainee intakes rather than widespread workforce reductions. 
THE CRITICAL DISTINCTION: LOWER UNIT COSTS DO NOT AUTOMATICALLY PRODUCE LOWER PRICES
Three outcomes are possible.
Businesses may pass productivity gains to customers through lower prices.
They may retain those gains through higher operating margins.
Or the savings may be absorbed by additional technology expenditure and organisational restructuring.
Consequently, AI-driven improvements in individual business processes do not necessarily produce immediate economy-wide disinflation.The Bank of England’s findings also identify a potential labour-market consequence.
If businesses reduce entry-level recruitment while retaining experienced employees, aggregate unemployment may initially remain relatively stable even as opportunities for younger workers deteriorate.
The first significant employment effect of AI may be fewer jobs created, rather than more existing jobs destroyed.
This creates a measurement problem for central banks.
Traditional employment indicators may underestimate the structural adjustment taking place beneath relatively stable headline labour-market statistics.
4. THE FEDERAL RESERVE: THE NEUTRAL-RATE DEBATE BECOMES MORE CONSEQUENTIAL
Lorie Logan’s October 30 intervention adds a further dimension.
She argues that US monetary policy remains insufficiently restrictive and that at least two additional quarter-point increases are required under her current outlook.
She also observes that longer-term Treasury yields have increased substantially during 2026, reflecting, in her assessment, expectations of stronger nominal growth and higher neutral interest rates.
Importantly, she acknowledges that the investment outlook could strengthen or weaken as businesses learn more about AI’s commercial applications.
The Federal Reserve is therefore confronting uncertainty over both the immediate inflationary effects of investment and the sustainable growth rate of the economy. 
This creates an important distinction between the Fed and ECB.
America’s stronger investment cycle may support expectations of higher equilibrium interest rates.
Europe’s weaker capital-market integration and more uneven investment diffusion could produce a different outcome.
However, neither central bank has established a definitive AI-specific revision to its neutral-rate estimate.
Higher market yields should not automatically be interpreted as proof that the equilibrium real interest rate has permanently increased.
THE ENERGY CONSTRAINT
A separate October 30 Dallas Fed report documents competition between data-centre developers and energy producers for labour, equipment, infrastructure, water and other resources in the Permian Basin.
This provides concrete regional evidence of the inflationary demand pressures associated with AI infrastructure construction. 
The monetary implication is straightforward.
Building the AI economy can raise resource costs before operating the AI economy reduces production costs.
That timing difference is becoming central to the next phase of monetary-policy analysis.
5. FINANCIAL STABILITY: AI ADOPTION IS ADVANCING FASTER THAN SUPERVISORY CERTAINTY
The Bank of England and Financial Conduct Authority’s AI survey, published October 26, provides additional context that should not be overlooked.
Among responding financial institutions, 86% report using AI, up from 75% in 2024.
Approximately 80% report having deployed AI agents, although only 2% report agents with broad autonomy.
More consequentially, third-party implementations now account for 53% of reported high-materiality AI systems.
The three largest cloud and computing providers account for 79% of reported provider relationships.
These are survey findings, not evidence of a current systemic incident. 
The supervisory implication is significant.
Financial institutions may become more operationally efficient while simultaneously increasing their dependence on concentrated external technology infrastructure.
That creates potential channels for correlated operational failures, cyber incidents and liquidity disruption.
The ECB’s October 30 speech similarly documents rising cybersecurity and IT incidents within banking operational-risk events.
The next financial-stability challenge is not simply the amount of credit financing AI. It is the interaction between that credit, shared technology dependencies and the speed of automated financial decisions.

THE GREAT SPLIT — FIVE STRATEGIC TAKEAWAYS
For monetary policymakers: Distinguish AI-driven investment demand from realised productivity growth.The two can have opposite inflationary implications, particularly when their timing differs.
For European investors: Evaluate AI exposure through sustainable returns on invested capital, cash-flow generation, financing sensitivity and the extent to which productivity gains are retained in margins or passed to customers.
For EU policymakers: Address the financing gap for intangible investment. Training, organisational transformation and proprietary applications are essential to productivity diffusion, but conventional collateral-based lending is poorly suited to many of these activities.
For banking supervisors: Intensify monitoring of AI-related credit exposures, common cloud dependencies, third-party concentration and operational resilience.
For European governments: Treat competitive electricity, deeper capital markets and widespread Industrial-AI adoption as complementary components of economic competitiveness rather than separate policy initiatives.
THE FINAL TAKE — EUROPE MUST FINANCE THE PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND, NOT MERELY THE INFRASTRUCTURE
The October 30 developments materially strengthen the Great Split thesis.
The ECB now provides evidence that AI-related investment is influencing credit growth and monetary transmission.
The Bank of England confirms that AI is changing employment, operating costs and pricing behaviour before the aggregate consequences become fully visible.
The Federal Reserve is confronting the possibility that stronger investment and productivity may alter the equilibrium interest-rate environment.
These findings suggest that the next major economic divergence will be determined by three factors.
The ability to finance technological adoption.
The ability to convert adoption into sustained productivity.
And the ability to maintain monetary and financial stability throughout that transition.
Europe’s challenge is particularly consequential because its financial system is still better equipped to finance tangible assets than the intangible capabilities on which much of the AI productivity dividend depends.
The objective should not be to shield technological investment from monetary discipline.
It should be to build capital markets capable of financing productive transformation across the interest-rate cycle.
VELOCITY INSIGHT
The defining competitive divide of the AI Supercycle will separate economies in which capital finances productivity from those in which the cost and allocation of capital prevent productivity from emerging.
