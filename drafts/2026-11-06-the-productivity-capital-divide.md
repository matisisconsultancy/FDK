US Productivity Accelerates as the AI Selloff Spreads from Equities to Credit—and Higher Interest Rates Raise the Investment Hurdle
Where Markets, Capital and Geopolitics Collide
Evidence cut-off: November 6, 2026 | 04:45 CET
THE BIG TAKE
The most consequential development in the Global Velocity Index is the widening divergence between measurable productivity growth and the financial capacity to sustain technological investment.
The latest evidence reveals four developments that investors should examine together.
US labour productivity accelerated to an annualised 3.6% in the third quarter, while unit labour costs declined. This is encouraging evidence of improving economic efficiency, although the contribution of artificial intelligence cannot yet be isolated.
At the same time, the Nasdaq fell 5.52% on November 5, with semiconductor shares experiencing substantially larger losses. The correction spread into Asian technology markets, triggering a trading halt in South Korea.
More consequentially, financial stress is beginning to appear in credit markets. SoftBank’s five-year credit-default-swap spread reached its highest level in four years.
Meanwhile, the Bank of England increased interest rates to 4%, reinforcing the pressure that inflation and financing costs place on long-duration investment.
The emerging investment regime combines stronger evidence of productivity with a more demanding price of capital.
The distinction is fundamental.
Technological progress may continue accelerating even as valuations decline, financing becomes more selective and expected returns on investment are reassessed.
For investors, the next opportunity lies in distinguishing companies capable of converting productivity into internally financed growth from those whose expansion depends on increasingly expensive external capital.

II. THE FOUR DEVELOPMENTS THAT MATTER
1. UNITED STATES — THE PRODUCTIVITY DIVIDEND BECOMES MEASURABLE
The US Bureau of Labor Statistics reported November 5 that third-quarter nonfarm business productivity increased 3.6% at an annualised rate, compared with a revised 2.1% in the second quarter.
Output increased 3.7%, while hours worked increased only 0.1%.
Unit labour costs declined 0.7%, compared with an increase of 1.9% in the preceding quarter.
This is the strongest new structural evidence in today’s assessment. 
The implications extend beyond inflation.
Higher productivity can support corporate margins, improve the economics of investment and create additional capacity for earnings growth.
However, two qualifications matter.
First, quarterly productivity estimates are volatile and subject to revision.
Second, stronger productivity does not establish that AI caused the improvement.The evidence nevertheless strengthens the case for monitoring actual economic output per hour rather than assuming that technological investment automatically generates productivity gains.
The BLS also reported that labour’s share of nonfarm business output reached 53.0%, the lowest in its series beginning in 1947.
This adds an important distributional dimension: productivity improvements do not necessarily translate proportionately into labour compensation. 
GVI implication: Material positive Enterprise Transformation evidence for the United States. A numerical structural upgrade remains conditional on persistence and consistent calibration.
2. GLOBAL MARKETS — THE CORRECTION ENTERS THE FINANCING CHANNEL
November 5 marked a significant deterioration in market conditions.
The Nasdaq fell 5.52%, confirming a correction from its October peak.
The S&P 500 declined 4.36%, while semiconductor shares experienced substantially larger losses.
Investors were responding to concerns about the profitability, financing and potential regulation of AI infrastructure following the US midterm elections.
Those concerns should be distinguished from enacted policy changes. Market expectations are not evidence that nationwide restrictions on data-centre construction have taken effect. 
The more important development emerged overnight.
On November 6, SoftBank’s five-year credit-default-swap spread increased 22 basis points to 466 basis points, its highest level since October 2022.
Credit protection becoming more expensive signals an increase in market-perceived credit risk. It does not mean default is imminent.
SoftBank represents a particularly relevant case because of its exposure to major AI investments and substantial financing commitments. 
This suggests a potential second-order transmission:
EQUITY REPRICING → CREDIT-RISK PREMIUMS → HIGHER FINANCING HURDLES → MORE SELECTIVE CAPITAL EXPENDITURE
Evidence of the first two stages is now observable in selected companies.
A broad reduction in AI infrastructure investment has not yet been established.
That distinction should govern today’s GVI assessment.
3. UNITED KINGDOM — THE COST OF CAPITAL RISES AGAIN
On November 5, the Bank of England increased its policy rate from 3.75% to 4.00%.
The Monetary Policy Committee voted 8–1 in favour of the increase, with one member preferring a larger rise.
The Bank expects UK inflation to approach 5% by the middle of 2027, reflecting persistent energy-price pressures.
The inflation projection is a forecast rather than a realised outcome. 
The investment implications are direct.
Higher borrowing costs raise the required returns on infrastructure, industrial investment and corporate expansion.
This is especially consequential for projects with substantial upfront expenditure and cash flows extending far into the future.
GVI implication: Negative Smart Capital policy signal for the United Kingdom. The 25-basis-point change qualifies for the monitoring register, but cannot be converted into an unsupported numerical national-score adjustment.
4. GERMANY — INDUSTRIAL ORDERS RECOVER, BUT THE TREND REMAINS MODEST
Germany’s Federal Statistical Office reported that manufacturing orders increased 4.4% in September, following a revised 10.1% contraction in August.
Automotive orders increased 10.5%, while machinery and equipment orders rose 8.3%.
However, the three-month comparison tells a more restrained story.
Total orders increased only 0.2% in the third quarter compared with the second quarter.
The September rebound therefore provides evidence of recovering demand, but not yet of sustained industrial acceleration. 
GVI implication: Positive near-term industrial momentum for Germany, with moderate structural confidence.

III. THE AI INFRASTRUCTURE FINANCING TEST
The latest corporate developments reinforce the financial implications.
On November 5, AI infrastructure developer IREN reported delays affecting some Microsoft-related infrastructure deliveries.The company attributed these to network-design changes and supplier timing, indicating that the delays would not trigger financial penalties.
Nevertheless, its timetable for achieving more than $4 billion in annualised run-rate revenue shifted from year-end toward January, potentially extending into February.
IREN also outlined approximately $15 billion in additional planned capital expenditure.
The spending figure is a forward-looking commitment, not completed investment. 
This development illustrates an increasingly important distinction.
The financial viability of AI infrastructure depends not only on contracted demand, but also on deployment schedules, financing costs, utilisation and the timing of cash generation.
Delays can influence financial returns even when customer demand remains strong.
For investors, operational execution and capital structure are becoming central to the evaluation of AI infrastructure companies.

VII. SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS — THE NEXT FINANCIAL REGIME
The most important development is the potential interaction between productivity growth and capital-market selectivity.
The United States is demonstrating stronger measured productivity.
But the financing conditions surrounding AI infrastructure are becoming more demanding.
The United Kingdom is raising interest rates.
German industrial activity is recovering unevenly.
Asian semiconductor markets are experiencing significant financial volatility.
These developments suggest three potential consequences.
First, strong balance sheets may become a more valuable competitive advantage.
Companies capable of financing investment from operating cash flow are less directly exposed to changes in external funding conditions.
Second, infrastructure investment may become more concentrated.
Higher financing hurdles could favour developers with contracted demand, available electricity, secure construction schedules and credible utilisation assumptions.
Third, capital allocation may begin moving toward companies demonstrating realised productivity benefits.
Industrial automation, enterprise software and selected service businesses could become increasingly interesting where AI adoption produces measurable improvements in margins and asset productivity.
These are potential investment transmissions, not confirmed reallocations.
The decisive sequence remains:
PRODUCTIVITY → EARNINGS → FREE CASH FLOW → REINVESTMENT → COMPOUNDING RETURNS
For capital-intensive businesses, that sequence must generate returns exceeding the full cost of capital.

VIII. FIVE TAKEAWAYS FOR INVESTORS AND BOARDS
1. Investors — Separate the productivity dividend from the AI valuation cycle. Stronger US productivity is positive macroeconomic evidence. It does not automatically justify current valuations across every AI-related asset.
2. Global capital allocators — Monitor credit, not just equities. SoftBank’s widening CDS spread highlights the importance of financing conditions as a potential transmission channel for technology-market stress.
3. Boards of directors — Reinforce capital discipline. Large infrastructure programmes should be evaluated against realistic deployment schedules, funding requirements, cash-flow milestones and incremental ROIC.
4. Corporate executives — Prioritise measurable productivity. The most important AI investment metric is increasingly the improvement in economic output relative to the full resources required to produce it.
5. Policymakers — Preserve productive investment. Inflation control, reliable energy systems and access to long-term capital remain essential for converting technological capabilities into sustained productivity growth.

IX. FINANCIAL VERDICT
Main accelerator: Stronger measured US productivity and signs of recovering German industrial demand.Main decelerator: Higher UK interest rates and deteriorating financing sentiment across selected AI-related equities and credit instruments.
Principal second-order risk: A sustained repricing of credit that raises financing costs and delays otherwise productive infrastructure investment.
Principal opportunity: Companies capable of converting productivity improvements into durable cash flow while maintaining strong balance sheets and disciplined capital expenditure.
THE BIG TAKE — WHAT COMES NEXT
November 6 provides a clearer distinction between technological potential, realised productivity and financial value creation.
The evidence of productivity growth is improving.
But investors are becoming more demanding about the capital required to sustain expansion.
The next phase will test whether recent financial-market stress remains principally a valuation adjustment or begins affecting the financing and deployment of productive assets.
That distinction will be critical to the evolution of the Global Velocity Index—and to the next major rotation in capital markets.
VELOCITY INSIGHT
The next investment premium will belong to companies capable of converting productivity into free cash flow faster than financing costs absorb the resulting gains.
Technological acceleration creates value. Capital productivity determines who captures it.
