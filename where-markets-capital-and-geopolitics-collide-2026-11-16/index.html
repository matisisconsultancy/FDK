Where Markets, Capital and Geopolitics Collide

The world is accelerating its investment in the future while the economy that must pay for it is losing momentum.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann November 16, 2026 5 min read Macro

−0.1%Japan Q3 GDP, annualized

“The ultimate advantage will not be the power to build the future — it will be the power to make it economically sustainable.”

The signal1 min
  1. China's October data disappointed: industrial production rose 4.8%, retail sales just 0.4%, fixed investment fell 8.1% and property investment dropped 21.6%.
  2. Japan unexpectedly contracted at a 0.1% annualized rate as consumption fell 0.3% while corporate capex rose 1.6%.
  3. AI capital keeps surging: Nvidia is expected to report ~$109bn in quarterly revenue, with Anthropic's IPO testing frontier-AI appetite.
  4. Financing stays demanding: the US 10-year yield finished near 5.14%, the dollar is strong and markets expect a December Fed hike.
  5. Energy is loosening: the IEA now sees a ~1.7m bpd Q4 surplus, with Brent near $95 despite Hormuz risk.
  6. The decisive test is whether AI deployment economics can improve faster than the cost of capital, energy and infrastructure.

The most consequential development in global markets this morning is the widening divergence between extraordinary technological investment and weakening underlying economic demand.

China's October releases are the clearest warning, Japan has unexpectedly contracted, and yet the companies building the Next Economy keep mobilizing extraordinary capital. The critical question is no longer whether AI is losing momentum — it is whether technological acceleration can generate enough economic value to justify its growing claims on global capital.

01

The Central Thesis

capital vs. monetization

The Next Economy is entering a phase in which the ability to mobilize capital matters less than the ability to monetize the productive capacity that capital creates.

This is the next evolution of The Great Split. The first divide concerned access to technology; the second, computing and infrastructure; the third, financing. The emerging divide concerns demand, productivity and economic profit: who builds the Next Economy, who can afford to use it, and who ultimately captures its returns.

02

Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy

the week's tests

  1. China's industrial growth slowed from 5.5% in September to 4.8% in October, even as high-technology exports still support manufacturing. Japan shows a parallel split: contracting consumption alongside resilient AI investment. signal: Differentiate Asian semiconductor and automation leaders from businesses dependent on domestic consumption and property.

  2. Nvidia reports on November 17 under interim CEO Colette Kress, with analysts expecting ~$109bn in revenue and ~90% earnings growth — forecasts, not results. Its valuation has fallen from ~$5.8tn in early October to ~$3.9tn Friday. Anthropic's expected November 19 IPO is a second test of investor appetite. signal: The next AI premium will reward turning computing demand into financial independence and durable profit.

  3. SK Hynix announced a ₩1.4 trillion ($1.05bn) five-year programme to support domestic suppliers, plus a ~₩874.6bn Trinity Fab testing facility at Yongin opening May 2027. The next competition concerns the whole ecosystem — materials, packaging, equipment — not advanced chips alone. signal: Opportunity may increasingly sit in the ecosystem around accelerated computing, not processors alone.

  4. The IEA expects Q4 supply to exceed demand by ~1.7m bpd; Brent traded near $95.30 as China refinery throughput fell 8.1%. A Trump–Xi–Putin meeting is planned in Shenzhen on November 19. But a commodity correction does not automatically lower financing costs. signal: Reliable electricity and energy productivity matter more long-term than single-session crude moves.

  5. European banks are expected to issue ~$35bn in significant risk transfers in 2026, releasing capital but deepening dependence on specialized credit funds. The ECB's Financial Stability Review lands November 18. Siemens, meanwhile, reported record €108.2bn orders and €12.1bn free cash flow. signal: Separate banks with strong capital generation from those dependent on costly risk transfers.

03

Four Hidden Patterns

beyond the headlines

01

The AI boom and the consumer economy move at different speeds

Technological investment can stay strong as household spending weakens — sustainable only if productivity eventually lifts wages and purchasing power.

02

The next scarcity may be sustainable demand, not computing capacity

Computing determines possibilities; utilization, pricing, depreciation and capital costs determine value.

03

The bond market may become the arbiter of ambition

Higher sovereign yields raise required returns. Cash-rich tech retains flexibility; leveraged operators must prove their commitments generate reliable revenue.

04

The Great Split becomes capital formation vs. economic absorption

Accelerated computing faces a demand, utilization and productivity test — value-creating acceleration on one side, capital-intensive divergence on the other.

04

Three Rotations to Anticipate

where capital may move

From AI infrastructure expansion toward AI financial productivity. From technological scarcity toward industrial efficiency and measurable returns. From broad geographic exposure toward differentiated sovereign financing and resilience.

The central three-month scenario (45%) is selective productivity reallocation — AI demand robust while capital redirects toward cash generation — not a technology collapse. A demand–capital squeeze carries 35%; a broad AI productivity acceleration, 20%.

Five Takeaways

for investors, boards and policymakers

  • Equity investors: the next valuation premium must be earned through economic profit, not capacity.
  • Credit investors: scrutinize contractual revenue, concentration, debt service and infrastructure economics over headline announcements.
  • Corporate boards: match technological investment with commercial absorption and returns above the cost of capital.
  • European policymakers: financial integration must produce productive investment, not merely more intermediation.
  • Global investors: the Great Split is becoming a divide between the capacity to produce the future and the capacity to profit from it.

The world is building the future faster than it is creating the economic demand required to pay for it — and the next great rotation will follow the companies and economies that can connect capacity, capital, demand and productivity.
