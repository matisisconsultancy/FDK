Where Markets, Capital and Geopolitics Collide

Oil is collapsing, bond yields are falling and global equities are recovering — yet the AI supply chain is selling off, and the next great rotation of capital has already begun.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann November 5, 2026 5 min read Markets
$94/bblBrent crude, lowest in ~2 months (−20% on the week)
4.85%US 10-year Treasury yield
−6%South Korea KOSPI, intraday
+1.6%S&P 500, November 4

“Capital is becoming cheaper while the market becomes more demanding about what capital must earn.”

The signal1 min
  1. Oil has collapsed roughly 20% this week, Brent near $94, while the US 10-year yield fell toward 4.85%, its lowest in two months.
  2. Yet the AI supply chain is selling off: South Korea's KOSPI down nearly 6%, Taiwan tech off more than 3%, Nasdaq futures around −1%.
  3. The inversion: cheaper capital no longer sustains valuations if expected returns on that capital deteriorate.
  4. The catalyst is political: the Democrats' November 3 win shifts the risk premium from energy toward AI regulation and data-centre scrutiny.
  5. The next rotation favours capital productivity and monetization over undifferentiated infrastructure expansion.
  6. Europe diverges: headline inflation at 4.0%, yet Commerzbank, Toyota and ArcelorMittal all show corporate resilience.
Read the full note ↓

The most consequential development this morning is not the extraordinary reversal in oil prices following the US midterm elections. It is the widening divergence between improving global financial conditions and deteriorating valuations across critical parts of the AI infrastructure economy.

The market is rewarding the possibility of lower inflation and reduced geopolitical risk while simultaneously repricing the capital intensity, regulatory exposure and future profitability of the AI Supercycle. That is the central inversion: an improvement in the discount rate no longer protects valuations if expected future cash flows deteriorate or execution risks increase.

Our central thesis: the next great rotation will move capital away from undifferentiated technological expansion and toward businesses capable of combining productivity, financial resilience, energy efficiency and demonstrable economic returns.

01

Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy

the two-speed repricing

  1. The Democrats' victory has driven Brent down, bonds up and broad equities higher on expectations of de-escalation with Iran. Simultaneously, opposition to large data-centre projects in Texas and Georgia has pushed Asian infrastructure suppliers lower. Two risk premiums are moving in opposite directions: the geopolitical energy premium is falling while the regulatory and execution premium on AI infrastructure is rising. These are expectations, not enacted policy — President Trump retains veto power and the Iran conflict is unresolved. signal: The next infrastructure premium will reflect certainty of utilization and execution, not installed or announced capacity.

  2. Korea, Taiwan and Japan's Nikkei weakened sharply, yet at Hong Kong's Global Financial Leaders' Summit executives confirmed strong near-term demand for computing. A decline in semiconductor valuations does not imply a decline in demand; it reflects reassessment of future capital productivity, profitability and deployment timing. Investors are repricing expected returns from the AI cycle more aggressively than they are rewarding lower sovereign yields. signal: Evaluate the correction as a reassessment of future capital productivity — not evidence the Supercycle is ending.

  3. Brent near $94 reflects reduced escalation expectations, but Iran remains unresolved and inventories are tight. Eurozone inflation rose to 4.0% in October, with energy up 20.8% and core at 2.5%; the Bank of England is expected to raise rates from 3.75% to 4.00% today on a 7–2 vote. Markets discount a disinflationary future while central banks still respond to yesterday's shock. signal: Opportunities may emerge in energy-sensitive businesses with operating leverage — provided the price decline proves durable.

  4. Inflation at 4.0%, French sovereign pressure and a looming ECB leadership transition coexist with corporate strength: Commerzbank Q3 profit +45% to €859m, ArcelorMittal EBITDA $2.02bn, Deutsche Telekom raising guidance with a €2bn buyback. Europe should not be treated as a single exposure to sovereign risk. signal: Europe's next winners will be selected by corporate capital productivity and financial resilience, not national growth alone.

  5. Toyota lifted quarterly operating profit 34% to ¥1.13tn, raised its annual forecast to ¥3.9tn and rose more than 14%; Aumovio improved its margin to 3.5% despite weak demand. The lesson: technological ambition creates advantage only when it improves the economics of the business. signal: The next wave of value may migrate from building infrastructure toward improving the productivity of the physical economy.

02

Beyond the Headlines

four hidden patterns

01

Cost of capital ≠ return on capital

Lower yields improve financing, but the AI correction shows investors examining expected cash flows independently of the discount rate. The next valuation hierarchy turns on the credibility of incremental earnings.

02

Political risk migrates through the chain

Constraints on future data-centre capacity can reprice semiconductors, equipment, utilities and lenders — changing the distribution of returns without destroying the technology's value.

03

China's alternative capital model

Hong Kong recorded 117 IPOs raising ~$51bn in 2026 with 600+ awaiting listing. If value migrates from infrastructure to industrial deployment, China's manufacturing scale becomes an advantage.

04

Acceleration vs. monetization

The defining divide is no longer leaders versus laggards, but those financing technological acceleration and those capturing its economic returns.

Key Takeaways

for investors, boards and policymakers

  • Equity investors: follow earnings quality, not the direction of bond yields alone.
  • Credit investors: distinguish financing relief from improving project economics.
  • Corporate boards: the next competitive advantage is capital productivity.
  • European policymakers: energy relief is an opportunity, not a solution.
  • Global investors: the Great Split is becoming a divide between technological capacity and economic value capture.

The next winners will not necessarily be those accelerating the fastest — they will be those capable of converting acceleration into superior financial returns. In the Next Economy, technological velocity creates opportunity; capital productivity determines economic power.
