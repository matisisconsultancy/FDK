Where Markets, Capital and Geopolitics Collide

The Great Split has moved from financial markets into the real economy, where AI acceleration is concentrating growth, capital and competitive advantage.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann November 1, 2026 5 min read Macro

“Technology determines what becomes possible. Capital productivity determines who ultimately wins.”

The signal1 min
  1. South Korea's exports surged 93.7% in October to a record $115.28bn, with semiconductor shipments up 259.4% on AI demand.
  2. China's manufacturing PMI fell to 49.3 and non-manufacturing to 49.7, signalling industrial contraction and weak domestic demand.
  3. The Nasdaq 100 lost over 5.8% in October, erasing roughly $2 trillion as investors questioned AI capital productivity.
  4. Fitch cut France's outlook to negative on October 30, affirming A+ but warning of debt nearing 124% of GDP by 2028.
  5. Germany entered November at 61.35% gas storage, well below its 80% target, with January Brent at $113.43 a barrel.
  6. The decisive shift is from technological capacity toward capital productivity — strong growth no longer guarantees strong returns.
Read the full note ↓

The most consequential development this morning is not the correction in technology valuations. It is the increasingly visible separation between those parts of the global economy accelerating through artificial intelligence and those struggling to finance, absorb or compete with that acceleration.

Our central thesis: the AI Supercycle remains structurally expansionary, but its financial and industrial benefits are becoming increasingly selective. Capital is migrating toward the intersection of technological scarcity, financial resilience and productive infrastructure. The Great Split is no longer a valuation phenomenon — it is becoming an economic development trajectory.

01

Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy

technology, capital, sovereigns, energy, infrastructure

  1. South Korea's semiconductor exports reached $56.44bn, computer shipments rose 496.6%, and shipments to China and the US jumped 133.6% and 185.8% — aided by a favourable working-day comparison. China's PMI, meanwhile, fell below expansion with new orders contracting. The AI Supercycle can accelerate one country's scarce technological industries while leaving another's broader cycle weak, even when deeply interconnected. signal: Differentiate within Asia — follow economic rents and pricing power, not aggregate export narratives.

  2. Amazon's trailing twelve-month free cash flow reached negative $42.4bn even as cloud revenue grows; Microsoft generated $18.6bn in quarterly free cash flow. Reuters cites Morgan Stanley estimates of AI infrastructure spend exceeding $1 trillion in 2027. The first phase rewarded capacity accumulation; the next rewards utilization, monetization and incremental returns on capital. signal: Remain constructive on the Supercycle, but prioritise the productivity of capital over the scale of capex.

  3. Fitch projects French debt approaching 124% of GDP by 2028, from 115.7% in 2025. Eurozone Q3 GDP grew 0.4%, but France contracted 0.1%, Italy stagnated, Germany grew 0.2% and Spain expanded 0.6%. A net 9% of eurozone banks reported tighter credit standards — the worst deterioration in France. Sovereign risk can transmit to bank funding, corporate credit and industrial investment. signal: Treat European sovereigns individually; the next divide depends on the relative cost of capital.

  4. German storage sits at 61.35% against an 80% target, prompting INES to call for stronger mechanisms, though Berlin expects no shortage. January Brent settled at $113.43 (the expiring December contract at $117.64). As computing grows more electricity-intensive, energy security becomes a financial variable, not merely an industrial-policy objective. signal: The advantage of energy-secure economies is likely to increase as the Supercycle becomes more power-hungry.

  5. Completing the trans-European high-speed rail network by 2040 requires roughly €345bn, with the Commission developing private-financing structures. Spain's October 28 Urda tragedy — with preliminary findings pointing to signalling and traffic-management failures — sharpens attention on reliability and lifecycle performance. signal: The next mobility cycle will reward infrastructure quality, resilience and operating performance, not expansion alone.

The next economy may expand rapidly while distributing its economic rewards increasingly unequally.

02

Where Capital May Move Next

the emerging allocation map

Potential relative beneficiaries include advanced memory and semiconductor manufacturing, cash-generative AI platforms, enterprise AI demonstrating productivity gains, financially resilient sovereigns, grid modernization and intelligent rail. Areas facing greater pressure include undifferentiated manufacturing, debt-dependent AI expansion, AI investment without measurable returns, fiscally fragile sovereigns and energy-intensive manufacturing without pricing power.

The next rotation is driven by three shifts: from technological capacity toward capital productivity; from sovereign familiarity toward financing credibility; and from infrastructure expansion toward infrastructure performance. It is no longer sufficient to identify the fastest-growing industries — investors must identify who retains the greatest share of the value that growth creates.

03

Three Scenarios for the Next Phase

illustrative three-month probabilities

50%

Selective Acceleration

AI demand stays strong while capital increasingly favours financial resilience — driving rotation toward technological scarcity and productivity. The central scenario.

30%

Financial Fragmentation

Sovereign, energy and credit pressures constrain investment simultaneously, widening spreads and weakening capital-intensive sectors.

20%

Productivity Breakthrough

AI deployment delivers faster improvements in operating returns, broadening recovery across enterprise technology and industrial automation.

Key Takeaways

for investors, boards and policymakers

  • Equity investors: follow where economic rents accumulate — scarcity, pricing power and capital productivity decide leadership.
  • Credit investors: sovereign differentiation is a structural risk; France requires particular attention.
  • Corporate boards: the next advantage is integrating technology, productivity, balance-sheet strength and cash generation.
  • European policymakers: energy security and capital-market integration are now central to industrial competitiveness.
  • Infrastructure investors: the next cycle rewards quality of assets — reliable performance, safety and lifecycle economics — not quantity.

The Great Split is no longer simply about who controls the technologies of tomorrow — it is about who possesses the capital, energy and productive capacity to turn those technologies into economic power.
