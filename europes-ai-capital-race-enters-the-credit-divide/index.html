Europe’s AI Capital Race Enters the Credit Divide

Investors are beginning to distinguish between industrial infrastructure supported by productive demand and highly leveraged computing businesses whose expansion depends on continued access to credit.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann October 19, 2026 7 min read AI Infrastructure

Three consequential developments since October 18 reveal an accelerating divergence in Europe's AI infrastructure race. Germany is mobilizing institutional capital for energy and digital infrastructure, Siemens is advancing its Industrial AI strategy, and Spain is confronting the financial risks of concentrated AI-compute customers.

The Big Take: Europe's AI Supercycle is entering a more selective phase of capital formation. Investors are beginning to distinguish between industrial infrastructure supported by productive demand and highly leveraged computing businesses whose expansion depends on continued access to credit.

The fundamental question is shifting: Which European AI ecosystems can convert infrastructure spending into recurring industrial cash flow without accumulating disproportionate financial risk?

01

Spain — AI Credit Risk Reaches European Infrastructure

CoreWeave's financial stress exposes Merlin Properties' customer concentration

On October 19, Spanish financial newspaper Cinco Días reported that CoreWeave accounts for approximately 90% of Merlin Properties' current data-center rental revenues.

The exposure represents approximately 8.6% of Merlin's total rental income.

CoreWeave's deteriorating credit-market indicators have raised concerns about the sustainability of heavily leveraged AI infrastructure expansion. However, market-implied default probabilities are estimates of risk, not evidence that a default has occurred.

Merlin has defended its position, emphasizing contractual protections, the high utilization of CoreWeave's leased capacity and its intention to diversify future customers.

Its development programme targets approximately 724 MW of IT capacity by 2032, although not all phases are fully financed.

The hidden pattern. The financial risk of the AI Supercycle is beginning to migrate from computing operators toward their infrastructure landlords.

AI CREDIT STRESS → CUSTOMER CONCENTRATION RISK → PROPERTY CASH-FLOW UNCERTAINTY → HIGHER FINANCING COSTS → SLOWER INFRASTRUCTURE EXPANSION

This is a potential transmission channel, not an established sequence of losses. The second-order effect could be especially important for Spain, where financial institutions, infrastructure developers and telecommunications operators are participating in large-scale AI investment programmes. For investors: customer creditworthiness and diversification deserve greater weight in data-center valuations. For banks: long-term leases must be evaluated alongside counterparty solvency and the residual value of infrastructure if a tenant defaults. For CEOs and boards: contracted demand is essential, but demand concentration can become a source of systemic vulnerability. The strategic verdict: Spain's AI infrastructure opportunity remains substantial. Its financing model now faces a more demanding counterparty-risk test.

02

Germany — Institutional Capital Moves Toward Energy and Digital Infrastructure

Brookfield identifies Germany as a strategic investment market

On October 19, Reuters reported two significant developments.

First, the Made for Germany initiative, representing 147 companies and investors, identified up to €4.1 trillion of potential investment by 2035, across sectors including artificial intelligence, energy, infrastructure and defense. Its members reported plans to invest more than €850 billion through 2028.

Second, Brookfield announced its intention to invest tens of billions of euros in Germany over five years, identifying energy and digital infrastructure as strategic priorities.

These figures cover multiple sectors. They are not new financing commitments to Germany's proposed EuroHPC Gigafactory, and the €4.1 trillion estimate is potential investment rather than contracted capital.

Why this matters. Germany is assembling potentially complementary components of an Industrial AI investment system.

INSTITUTIONAL CAPITAL → ENERGY INFRASTRUCTURE → COMPUTE → INDUSTRIAL APPLICATIONS → PRODUCTIVITY

The entrance or expansion of major infrastructure investors could improve financing options for electricity networks, generation assets, digital infrastructure and related development projects. The second-order effect is potentially powerful. When infrastructure investors finance the physical foundations of AI, industrial companies can concentrate more capital on applications, software and productivity improvements. That separation can improve capital efficiency, provided infrastructure investors earn adequate returns and industrial customers generate sufficient demand. For investors: Germany's opportunity extends beyond semiconductors and cloud providers into electricity networks, energy infrastructure and industrial technology. For boards: distinguishing infrastructure ownership from industrial AI deployment could improve capital allocation. For policymakers: the priority should be converting broad investment intentions into financed projects through predictable permitting, electricity access and competitive operating conditions. The strategic verdict: Germany is strengthening the investment architecture surrounding Industrial AI, but the conversion of announced capital into completed infrastructure remains the critical test.

03

Siemens — Industrial AI Moves From Ambition Toward Factory Execution

A €500 million investment and a new automation objective

On October 19, Siemens executive board member Cedrik Neike outlined an ambitious Industrial AI strategy at the company's Erlangen electronics plant.

Siemens aims to raise factory automation from approximately 30–40% today to 70–80%, using AI, digital twins and advanced manufacturing technologies. The company intends to invest €500 million in expanding the Erlangen facility by 2030.

Neike argued that manufacturers in high-cost economies need annual productivity improvements of approximately 7–9% to remain competitive.

These automation and productivity figures are targets and management assessments, not achieved results.

The second-order effect. This development provides a more concrete economic rationale for Industrial AI computing investment. Gigafactory infrastructure becomes strategically valuable when industrial customers can demonstrate that additional computing capacity improves operational performance.

COMPUTE → PHYSICAL AI → FACTORY AUTOMATION → PRODUCTIVITY → INDUSTRIAL MARGINS → REINVESTMENT

The implications extend beyond Siemens. Germany's automotive, engineering, machinery and manufacturing industries could become important customers for sovereign and commercially operated AI computing infrastructure. However, greater factory automation does not automatically translate into proportional increases in computing expenditure. The relationship depends on application architecture, inference efficiency and deployment economics. For investors: the critical metric is the return on industrial AI implementation, not the volume of computing expenditure alone. For CEOs and boards: prioritize applications producing verifiable improvements in throughput, quality, utilization and operating costs. For policymakers: Industrial AI adoption may ultimately contribute more to European competitiveness than computing infrastructure built without identifiable productive demand.

04

The European Competitive Read-Through

Current strategic position and next decisive test

01

Germany

Strengthening institutional capital formation and Industrial AI demand. Next decisive test: convert investment intentions into infrastructure and measurable productivity.

02

Spain

Advanced public-private AI financing architecture. Next decisive test: contain customer-credit concentration and resolve grid constraints.

03

France

Integrated AI stack, nuclear energy and infrastructure equipment. Next decisive test: protect investment returns amid higher financing costs.

04

Italy

Significant Industrial AI potential and emerging consortium architecture. Next decisive test: secure binding financing, competitive power and anchor demand.

05

Nordics

Strong attraction of AI infrastructure and computing investment. Next decisive test: convert announced capacity into permitted, financed and operating assets.

05

Verification Note

EuroHPC Gigafactory status

No new EuroHPC Gigafactory tender award, finalized site selection or binding Gigafactory-specific financing agreement was verified in today's review for France, Italy or the Nordics.

Five Key Takeaways

The selective phase of Europe's AI capital formation

  • AI credit risk is becoming a European infrastructure issue. Spain's Merlin–CoreWeave exposure illustrates how financial stress among computing customers can affect infrastructure valuations.
  • Germany is building a broader capital-formation ecosystem. Brookfield's investment intentions strengthen the potential connection between energy infrastructure, institutional finance and digital investment.
  • Industrial AI provides an alternative growth engine. Siemens demonstrates how computing demand can be connected to operational productivity rather than speculative capacity expansion.
  • The next market rotation may favor productive infrastructure. Grid assets, electrical equipment, automation and industrial software could prove more resilient than highly leveraged computing operators, although valuations and project economics remain decisive.
  • European policymakers must improve capital productivity. Public funding should support commercially executable projects with transparent financing, credible electricity delivery and measurable economic benefits.
06

The Closing Verdict

Europe's AI Gigafactory race enters a decisive financial phase

Germany is seeking to mobilize capital around industrial infrastructure and productive applications. Spain is confronting the financial risks created when infrastructure expansion depends heavily on a small number of leveraged computing customers.

The distinction is becoming increasingly consequential. The Great Split in European AI will not necessarily occur between countries with and without Gigafactories. It may emerge between ecosystems that generate productive industrial cash flow and those that accumulate capital-intensive computing capacity faster than they monetize it.

For investors and corporate boards, that distinction should increasingly govern capital allocation.

Europe's next AI winners will be determined not by how much capital they attract, but by how effectively they convert that capital into industrial productivity, sustainable cash flow and durable competitive advantage.
