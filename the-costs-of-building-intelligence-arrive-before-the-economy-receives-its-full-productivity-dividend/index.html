Central banks must therefore distinguish between inflation caused by excess consumption and inflation caused by productive-capacity formation. Treating both identically risks slowing the investment required to generate future productivity. ⸻ The Great Split Is Becoming a Distributional Regime The original Great Split divided equity optimism from bond-market discipline. The new split divides: output from employment; profits from wages; productivity from purchasing power; and capital owners from workers whose tasks are being reorganized. A system can produce record corporate earnings and weak incremental hiring at the same time. That is not a contradiction. It is the emerging distributional logic of Industrial AI. ⸻ Europe’s Advantage Lies in Orchestration at Scale The United States leads frontier models and hyperscale infrastructure. Europe’s strongest assets are in the integration layer: industrial software; factory automation; regulated industries; mobility; sovereign infrastructure; engineering; and operational control. Europe should stop measuring AI leadership exclusively through model size or venture-capital formation. Its relevant test is whether it can convert existing industrial depth into an integrated system of intelligence. ⸻ Energy Routes Are Becoming Sovereign Assets Hormuz demonstrates that physical geography can still overrule financial assumptions. The ability to control, protect or deny passage now produces economic power comparable to ownership of the underlying commodity. Rail corridors, ports, pipelines, shipping lanes and electricity interconnectors will therefore attract more sovereign capital and political intervention. ⸻ The AI Supercycle Is Raising the Return Threshold for Everyone Else Capital committed to AI infrastructure is capital unavailable elsewhere unless global savings expand. Industries without: pricing power; strategic relevance; contracted demand; or short payback periods will find it increasingly difficult to compete for funding. The AI Supercycle is not merely reallocating equity-market value. It is repricing the global hierarchy of investment. ⸻ Implications For Investors The strongest positioning remains in companies that combine: control of physical or digital bottlenecks; growing incremental margins; contracted demand; internal cash generation; and limited refinancing dependency. European industrial AI deserves greater attention. Siemens, enterprise software providers, sovereign-cloud platforms, power systems, automation and semiconductor-material suppliers offer exposure to AI deployment without depending entirely on frontier-model economics. The principal risks are shifting toward: AI businesses whose capital spending grows faster than incremental profit; long-duration assets dependent on the restoration of low interest rates; consumer companies exposed to weaker labor-income formation; and industrial companies lacking pricing power over scarce components. Own productivity. Demand cash conversion. Hedge distributional risk. ⸻ For Executives Boards should begin measuring AI programmes across three separate accounts: Account Core question Productivity How much output or decision velocity is created per employee? Capital How much infrastructure and working capital are required? Distribution How are productivity gains divided among margins, wages, customers and reinvestment? A programme that improves productivity but fails to generate cash flow is incomplete. A programme that cuts employment without redesigning skills and incentives may create short-term margins but weaken long-term institutional capability. Executives should also monitor supplier solvency. Evidence of smaller suppliers demanding progress payments and struggling with delivery suggests that strong aggregate demand can conceal fragility deeper in the industrial system. ⸻ For Policymakers The emerging policy challenge is not simply technological adoption.
