The New Economics of European Banking Power

Italy's €5.8 billion fiscal intervention collides with the race for continental scale, repricing the economics of European banking consolidation.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann November 1, 2026 9 min read Capital

“Accounting scale can be acquired; economic scale must be earned.”

The signal1 min

  2. MPS shareholders rejected the Banco BPM, Banca Generali and Mediobanca transactions, with ~91% of votes against, weakening the independent third-pole strategy.
  3. Intesa confirmed its MPS offer proceeds, targeting ~€2.9 billion run-rate synergies by 2029, with a possible mandatory offer for Mediobanca.
  4. UniCredit expects to hold ~50% of Commerzbank voting rights around year-end after ECB clearance to cross the 30% threshold.
  5. Crédit Agricole reported €7.65 billion nine-month net income, up 13.7%, holding roughly 29.3% of Banco BPM.
  6. The contest is shifting from who controls banks toward whether they can earn after-tax returns above fiscal, regulatory and integration costs.
Read the full note ↓

Italy's banking consolidation has entered a more consequential phase. Just as shareholders clear the way for larger institutions, the government is increasing its claim on the profits those institutions are expected to generate — a fundamental change in the economics of consolidation.

The decisive question is no longer which banks will acquire competitors or control distribution networks. It is whether the resulting institutions can generate enough incremental return to compensate shareholders for acquisition costs, regulatory constraints, greater fiscal burdens and integration risk.

The architecture is clear: Intesa Sanpaolo moving toward MPS and indirect control of Mediobanca; UniCredit building a larger European platform through Commerzbank; Crédit Agricole strengthening its Italian position via Banco BPM; Unipol assembling resources for a domestic banking-and-insurance group. The hidden consequence: Europe is consolidating its banks while national governments continue to shape the economics of that consolidation.

01

What Changed

the fiscal cost of profitability

The most important new information comes from Italy's draft 2027 budget, reported on October 31. The proposed contribution from banks and insurers is estimated at €5.8 billion in 2027, via a three-point increase in the additional IRES burden for 2027–2028, changes affecting deferred tax assets, and a doubling of specified financial transaction tax rates. For banks, the relevant corporate rate would rise from 27.5% to 30.5%. The bill must still pass Parliament.

These are proposed measures, not enacted law, and the €5.8 billion should not be treated as a uniform cut to bank earnings or attributed entirely to listed banks. Corporate income taxation affects earnings after tax; deferred-tax-asset measures change the timing of cash taxes; transaction taxes raise trading costs. The new investment question is how much of the burden will be absorbed by earnings, distributions, customers and valuations.

On consolidation, no verified corporate announcement reversed the October 29–30 developments. MPS shareholders rejected the acquisitions of Banco BPM and Banca Generali and the merger of Mediobanca into MPS. On October 30, Intesa confirmed its offer for MPS would proceed, waiving the condition tied to the failed Mediobanca merger, and noted a successful acquisition could trigger a mandatory offer for remaining Mediobanca shares. Competition review, offer-document approval and shareholder acceptance remain open conditions.

02

Consolidation Versus Capital Productivity

the decisive issue

The sector faces two simultaneous transitions. The first is industrial: scale through acquisitions, distribution, wealth management and insurance. The second is financial: governments, supervisors and markets reassessing how much value shareholders should retain from the profitability of larger institutions. These can reinforce each other — or conflict.

Consider a hypothetical €1 billion in annual pre-tax synergies. Those synergies have value only after taxation, integration spend, capital requirements and execution risk. If the effective tax burden rises, their net present value falls, all else equal. This creates a distinction between Accounting Scale — assets, deposits, branches, reported revenues — and Economic Scale — sustainable earnings, capital efficiency and shareholder returns after all costs.

The next phase will reward institutions that increase returns on capital faster than governments, regulators and integration costs absorb those returns.

03

The Updated Strategic Map

confirmed facts and open questions

01

Intesa Sanpaolo

Offer for MPS reaffirmed after the vote, with ECB prudential approval secured and a planned sale of an MPS perimeter to Unipol. Target: ~€2.9 billion run-rate synergies by 2029 — a management target, not a result. The fiscal burden is a new challenge to the value equation; watch for updated post-tax assumptions. Risk: antitrust remedies and translating projected synergies into realized returns.

02

UniCredit–Commerzbank

ECB authorization on October 15 to cross the 30% threshold without an upper limit; ~50% of voting rights expected around year-end. On October 30 UniCredit presented commitments to Germany's Mittelstand to establish industrial legitimacy. Advantage: geographic diversification reducing single-country exposure. Risk: whether control converts into an economically effective combination accepted by German stakeholders.

03

Crédit Agricole–Banco BPM

Nine-month net income €7.65 billion, up 13.7%; the ~29.3% Banco BPM stake contributed €106 million via equity accounting in Q3. Positioned to influence Banco BPM without an immediate full acquisition; broad European earnings offer some diversification against Italian taxation. Risk: valuation, governance and rationale of any future combination.

04

MPS–Mediobanca

Shareholders rejected the principal independence-preserving transactions. MPS is now valued through the probability and terms of Intesa's offer rather than independent expansion. Mediobanca remains significant for investment banking, wealth management and its Generali stake. Risk: eventual ownership structure and the cost of any mandatory offer.

05

Unipol–BPER

Approximately €2.5 billion rights issue fully subscribed on October 30, supporting acquisition of the agreed MPS banking perimeter; authorization obtained for de facto control of BPER. The financing milestone strengthens credibility. Risk: final transaction perimeter, capital deployment and realized integration returns.

06

Generali–Banca Generali

The rejected Banca Generali deal removes one restructuring route. On October 27 Intesa's Carlo Messina said the bank intends to protect Generali's independence. Assess on insurance profitability, capital generation and asset management; separate the wealth franchise from speculative premiums.

04

Capital-Markets Reaction and Valuation Implications

repricing after-tax execution

European markets were closed on Sunday, November 1; the next price-discovery session is Monday, November 2. Four implications stand out. First, the intervention may alter normalized earnings — banks with more taxable income in Italy face a larger direct effect than diversified groups, though incidence depends on legal entities and rules. Second, synergy valuation must shift from announced pre-tax figures to risk-adjusted, after-tax net present value. Third, the market may reward geographic diversification more explicitly — but only where it improves risk-adjusted capital productivity. Fourth, event-driven valuations remain exposed to transaction mechanics: the MPS offer spread, potential Mediobanca mandatory-offer terms, Banco BPM's standalone-versus-transaction value, and the incremental value of banking assets for Unipol and BPER. The market is moving from rewarding announcements toward testing after-tax execution economics.

05

Updated Investment Hierarchy

qualitative, not a buy recommendation

1

UniCredit

Continental scale, geographic diversification and optionality; risk is political resistance and integration economics. It moves to the top because continental earnings may become more valuable amid rising national fiscal differentiation.

2

Intesa Sanpaolo

Strong domestic franchise and enhanced consolidation prospects; risk is the fiscal burden, antitrust and integration. Its Italian concentration warrants closer scrutiny of normalized earnings and after-tax synergy value.

3

Crédit Agricole

Diversified European earnings and valuable Italian strategic exposure; risk is capital efficiency and the Italian transaction structure.

4

Unipol / BPER

Financed restructuring and potential distribution synergies; risk is execution, capital requirements and domestic fiscal exposure.

5

Banco BPM

Strategic scarcity and a possible future combination; risk is uncertain timing and valuation.

6

Generali / Banca Generali

Insurance and wealth-management franchises; risk is ownership uncertainty and transaction-premium volatility.

7

MPS / Mediobanca

Event-driven transaction opportunities; risk is offer terms, regulatory outcomes and limited standalone optionality.

06

Three Scenarios

next six months, judgmental probabilities

65%

Consolidation advances despite fiscal friction

Intesa completes MPS, Unipol progresses, UniCredit moves toward Commerzbank control, Crédit Agricole holds its Banco BPM position, and fiscal measures are enacted broadly as framed. Consolidation premiums become more selective; capital efficiency gains importance. (Previously 70%.)

25%

Fiscal and regulatory friction delays value realization

Acquisitions remain viable but remedies, political objections and higher costs cut returns. Investors raise required returns, discounts widen, dividends turn cautious; standalone and diversified banks may outperform. (Previously 20%.)

10%

Ownership disruption changes the map

Unexpected ownership moves alter one or more transactions. Reuters reported on October 29 that Czech investor Michal Strnad proposed acquiring Delfin for ~€30 billion — a proposal, not a confirmed sale. Control premiums become more volatile; governance uncertainty rises. (Unchanged.)

07

Political and Regulatory Implications

Rome, Brussels, the ECB and Berlin

Italy's intervention exposes a tension: the government wants a banking system strong enough to finance the economy and compete internationally, yet seeks additional revenue from highly profitable institutions. Strategic independence cannot be strengthened indefinitely by taxing the capital that finances it; policymakers must weigh fiscal intervention, supervisory capital requirements and modernization needs together.

In Brussels, the Commission has continued to challenge aspects of Italy's Golden Power framework — Reuters reported in March 2026 that it was pressing Rome for further changes. Europe cannot build a fully effective Banking Union through prudential integration alone while regulatory intervention and fiscal treatment remain fragmented. The ECB has granted prudential approvals for both Intesa–MPS and UniCredit–Commerzbank, but approval is not a guarantee of value creation: regulatory feasibility is a condition of execution; economic value is a separate test. In Germany, UniCredit's strategy confronts concerns over the corporate banking system, with the Commission's competition review carrying a reported deadline of November 16.

08

The Hidden Pattern

the repricing of institutional sovereignty

The deeper contest concerns who determines the allocation of European savings. Capital increasingly seeks continental efficiency while political legitimacy remains predominantly national. Three consequences follow: ownership and economic control are becoming less synonymous; scale becomes a mechanism of independence only if it improves capital generation and resilience; and fiscal policy becomes a variable in financial competitiveness, since Europe lacks a harmonized banking-tax framework. A change in the after-tax economics of capital can alter where institutions choose to deploy it — especially as banks compete to finance the AI-driven industrial transition. The next contest may concern not only who controls Europe's banking institutions, but where those institutions deploy their capital.

Five conclusions for boards and investors

what to carry forward

  • Evaluate acquisitions on risk-adjusted, after-tax economics, not announced synergies alone.
  • Treat geographic diversification as a potential capability, not an automatic source of value.
  • Distinguish ownership consolidation from genuine competitive advantage.
  • Incorporate fiscal and regulatory uncertainty explicitly into capital allocation.
  • Monitor shareholder structures and political relationships alongside profitability and capital adequacy.

The next banking premium will belong not to those who accumulate the most assets, but to those who generate the highest sustainable returns from the capital they control.
