The Power Premium

Computing leadership is becoming a competition for electricity, infrastructure and capital, not merely advanced processors.

A consequential development strengthens our U.S.–China AI thesis: the scarcity constraining frontier intelligence is extending beyond semiconductors to the physical infrastructure required to operate them.

Three developments reported November 8–9 reveal the transition. America continues to lead the AI frontier, but preserving that leadership increasingly requires securing electricity, infrastructure approvals and long-duration financing—not simply acquiring more advanced processors. This introduces another dimension into China's challenge.

01

The Big Take

Three developments, one transition

First, Anthropic is reportedly committing $3.4 billion to secure 1.7 gigawatts of gas-fired generation capacity. The Financial Times disclosed the arrangement on November 8. The underlying commitments date to September and cover equipment previously ordered by OnePWR Solutions, rather than entirely new orders. Reuters could not independently verify the transaction.

Second, American power-generation equipment is becoming extraordinarily difficult to obtain. Reuters reported November 9 that U.S. gas-turbine delivery times average approximately six years. Worldwide orders exceed 100 GW, against estimated annual manufacturing capacity of 60–70 GW. Developers are increasingly seeking refurbished turbines and repurposed aircraft engines.

Third, infrastructure restrictions are becoming tangible. Norway's transmission-system operator, Statnett, announced November 9 that it was temporarily suspending new data-center grid-capacity reservations while protecting the electricity system. Existing approved connections are unaffected.

02

The American Inflection — From Computing Scarcity to Power Scarcity

What Anthropic's move reveals

A frontier-model developer is moving beyond purchasing computing services toward securing the equipment required to produce electricity. The economic consequences extend well beyond the $3.4 billion commitment: when power equipment requires years to deliver, access to previously contracted capacity acquires strategic value.

Companies with secured electricity supplies, operating data centers and available grid connections may command increasingly valuable competitive positions. Developers without reliable access to power face higher construction costs, delayed revenue and deteriorating returns.

The next bottleneck may therefore migrate from semiconductor availability to energized computing capacity. The distinction matters financially: installing GPUs creates technological potential; supplying reliable electricity makes those GPUs economically productive.

03

The Chinese Opportunity — The Political Window Meets the Industrial Economy

Where the asymmetry sits

The Financial Times reported November 9 that analysts see an opportunity for China following the Democratic victory in the U.S. midterms: additional American scrutiny of data-center construction could slow infrastructure expansion while China keeps investing in domestic computing capacity, efficient models and industrial deployment. The analysis identifies China's electricity availability and manufacturing ecosystem as advantages, while emphasizing that semiconductor constraints remain substantial.

This reinforces—but does not yet prove—a consequential asymmetry. The United States possesses superior frontier computing but increasingly confronts physical infrastructure constraints; China possesses significant deployment capacity but remains constrained in advanced computing. The decisive question is whether China can deploy sufficiently capable models across its industrial economy to compensate economically for part of its technological disadvantage—generating competitive productivity gains at lower total deployment costs.

04

The Second-Order Effect — The Repricing of AI Infrastructure

Four financial implications

First, electricity infrastructure is acquiring a scarcity premium: power equipment, transmission capacity and energized sites could capture a larger share of investment.

Second, frontier-model economics are becoming more capital-intensive: securing electricity reduces execution risk but increases capital commitments and exposure.

Third, efficient AI models strengthen: when infrastructure is more expensive, models that complete workloads using fewer resources become relatively more attractive.

Fourth, infrastructure ownership and productive utilization may matter more than announced capacity. Investors should distinguish committed capital from operational capacity generating revenue.

The central measure remains the fully loaded cost per successfully completed economic task—including computing, electricity, infrastructure depreciation and reliability.

05

What to Expect Next

Three indicators to watch

Infrastructure delivery: whether American electricity and grid-connection constraints delay commercially significant AI capacity. Chinese deployment economics: whether Chinese models and domestic accelerators demonstrate independently verified cost advantages. Capital discipline: whether frontier-model developers can generate sufficient cash flow to justify substantial infrastructure commitments.

President Trump's November 8 comments indicating openness to AI safeguards warrant monitoring—a possible political shift, not an enacted regulatory change. No newly verified November 9 DeepSeek frontier-model release or implemented U.S.–China export-control change was identified that establishes a reversal of American technological leadership.

06

The Investment Verdict

Who the opportunity favors

Today's evidence materially strengthens the infrastructure and financial dimensions of our thesis, without demonstrating that China has narrowed the frontier-model gap.

For investors, the opportunity is expanding toward businesses controlling scarce infrastructure and companies converting AI into measurable productivity. For boards, energy availability, capital intensity and deployment economics are becoming central to AI investment decisions. For policymakers, technological leadership increasingly depends on translating innovation into physical productive capacity.

07

The Final Take

A more demanding phase

America retains important advantages in frontier intelligence and advanced computing. China continues challenging the economics and diffusion of intelligence, supported by its industrial ecosystem and emphasis on efficient deployment. But the competitive equation is changing: the next decisive advantage will depend not only on who develops the most powerful intelligence, but on who can produce, finance and deploy it at scale.

Chips determine the frontier of intelligence. Energy determines its industrial scale. Capital discipline determines who captures its economic value.
