GVI: Global Velocity Index: The 32-Country Growth Divide

The AI Supercycle is sustaining global growth while concentrating its benefits in an increasingly narrow group of economies.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann October 13, 2026 5 min read Macro
3.1%IMF forecast for global GDP growth in 2026, revised upward from 3.0% in July.
32Countries forecast to account for 95% of global growth, compared with 54 in April.
$106.65Brent crude settlement on October 12, up 1.8%, reinforcing the energy-cost constraint.
−1.1%Potential reduction in global output by 2028 under the IMF's adverse AI-investment scenario.

“Capital builds intelligence. Industrial adoption converts it into productivity.”

The AI Supercycle is sustaining global economic growth while concentrating its benefits in a narrow group of economies. The next Great Split will be between countries that capture AI-driven growth, those that convert intelligence into industrial productivity, and those that absorb higher energy and financing costs without participating in either.

The IMF's new World Economic Outlook provides the strongest institutional confirmation yet: global growth remains resilient, but the number of countries generating most of that growth is shrinking dramatically.

GVI headline: 108.0 — carried forward provisionally. No verified score changes or ranking movements. Evidence cut-off: October 13, 2026, 04:40 CEST.

01

The New Evidence

Five signals from the October 13 outlook

  1. AI infrastructure investment is offsetting the energy shock. The IMF forecasts global growth of 3.1% in 2026 and 3.4% in 2027, but unevenly: the US grows 2.3%, the eurozone was upgraded to 1.1%, the UK to 1.3%, China lowered to 4.5%. AI has become a significant contributor to aggregate demand — and a vulnerability if investment weakens before productivity materialises. signal: AI is now a macroeconomic demand engine — and therefore a systemic risk.

  2. Just 32 countries now account for 95% of global growth, versus 54 in April; the IMF has downgraded 47% of economies since April. Geographical diversification offers less protection when a concentrated set of technologies and supply chains drives growth. signal: Technological acceleration reinforces existing advantages before it converges them.

  3. Omron will acquire US industrial-computing firm Onlogic and its parent for approximately $675 million, combining industrial computing with precision control: industrial computing → AI control → automation → physical productivity. signal: Japan's industrial-AI momentum deserves closer monitoring; score unchanged.

  4. Tencent is preparing an offshore bond offering of at least $4.5 billion, with maturities to 30 years. First-half 2026 capex reached 96.3 billion yuan, versus 45.9 billion a year earlier. Long-duration financing is becoming a competitive asset — but capex is not productivity. signal: Capital access is a weapon; conversion to productivity remains unproven.

  5. Brent settled at $106.65 on October 12 after renewed attacks in Saudi Arabia. The IMF warns an adverse scenario near $130 per barrel would weaken activity, and that a sharp AI-investment correction could cut global output 1.1% by 2028. signal: The next cycle is a contest between AI demand and the cost of capital and energy.

04

Second-Order Effects

The new global hierarchy

AI is both shock absorber and shock amplifier: the same cycle that supports growth creates financial exposure if productivity fails to materialise. Physical AI could spread benefits beyond semiconductor suppliers and hyperscalers into manufacturing, logistics, energy and mobility.

The productivity divide may deepen, producing greater connectivity alongside greater divergence. Europe's test is industrial conversion — connecting its machinery, automation and engineering strengths to AI infrastructure. Its opportunity is not to build more intelligence but to industrialise intelligence faster.

At national level the question is whether AI's productivity benefits can exceed the combined burden of infrastructure financing, energy and workforce disruption.

Institutional Takeaways

Positioning across stakeholders

  • Investors: distinguish economies benefiting from AI capex from those generating measurable productivity and free cash flow.
  • Executives: accelerate physical-AI implementation; measure time-to-deployment, margins, asset productivity and return on invested capital.
  • Policymakers: treat energy security, capital formation, automation, workforce adaptation and innovation financing as one competitiveness architecture.
  • Central banks: separate inflationary AI-investment demand from realised productivity that may ease unit-cost pressures.

The next competitive advantage will belong not to those who accumulate the most intelligence, but to those who turn intelligence into productive economic growth at scale.
