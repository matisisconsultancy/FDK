The Global Growth Spread

Strong AI demand no longer guarantees rising valuations as technological acceleration and economic acceleration become increasingly disconnected.

The global economy is not experiencing a uniform slowdown. It is entering a more selective growth regime in which productive capacity, access to capital and financial returns are diverging across companies and countries.

Three developments define the October 31 Global Velocity assessment: Friday's technology selloff showed that strong AI demand no longer guarantees rising valuations; Europe's Q3 GDP releases revealed substantial performance differences; and China's October manufacturing PMI fell back below the expansion threshold even as high-technology manufacturing kept growing.

The central implication is that technological acceleration and economic acceleration are becoming increasingly disconnected. The next phase of capital allocation will depend on identifying the economies and enterprises capable of closing that gap.

01

United States — The AI Investment Cycle Faces a Market Test

evidence

Friday's correction extended the repricing of AI-related equities. The Nasdaq declined 2.86%, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index lost about 7.2%, and Intel fell 28.6% despite 21% year-over-year revenue growth and 78% growth in its Data Center and AI division — weaker forward guidance and continuing investment requirements overshadowed the gains.

Amazon's earlier disclosure of negative $42.4 billion in trailing free cash flow reinforced concern about the financial intensity of the AI Supercycle. The US 10-year Treasury yield fell roughly six basis points to 4.93% as investors reduced risk.

Strong technological demand does not necessarily produce attractive returns at every point in the AI value chain. Valuation differentiation will increasingly depend on utilisation, pricing power, depreciation, financing costs and incremental returns on invested capital.

02

Europe — The Growth Divide Becomes Measurable

divergence

Eurostat's October 30 release is the strongest new evidence of divergence within the euro area. France contracted 0.1%, with fixed investment down another 0.6% after a 0.5% decline the prior quarter. Italy stagnated at 0.0% while national CPI accelerated to 5.4% from 4.2%, driven largely by energy. Spain and the Netherlands each expanded 0.6%, Germany recovered modestly at 0.2%, and eurozone growth slowed to +0.4%.

European sovereign, banking and corporate risk can no longer be read through a single regional growth narrative. Country-specific financing costs, industrial exposure and balance-sheet resilience now matter more.

03

China — Industrial Weakness Coexists With Technological Expansion

differentiation

The October manufacturing PMI fell to 49.3 from 50.1, a drop of 0.8 points back into contraction, with non-manufacturing also weakening to 49.7. Yet high-technology manufacturing recorded 51.3 and equipment manufacturing held at 50.6 — advanced manufacturing continues to outperform the broader industrial economy.

The second-order risk is that industrial expansion becomes too narrow to support sustained national productivity growth.

04

Second-Order Effects — The Next Capital-Allocation Test

transmission

Three transmission mechanisms are increasingly important. AI equity repricing can raise external funding costs for leveraged infrastructure developers, while cash-rich hyperscalers may be less affected; the test is whether financing conditions begin delaying actual capex. European growth divergence can reinforce differences in sovereign and corporate financing costs, with France's contraction and Italy's inflation deserving particular attention. And China's technological expansion may become disconnected from broader domestic demand, leaving aggregate productivity and cash generation to disappoint despite continued investment.

The common conclusion: the next phase of competition depends less on the absolute pace of technological investment and more on the capacity to sustain productive capital formation throughout the economy — favouring companies and economies where capital produces cumulative economic returns rather than absorbing an increasing share of future cash flow.

The central investment test remains: INTELLIGENCE → PRODUCTIVITY → FREE CASH FLOW → ROIC ABOVE WACC.

The AI Supercycle will keep creating extraordinary opportunities, but the next premium will accrue to those who convert progress into durable earnings, free cash flow and superior returns — in the next economy, the quality of growth will matter as much as its speed.
