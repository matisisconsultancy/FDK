The Great Split Enters the Cost-of-capital Era

The AI Supercycle is colliding with a new regime of expensive energy, selective credit and constrained sovereign balance sheets — and markets are starting to price capital productivity over raw compute.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann October 18, 2026 5 min read Capital

The Nasdaq lost 4.5% last week. Brent closed Friday near $110. CoreWeave's bond yields exceeded 15%. The United States reported a $1.99 trillion annual fiscal deficit. These are not unrelated developments.

The AI Supercycle is colliding with a new regime of expensive energy, increasingly selective credit and constrained sovereign balance sheets. The market is beginning to distinguish technological leadership from financial sustainability.

Capital is not simply abandoning artificial intelligence. It is questioning which business models can finance accelerated computing, secure reliable electricity and convert expanding capacity into sustainable free cash flow. The Great Split is moving from technological capability to capital productivity.

01

The Fundamental Question

What the next move depends on

Is AI still repricing the global economy — or are energy shocks, sovereign debt and the cost of capital now repricing AI? With US and European markets closed Sunday, Friday's prices set the baseline for Monday, October 19. The answer will determine where capital moves next.

02

Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy

The organizing principle of the next rotation

  1. CoreWeave fell roughly 12% Friday and about 25% over five sessions; Broadcom lost 3.6%; the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index extended its losing streak to eight sessions. The correction tests the economics of AI infrastructure, not technological potential. Higher financing costs weaken project economics and favour cash-rich operators — AI deployment may keep accelerating even as some infrastructure valuations and credit conditions deteriorate. signal: Capital will reward economic capture, not announced computing capacity.

  2. Brent's Friday advance reflected Middle Eastern supply disruption fears and risks to Russian energy infrastructure; on October 17 Egypt signaled willingness to help defend Saudi Arabia. Higher energy costs pressure industrial margins, logistics and chemicals — while AI itself demands heavy investment in generation, networks and cooling. The emerging hierarchy separates economies able to deliver reliable, competitively priced energy alongside accelerated computing. signal: Energy security is becoming a determinant of capital productivity.

  3. The US deficit equals about 6.2% of GDP, with debt-service spending at $1.36 trillion. French 10-year yields neared 4.9% on October 16, a roughly 140bp spread over Bunds; ECB President Lagarde warned rising financing costs will restrain growth. Strong balance sheets retain strategic flexibility; indebted developers and constrained governments face harder choices. France is Europe's consequential test. signal: The next advantage is investing without dependence on increasingly expensive external financing.

  4. On October 16 Beijing proposed export controls covering 23 AI technologies — models, agents and computing clusters — distinguishing sensitive transfers from access via APIs and cloud. Nvidia's share of China's data-centre accelerator market fell to 34% in the first half of 2026. The contest now spans the full stack, risking two increasingly differentiated AI ecosystems with competing standards and capital structures. signal: Geopolitical optionality is now a material component of technology valuations.

  5. European equities gained more than 2% last week; the STOXX 600 rose 0.7% Friday; third-quarter profit growth is estimated near 20.7%, and France and Germany reached a compromise on automotive policy. Europe's opportunity is to convert AI deployment into industrial productivity — electrification, automation, mobility, storage — not to replicate the US financing model. Spain's energy and digital infrastructure and Germany's automation stand out; France faces a harder financing environment. signal: Europe could lead in industrial AI deployment while lagging in frontier compute.

03

The Hidden Pattern — A New Circular Causality

Two reinforcing mechanisms

One mechanism penalizes capital-intensive expansion: geopolitical disruption → higher energy costs → inflation persistence → higher financing requirements → tighter allocation → pressure on leveraged AI infrastructure. A second rewards productivity: AI deployment → industrial productivity → stronger cash flow → greater self-financing → accelerated adoption.

The result is a bifurcation in which technological demand and equity performance diverge. AI adoption can accelerate while the valuations of particular suppliers decline — the defining financial paradox of the emerging Next Economy.

04

What's Next — The Likely Rotation

Toward capital quality, not withdrawal from risk

Within technology, investors will distinguish cash-generative semiconductor and infrastructure suppliers from leveraged operators dependent on continuous financing; the decisive variables are backlog conversion, customer concentration, funding costs and free-cash-flow visibility. Across sectors, energy infrastructure, select utilities, healthcare, industrial automation and firms with pricing power may attract interest, while oil-sensitive transport and indebted capital-intensive businesses face earnings pressure. Across regions, Europe may benefit from broadening leadership, though sovereign spreads and energy dependence could limit the advantage. The next question is whether the rotation survives earnings season.

The next rotation will not be decided by who spends the most on artificial intelligence. It will be decided by who earns the highest return on intelligence.

What to Watch Before Monday's Close

The immediate test when markets reopen October 19

  • Investors: watch whether AI credit spreads stabilize, semiconductor selling broadens and Brent sustains its advance; monitor Treasury yields against growth-stock valuations.
  • Executives: reassess capex against higher energy and financing costs; prioritize projects with identifiable productivity gains, secure power, contractual revenue and shorter payback.
  • Policymakers: track the transmission of energy inflation into sovereign yields; the IMF's October 17 warning on war-related financing strains underscores the need for credible fiscal frameworks.
  • The week's PMI surveys, China data and major earnings will separate a temporary correction from a persistent deterioration in investment conditions.

In the Next Economy, the ultimate competitive advantage will belong to those who accelerate productivity faster than they accumulate capital obligations.
