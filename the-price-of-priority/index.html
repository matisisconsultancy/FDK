The Price of Priority

The AI Supercycle is now competing with the economy it promises to transform—for capital, energy and credit.

The most consequential development in global markets is no longer simply the correction in artificial intelligence. It is the growing competition between AI and the rest of the economy for scarce capital, electricity and productive capacity.

The Nasdaq suffered a 10% weekly decline, its worst since March 2020. Yet global equity funds attracted $59.2 billion in the week ending November 18. This divergence suggests that investors are reconsidering where capital should go—not uniformly abandoning risk assets.

The ECB's November 18 Financial Stability Review adds a systemic dimension: AI-related financing is increasingly connecting equity, corporate credit, sovereign debt and private markets. The Supercycle remains a major source of economic growth. But its financing and physical demands are becoming a potential constraint on growth elsewhere.

THE FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION

Can the global economy finance AI's extraordinary expansion without crowding out the investment, energy and credit required to sustain the rest of its productive system?

01

Five Consequential Signals

second-order effects

  1. The November 19 flotation of Anthropic raised approximately $81 billion through its IPO, establishing a new public-market benchmark for frontier AI. Meanwhile, Nvidia faces an SEC investigation into potential accounting issues, according to Reuters reporting on November 20. An investigation does not establish wrongdoing.

    The deeper issue is the connectivity of the AI financial ecosystem: suppliers, customers, infrastructure developers and lenders increasingly depend on the same spending cycle. signal: A financing shock at one major participant can transmit through orders, collateral valuations and credit availability across the industry.

  2. Brent crude stood at $92.67 per barrel on Nov 23 (04:23 GMT), while EU and UK gas inventories on Nov 11 sat 20% below the 10-year seasonal average. Oil has declined despite renewed attacks on Gulf shipping. LNG remains structurally tighter, with European winter supplies vulnerable and Qatari exports constrained.

    COP31's collapse on November 22 introduces another uncertainty: the failure to agree on fossil-fuel transition language and new climate-financing commitments leaves greater responsibility with national governments and private investors. signal: Energy security and predictable electricity costs will increasingly determine where AI infrastructure and industrial investment are located.

  3. The ECB warns that rising AI-related debt issuance could compete with government and corporate borrowing, although evidence of broad crowding-out remains limited. France is especially exposed. The European Commission's November 17 forecast projects growth of only 0.4% in 2026 and 0.6% in 2027, with public debt potentially reaching 123.2% of GDP by 2028.

    Wednesday's US PCE inflation release will test whether markets can anticipate monetary relief or must accommodate persistently restrictive financing conditions. signal: Sovereign funding requirements and private investment commitments increasingly compete for the same long-duration capital.

  4. Schneider Electric warned on November 19 that Europe's elevated electricity costs, grid underinvestment and fragmented regulation could drive AI-related investment elsewhere. Yet Europe's industrial opportunity is substantial. Siemens reported record orders supported by data-centre infrastructure, while industrial AI software company IFS reported annual recurring revenue exceeding €1.3 billion and rapidly expanding industrial AI bookings.

    Europe must connect this industrial capability to deeper financial markets. SIX's Swiss-Spanish-British exchange infrastructure, expanded through Aquis, offers one potential channel for scaling European growth-company financing. signal: The challenge is no longer merely building European AI—it is financing commercially productive European AI.

  5. The global selloff is now affecting economies whose export models rely heavily on AI hardware. Reuters Breakingviews reports that a hypothetical 20% decline in global AI hardware demand could reduce Taiwan's 2027 GDP growth by 4.4 percentage points and South Korea's by 1.4 percentage points, according to HSBC estimates. These are scenarios, not forecasts.

    Monday's rebound in US futures does not yet establish a durable recovery. signal: The more consequential development is the potential migration of capital toward diversified earnings, resilient balance sheets, industrial productivity and dependable infrastructure revenues.

02

The Hidden Pattern

AI competes with the economy it promises to transform

AI is beginning to compete with the economy it promises to transform. As AI investment accelerates—demanding more computing, electricity, equipment and external finance—three constraints tighten in parallel: capital (funding competition), energy (delivery constraints) and policy (fiscal and regulatory limits).

The next market rotation moves from spending leadership to demonstrated economic returns. The paradox is consequential: AI may increase long-term productivity while creating near-term competition for resources and financing. The determining factor is the speed at which productivity gains spread beyond the companies building the infrastructure.

03

The Investor Angle

four priorities, immediate watchpoints

For investors and boards, four priorities emerge: monitor AI credit spreads alongside equity prices; distinguish secured electricity from nominal computing capacity; favor European industrial businesses converting AI into cash flow; and track sovereign yields against corporate financing costs.

The immediate watchpoints are US PCE inflation on November 25, European manufacturing PMIs today, LNG storage withdrawals, and OPEC+'s November 29 production-policy meeting. The key valuation test remains incremental ROIC minus the risk-adjusted cost of capital.

04

The Final Take

consequences beyond technology

The AI Supercycle is not necessarily ending. Its economic consequences are spreading beyond technology into the allocation of society's financial and physical resources.

The next generation of market leaders will be determined not by who commands the greatest investment budgets, but by who converts scarce capital and energy into the greatest sustainable economic value.

The next great rotation will follow returns, not promises.

Francesco De Leo Kaufmann · The Velocity Edge
