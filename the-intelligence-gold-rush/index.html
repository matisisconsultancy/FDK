The Intelligence Gold Rush

The AI Supercycle is turning intelligence into an industrial product — and markets are about to discover that building intelligence is not the same as monetizing it.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann November 18, 2026 5 min read Capital

The signal1 min
  1. The thesis: AI is becoming the largest capital-intensive industrial undertaking of the century, not merely another chapter of the digital revolution.
  2. The cost shift: Unlike software, the marginal cost of delivering intelligence does not approach zero — every inference consumes compute and energy.
  3. The scale: 2026 combined capex guidance for Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta is roughly $720–740 billion.
  4. The constraint: Data centers used ~415 TWh in 2024 (about 1.5% of global electricity); the IEA projects ~945 TWh by 2030.
  5. The historical warning: Infrastructure revolutions reward adoption, not always the investors who finance construction.
  6. The next frontier: The governing metric moves from compute deployed to economic return on every dollar invested.
Read the full note ↓

The world is entering a new economic era. For three decades the digital economy ran on extraordinary scalability at modest marginal cost. Artificial intelligence is changing that equation.

The AI Supercycle is the convergence of software economics with the capital intensity of energy, telecommunications, semiconductor manufacturing and industrial infrastructure. Intelligence is becoming an industrial product, computing capacity strategic infrastructure, and access to capital, electricity and ecosystems a determinant of national competitiveness.

The next phase will be decided not only by who builds the most powerful models, but by who can finance, construct, operate and continuously renew the infrastructure to deploy intelligence at planetary scale. This is the Intelligence Gold Rush.

01

The Economics of Building a Hyperscaler

Capital intensity meets obsolescence

A conventional hyperscale center can require hundreds of millions; a large AI campus several billion. At the frontier, integrated gigawatt-scale campuses rival major industrial complexes at $20–50 billion and above — ecosystems of substations, transmission, cooling and long-term energy procurement.

The structural tension is obsolescence. An operator may finance a complex over a decade while its most valuable computing hardware ages in a fraction of that time. Consider a stylized $20 billion investment — $14 billion of compute depreciated over four years, $6 billion of infrastructure over twenty. Annual depreciation reaches $3.8 billion, electricity at 1 GW and 90% utilization roughly $552 million, and an 8% capital charge about $1.6 billion.

The lesson is decisive. A $20 billion investment does not succeed simply because demand is growing. It must generate enough operating cash flow to cover energy, maintenance, financing and reinvestment while earning an attractive return. The frontier is not the cost of building capacity — it is keeping that capacity productive, competitive and profitable.

The companies that command the highest valuations may not be those that spend the most, but those that best convert capital deployed into useful intelligence and cash flow.

02

The Great Capital Reallocation

From billions to trillions

Some of the world's most valuable, asset-light technology companies are becoming among the largest investors in physical infrastructure. Capex now drives free cash flow; depreciation and replacement increasingly shape earnings; and revenue growth no longer maps cleanly to shareholder value.

The second-order effects are consequential. AI is becoming a major driver of industrial capital formation — transformers, electrical equipment, cooling, generation and transmission. It demands new financial intermediation across corporate capital, long-term debt, private credit and infrastructure funds. The central question is whether AI's productivity gains expand economic capacity fast enough to absorb this cycle without persistent pressure on interest rates and energy costs.

03

Electricity: The New Strategic Constraint

Geography follows energy

A technologically advanced data center without reliable power is not a productive asset — it is stranded capital. The next generation of AI infrastructure depends on integrating three systems: computing, electricity and long-duration capital. Regions with abundant, affordable, reliable energy may gain strategic advantages far beyond technology; constrained grids may limit participation regardless of ambition.

04

The Historical Lesson

Adoption, not construction

The nineteenth-century railways and the late-twentieth-century fiber-optic boom both transformed economies while destroying substantial investor capital. The benefits were often captured by those who used the infrastructure, not those who financed it. Technological inevitability does not guarantee financial profitability — and the greatest beneficiaries of an infrastructure revolution are not always its largest capital providers.

05

Three Scenarios, 2027–2030

Analytical judgments, not statistical forecasts

50%

Productive Acceleration

AI revenues and productivity gains expand sufficiently to support elevated investment and broader equity participation, with leadership widening toward industrial automation, electrical infrastructure and AI-enabled businesses.

35%

The Capital Efficiency Reckoning

Capex outpaces monetization, triggering valuation compression and consolidation as hyperscalers moderate plans — a healthy but financially painful maturation.

15%

The Infrastructure Shock

Energy bottlenecks, financing stress or technological disruption force a sharper reset, even as AI's long-term economic potential remains substantial.

Five Strategic Takeaways

For investors and boards

  • Remain constructive on the AI Supercycle, but increasingly selective on valuations.
  • Follow capital efficiency rather than capital expenditure alone.
  • Look beyond the obvious technology beneficiaries — electrical infrastructure, automation, engineering, specialized software and AI-enabled enterprises.
  • Monitor the interaction between AI investment and financial conditions — rates, credit, energy and replacement cycles.
  • Prepare for a more discriminating market, with technological acceleration and financial consolidation at once.

The defining question of the coming decade will not be who builds the most intelligence — but who captures the greatest economic value from every dollar invested in creating it.
