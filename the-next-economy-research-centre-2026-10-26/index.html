The Next Economy Research Centre

SpaceX's $40 billion financing claim signals a new regime where access to capital depends on cash flows outside AI infrastructure — not ambition alone.

“Technology creates possibility. Capital enables deployment. Financial resilience sustains competition.”

The AI Supercycle is entering a more selective financial regime. Access to capital is increasingly determined not only by technological ambition or contracted computing demand, but by the credibility, diversification and durability of the cash flows supporting investment.

On October 25, Elon Musk announced that SpaceX had secured $40 billion in debt financing to purchase Nvidia GPUs scheduled for delivery in the first half of 2027, attributing it to the company's investment-grade standing and its Starlink and launch businesses. Reuters confirmed the announcement but noted that closing status, pricing and terms remain unclear — so it should be treated as a management-reported achievement, not a verified issuance.

The ability to finance AI infrastructure may increasingly depend on the existence of profitable activities outside the AI infrastructure itself.

The Great Split is evolving from a competition in technological capacity into a competition in capital resilience.

01

Capital Formation and the Great Split

The $40 billion test

SpaceX's financing is intended mainly for Nvidia GPUs arriving Q1–Q2 2027; separately, its CFO guides fourth-quarter 2026 capital expenditure of $35–40 billion — different commitments covering different periods. Q3 revenue reached $14.58 billion (+161% year over year), with AI-related revenue of $7.56 billion, roughly 52% of the total. Diversified satellite-internet and launch businesses may support financing flexibility, though diversification alone does not establish the unrestricted cash available for debt service.

Musk's warning of widespread neocloud failures is an opinion, not an outcome. On October 22, Nebius announced a $3.5 billion senior secured facility arranged by Citi at Term SOFR plus 275 basis points, with a 364-day maturity — demonstrating that debt access has not disappeared for specialized providers. The correct conclusion is narrower: AI infrastructure financing is becoming more differentiated, not universally unavailable.

02

Semiconductors and Capital Allocation

A different problem

On October 26, SK Hynix confirmed it is reviewing additional shareholder-return measures, following reports of a special dividend of approximately ₩16 trillion, potentially supported by gains on its Kioxia investment. The company has committed more than 50% of free cash flow from 2025–2027 to shareholder returns; results and details arrive October 28. A financial asymmetry is emerging: manufacturers reap profits from the investment cycle, while infrastructure operators must commit capital for chips, facilities and electricity. The broader semiconductor baseline remains strong after TSMC, ASML, Lam Research and Besi — but no new sector-wide demand acceleration was established this morning.

03

Industrial AI and Productivity

From investment to transformation

The Joint Research Centre reported on October 14 that AI adoption in strategic European sectors rose from 7% in 2021 to 18% in 2025, reaching 20% across the wider surveyed business population — though much reflects accessible general-purpose AI rather than organizational transformation. Barclays plans to eliminate roughly 5,000 positions in 2026 (about 5% of its workforce) in a restructuring incorporating greater AI adoption; this evidences reallocation, not proven aggregate productivity gains. There is no new economy-wide productivity inflection. The relevant evidence remains sustainable operating improvement after restructuring costs.

04

Energy, Geography and European Policy

The binding constraints

Brent crude fell to approximately $114.48 per barrel (down 1.9%) in early Asian trading on renewed diplomacy involving Qatar and Iran — a moderation, not a resolution. The IEA's October 14 study spans 34 jurisdictions and ~90% of global data-centre capacity; grid connections remain the binding constraint, and the decisive metric is commercially productive computing output per reliably delivered megawatt.

The geographies of investment, profits and productivity are not the same. China's party plenum opened October 26 with no verified AI decision. Europe's AI Gigafactories program targets up to seven facilities, with as much as €10 billion in public funding aiming to mobilize at least €20 billion private. EuroHPC procurement deadlines are now differentiated — Lot 2 on December 3, 2026 and Lot 1 extended to February 4, 2027 — superseding the earlier November 12 date. France's sovereign rating was affirmed at Aa3 with a negative outlook by Moody's on October 23.

Five Key Takeaways

For investors, boards and policymakers

  • Financing resilience is becoming a competitive advantage; SpaceX's $40 billion claim still requires independent confirmation of final terms.
  • The AI credit market is selective, not universally closed — Nebius shows specialized providers can still borrow, though maturities matter.
  • Semiconductor profits and infrastructure financing face different constraints; SK Hynix's October 28 disclosure will show how AI earnings are distributed.
  • Europe must place financial execution at the center of technological sovereignty, judging Gigafactories on financing, electricity, utilization and value added.
  • Capital productivity remains the ultimate measure: distinguish capital formation from sustainable returns, and adoption from realized productivity.

The next Great Split will distinguish companies that can attract capital from those that can sustain it — and those that can sustain it from those capable of converting it into productive wealth.
