When Governability Becomes the Risk Premium

Europe's real vulnerability is a loop in which weak growth, social conflict and fiscal limits reinforce one another — until investors reprice the capacity of governments to deliver.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann October 30, 2026 5 min read Macro

“The scarce resource is no longer money — it is the capacity to adjust.”

The signal1 min
  1. Adjustment capacity is the scarce resource — the ability to distribute unavoidable costs through credible, durable arrangements.
  2. France is the test of whether conflict can be converted into a settlement households, businesses and investors accept.
  3. Contagion spreads by imitation before it reaches markets, through a cross-border demonstration effect.
  4. Postponed investment is the decisive second-order cost, weakening the tax base and productivity that ease the original pressures.
  5. Governability can become a common risk factor linking sovereigns, banks, equities and infrastructure.
  6. Lower rates offer incomplete relief, since benchmark yields and borrower spreads can move in opposite directions.
Read the full note ↓

Europe's vulnerability is the possibility that economic weakness, social conflict and fiscal constraints begin to reinforce one another—until investors reassess the capacity of governments to deliver the policies on which growth and debt sustainability depend. That would mark a consequential transition from the long-held assumption that political friction would ultimately produce workable compromises.

If that assumption weakens, the implications reach beyond sovereign borrowing costs to bank funding, corporate investment, infrastructure valuations and the geographical allocation of capital. The central investment question becomes whether a country can sustain the political consent required to deliver its economic commitments. This note maps the transmission mechanism; it does not assume a systemic crisis is already underway.

01

Adjustment Capacity Is the Scarce Resource

An economy can absorb considerable disagreement when growth creates resources for compromise. Weak growth changes that arithmetic: demands for higher wages, lower taxes and better services become harder to reconcile, and the political process determines who bears costs growth no longer absorbs.

The crucial variable is adjustment capacity — the ability to distribute unavoidable costs through arrangements that remain economically credible and politically durable. Countries with weaker capacity risk repeated reversals, temporary settlements and delayed investment. Over time, that difference becomes a source of competitive advantage.

02

France Is the Test

France matters because persistent difficulty reconciling adjustment with consent would reach beyond its borders through demand, cross-border investment and financial exposures. But the first question is domestic: can disagreement produce a settlement households consider legitimate, businesses can plan around and investors regard as sustainable?

A credible agreement needs a financing mechanism, an implementation timetable and enough political support to survive its first distributional consequences. The financial value of compromise depends on whether it changes the trajectory or merely buys time.

The quality of the settlement matters more than the announcement.

03

Contagion Begins Through Imitation

Social conflict crosses borders without central coordination. A successful demand in one country raises expectations in another; a policy reversal makes similar reforms harder to defend elsewhere. Visible conflict changes expectations; changed expectations alter bargaining; altered bargaining constrains policy; constrained policy affects investment. The size of a demonstration is an incomplete indicator — what matters is whether events change wage agreements, budgets, project timetables and corporate commitments.

04

Postponed Investment Is the Decisive Cost

The larger cost develops quietly: a deferred factory expansion, stronger contractual protections, more selective lending, a project placed in another jurisdiction. Together these weaken the future tax base and productivity needed to ease the original pressures. The asymmetry is dangerous — spending concessions take effect quickly, while the investment response that finances them arrives slowly and remains uncertain.

05

Governability as a Common Risk Factor

If policy commitments become less reliable, sovereign investors require more compensation, banks face costlier funding, companies cut capital expenditure and infrastructure investors raise allowances for delay. The common driver is confidence in execution, so a portfolio diversified across domestic asset classes may remain concentrated in the same national political risk.

Lower rates offer only partial relief: a borrower can benefit from lower underlying rates while facing a wider spread. Watch the combination of sovereign spreads, bank funding, lending conditions and project execution — a decline in benchmark yields alone is insufficient evidence of stabilization.

06

Three Possible Outcomes

01

Credible accommodation

Targeted agreements reduce conflict while preserving a workable fiscal and investment path. Risk premiums stabilize; postponed projects resume.

02

Prolonged policy attrition

Repeated temporary settlements consume resources and delay reform without resolving underlying pressures. Capital increasingly favors countries and businesses with more predictable execution.

03

Institutional renewal

Sustained pressure produces durable national agreements and more effective European investment arrangements. Confidence improves as commitments become financed, executable projects. A more severe deterioration remains a downside possibility if attrition combines with tighter credit and a loss of fiscal credibility — judged through observable transmission, not inferred from protest activity alone.

The Early-Warning Dashboard

No single indicator establishes a systemic problem

  • Fiscal execution — budget approval, implementation gaps and unfunded concessions.
  • Investment decisions — project deferrals, cancellations and changes in location.
  • Credit transmission — bank funding, lending standards and corporate refinancing spreads.
  • Operational disruption — persistent interruptions to transport, energy and essential services.
  • Durability of agreements — compliance with settlements, policy reversals and renewed disputes.
07

The Strategic Agenda

Governments must make settlements financially durable, protect productive investment during adjustment, negotiate the distribution of costs explicitly and demonstrate execution through measurable milestones.

Investors must price governability through cash-flow exposure, reassess diversification across domestic assets, watch the interaction between spreads and investment, and allocate toward demonstrated delivery.

EU policymakers must separate liquidity stress from failures of adjustment, preserve investment within credible fiscal frameworks, reduce fragmentation in business financing, and concentrate joint action where European scale changes outcomes — energy interconnection, cross-border infrastructure and digital capacity.

Europe's next competitive advantage will belong to the economies that can turn consent into execution—and execution into growth.
