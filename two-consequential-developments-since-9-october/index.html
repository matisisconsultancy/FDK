Two Consequential Developments Since 9 October

Rotterdam secures its first major AI customer as Europe’s telecoms seek to unlock €30 billion in investment capacity.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann October 12, 2026 5 min read AI Infrastructure
€7Bfirst-phase investment for Rotterdam’s AI Gigafactory
600 MWVOLT’s targeted campus capacity
€30Bpotential spectrum savings over a decade
2028VOLT’s targeted construction start

“The critical missing asset is not always capital — it is credible demand.”

Europe’s AI capital-formation race has reached another turning point: customer commitments are becoming the critical bridge between infrastructure ambition and bankable investment.

In Rotterdam, a European AI cloud company has agreed in principle to become the first major customer of a proposed €7 billion AI Gigafactory. Meanwhile, Europe’s telecommunications industry is pressing Brussels to reform spectrum licensing, potentially releasing €30 billion over ten years for digital-network investment.

Neither announcement represents €7 billion or €30 billion of newly committed capital. But both are consequential for how Europe finances its next generation of digital infrastructure.

01

Netherlands — Nebius Becomes Rotterdam’s First Major AI Customer

12 October 2026

Amsterdam-headquartered Nebius has signed a letter of intent with Dutch developer VOLT to acquire a substantial portion of the computing capacity planned for Rotterdam’s AI Gigafactory.

VOLT’s proposed campus now targets 600 MW, with an initial development phase comprising two 100-MW facilities. The first phase carries an estimated investment requirement of €7 billion — €2.5 billion for buildings and infrastructure and €4.5 billion for computing equipment. Construction is not yet financed, and definitive commercial arrangements remain outstanding. VOLT is targeting construction from 2028.

Until now, European Gigafactory proposals have largely emphasized sites, technology partners, public financing and energy infrastructure. Rotterdam introduces a more commercially meaningful dimension: identifiable demand before financial close. A credible long-term customer can improve projected utilization, provide revenue visibility and potentially support project-finance underwriting. However, the Nebius letter of intent is not yet a binding, fully priced offtake contract.

The power architecture is also significant. VOLT intends to use offshore wind electricity near its landing point in Rotterdam’s port, potentially reducing pressure on congested inland transmission. Permits and full power arrangements remain important execution risks.

The financing sequence is becoming: anchor demand → contracted revenue → financing → compute deployment → additional customers.

AI infrastructure is beginning to resemble project finance with technology risk attached. For investors, the decisive questions shift toward counterparty creditworthiness, contract duration, accelerator utilization and equipment depreciation. For boards, advantage increasingly lies in securing customers before committing billions. The qualification: Rotterdam has crossed an important commercial threshold, but it has not yet secured financial close.

02

European Telecoms — A €30 Billion Capital-Allocation Opportunity

12 October 2026

The GSMA formally renewed its call for European spectrum-licensing reform as EU telecommunications ministers gathered in Limerick. Its proposal advocates longer-term or indefinite spectrum rights and more predictable renewals.

The industry estimates reform could release up to €30 billion over the next decade, currently associated with spectrum-related costs. This is an estimate of potential savings, not an approved funding programme. The proposal feeds into discussions surrounding the EU Digital Networks Act; no binding legislative decision has been taken.

European operators face competing investment requirements: fiber, 5G, edge computing, cybersecurity, sovereign cloud and enterprise AI. Spectrum reform could release capital for these. The second-order effect would be particularly important for operators such as Telefónica, Orange, Deutsche Telekom and TIM, improving their ability to participate in Industrial AI while maintaining financial discipline.

However, there is no assurance that prospective savings would be allocated to AI rather than network modernization, debt reduction or shareholder distributions. For investors, the opportunity lies in improved free cash flow and capital allocation, not automatic AI investment. For boards, the challenge is to translate regulatory savings into productivity and recurring revenue.

03

The Competitive Read-Through

Six models for Europe’s AI infrastructure

01

France — Stack Integration

The challenge is to convert sovereign technology capabilities into sufficient commercial demand.

02

Spain — Capital Formation

Rotterdam reinforces the importance of securing long-term customers alongside Spain’s existing public-private financing architecture.

03

Germany — Industrial Demand

The opportunity remains substantial, but industrial customers must translate AI ambitions into commercially committed workloads.

04

Italy — Industrial Conversion

Italy’s potential advantage will matter only if its Gigafactory proposal can demonstrate actual demand, secured power and financeable economics.

05

Nordics — Physical Infrastructure

Finland’s recent announcements show its attractiveness to developers. Rotterdam shows access to power must be matched with contracted demand.

06

Netherlands — Commercial Validation

The Nebius–VOLT agreement is a meaningful step toward connecting AI infrastructure development with a prospective revenue base. No comparably material new Gigafactory-specific financing, site, consortium or construction commitment was verified today for Spain, France, Germany, Italy or the Nordics.

Five Key Takeaways

From the announcement economy to bankable projects

  • Contracted demand is becoming a competitive differentiator — a credible customer commitment can materially improve a Gigafactory’s financing prospects.
  • Europe’s AI financing architecture is evolving: the question is shifting from how much equity can be raised to how reliably future computing revenues can support investment.
  • Power remains essential but insufficient — secured electricity must translate into utilized capacity and recurring revenue.
  • European telecoms face a major capital-allocation decision — potential spectrum savings could strengthen investment capacity, but benefits depend on deployment discipline.
  • Investors should distinguish commercial progress from financial completion — Rotterdam has an anchor customer in principle, but still needs definitive contracts, financing, permits and execution.

Europe’s AI race is moving beyond the announcement economy. The winners will not be those who raise the most capital — they will be those who make capital productive fastest.
