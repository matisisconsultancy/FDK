The Six-year Divide

Financial markets can recover in a morning; the infrastructure to sustain the AI Supercycle now takes six years to deliver — and that divergence is repricing everything.

The signal1 min
  1. Six-year delivery times on US gas turbines are repricing the economics of AI infrastructure as orders exceed 100GW against 60–70GW of annual capacity.
  2. Asia rebounds — Taiwan +6%, Nikkei +3.5% — but the Nasdaq remains more than 10% below its October peak; this is a liquidity test, not a productivity verdict.
  3. China's five-point divide — PPI at 5.7%, CPI at 0.7% — signals rising industrial costs with weak final demand and limited pass-through.
  4. A barbell forms as equity funds take $38.63B while money-market funds absorb $172.71B, the largest weekly inflow since April 2020.

Read the full note ↓

Financial markets can recover in a morning. The infrastructure required to sustain the AI Supercycle can now take six years to deliver. That divergence is becoming one of the most consequential economic realities of the Next Economy.

Monday opens with a powerful recovery across Asian technology markets — Taiwan up more than 6%, Japan's Nikkei near 3.5%, US futures higher after last week's global AI correction. But beneath the rebound, three developments reveal a fundamentally different picture: a physical power-equipment bottleneck, China's widening industrial price divide, and investors buying equities while hoarding record liquidity.

THE MARKET CALL

The next advantage may emerge not from the greatest computing power, but from controlling the physical infrastructure and financial resources that let computing power generate returns.

01

The Five Overnight Signals

What the morning really says

  1. Taiwan led after authorities restricted short selling and signaled a NT$500 billion stabilization fund. The rebound proves investors will buy technology after sharp declines; it does not establish that the financial adjustment has ended. This week's earnings from CoreWeave, Applied Materials and Cisco will separate demand for AI infrastructure from the profitability of financing it. signal: The next AI rally must eventually be financed by earnings, not only by positioning.

  2. Reuters reports worldwide gas-turbine orders above 100GW against 60–70GW of annual capacity, with some US orders now slipping into 2030–2031. New turbines cost roughly $2–2.5 million per MW; developers are reviving mothballed and jet-engine units. The constraint is migrating from semiconductors to the electricity required to run them. signal: The next AI advantage may belong to economies that deliver a megawatt faster, not those that design a better chip.

  3. October producer prices rose 5.7% year-on-year while consumer inflation slowed to 0.7% and core CPI to 0.8%. Energy drove producer costs; weak domestic demand blocked pass-through. Export-dependent manufacturers facing rising costs may intensify global pricing competition — a particular exposure for European industry. signal: Strong productive capacity can now coexist with weak final demand inside the same industrial economy.

  4. In the week to November 4, global equity funds drew $38.63B and bond funds $9.91B, while money-market funds absorbed $172.71B. Technology inflows fell to roughly $624 million as healthcare and financials gained. Investors are holding selected risk while preserving liquidity — making optionality itself the scarce asset. signal: The next rotation may be from financial dependency into financial resilience.

  5. UniCredit reported €3.3B third-quarter profit, up 24% and above the €2.8B expected, raising full-year guidance above €11.5B as it advances toward Commerzbank. Crédit Agricole proposed merging its Italian arm with Banco BPM, lifting its stake toward 45%. Euro-area ministers review Knot and Hernández de Cos for the ECB, with the deposit rate at 2.50%. signal: The next European advantage depends on whether consolidation becomes integration — and integration becomes productivity.

02

The Hidden Pattern

A divide in economic time

The structural development is the widening gap between three clocks. Technological time moves fast: models improve, intelligence costs fall. Physical time moves slowly: equipment must be manufactured, grids built, data centers completed — processes of years. Financial time moves instantly: interest accrues, debt matures, valuations reset daily.

The three clocks are connected but do not move at the same speed. The Great Split is becoming a divide between those who can convert intelligence into productive returns quickly and those whose financial and physical constraints delay that conversion. FDK's central case — 50% probability — is selective infrastructure repricing, with productivity-led broadening and a capital-and-energy shock each at 25%.

Key takeaways

For investors, boards and policymakers

  • The next AI premium may belong to physical infrastructure — power generation, transmission, grid capacity — assessed against valuation and durable pricing power.
  • Financial resilience is becoming optionality; record money-market inflows show liquidity's strategic value during dislocations.
  • For boards, the time to recover capital is now a strategic variable — maximum speed is not maximum value.
  • European banking integration must become productive capital formation, with sovereign credibility and financial integration reinforcing one another.
  • The Great Split will increasingly be decided by execution velocity — the risk is financing expansion whose infrastructure and cash flows cannot be reconciled in time.

The global economy is moving from an era in which capital financed the expansion of intelligence toward one in which the availability of productive infrastructure determines the returns on capital. The decisive variable may be the one markets have systematically underestimated: time.
