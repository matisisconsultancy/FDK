40 Dollar Funding and Currency Pressure

The AI debt wave, sovereign refinancing and Europe's financial fragmentation converge on one question — who will command the capital to build the Next Economy?

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann November 15, 2026 5 min read Capital

“The next bottleneck of the AI economy may not be a chip or a megawatt. It may be the price of long-term capital.”

The signal1 min
  1. The AI debt wave has produced more than $680B in advanced-computing issuance through October, about 31% of 2026 investment-grade corporate debt.
  2. US 10-year yields near 5.14% raise the discount rate on every capital-intensive investment at once.
  3. Europe drew $35.1B into equity funds in a week, even as Italy's proposed €7B bank tax strains the sovereign–banking link.
  4. Nvidia reports November 17 and Anthropic prices November 18, testing both mature AI earnings and frontier valuations.
  5. Iran reported three mined tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, a maritime premium against the IEA's projected 1.7M b/d Q4 surplus.
  6. The scarce resource is patient capital, and the Great Split now divides those who can finance intelligence from those who cannot.
Read the full note ↓

The next great financial confrontation may not take place between competing technology companies, or even between the United States and China. It may take place in the global capital markets, where governments, hyperscalers, infrastructure operators and industrial companies increasingly compete for long-duration financing at a time when inflation is raising its price.

The system is no longer being asked simply to fund technological acceleration. It is being asked to fund that acceleration while also financing sovereign debt, energy security and the resilience of increasingly interconnected economies. The central question is not whether the world can afford artificial intelligence, but whether capital can be allocated among competing priorities without pricing productive investment out of reach.

THE GREAT AUCTION

What happens when the world's most powerful technology companies, its most indebted governments and its most critical infrastructure all need long-term capital — at the very moment money is becoming more expensive?

01

The Five Signals

What matters most

  1. The Fed's November 13 Financial Stability Report shows advanced-computing debt issuance exceeded $680 billion by October, versus roughly $130 billion in all of 2023; outstanding public debt now tops $1.1 trillion. US technology firms increasingly fund expansion abroad — Alphabet raised ~$3.8B in Hong Kong and Singapore dollar bonds, and Meta drew €53B in orders for a €9B euro issue. signal: The next bottleneck of the AI economy may not be a chip or a megawatt, but the price of long-term capital.

  2. Italy proposes raising the bank tax rate from 27.5% to 30.5%, ~€7B through 2029, while deferring ~€20B in tax credits over ten years. Intesa Sanpaolo's Carlo Messina warns banks could cut Italian sovereign holdings. France's November 14 protests drew 106,000 (official) to 200,000 (organizers), with further public-sector strikes set for November 17. signal: Europe's risk is not simply that governments borrow too much, but that they may undermine the conditions required to borrow credibly.

  3. Nvidia reports November 17 after Jensen Huang's resignation; analysts expect ~$109B revenue and ~90% earnings growth on LSEG consensus. Anthropic is expected to price November 18 and trade November 19, potentially valuing it near $1.4 trillion. signal: The week will not decide whether intelligence has value — only the price investors will pay for it.

  4. The IEA projects a Q4 surplus of ~1.7M b/d on ~105M b/d supply; Brent settled Friday at $95.84. Iran reported three tankers struck mines in the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday — incidents Reuters could not independently verify. signal: The market can price an oil surplus; the physical economy must still deliver the barrel.

  5. A global Microsoft 365 outage hit more than 70 countries (attributed to a network-capacity change, not a cyberattack), while a US air-traffic communications failure forced a nationwide ground stop on November 13. signal: The Next Economy will be valued not only by the productivity its infrastructure creates, but by how much it preserves when something fails.

02

The Hidden Pattern

Patient capital

The scarcity that increasingly matters is not capital itself, but patient, risk-bearing capital willing to finance productive investment over long, uncertain periods. The Great Split is evolving into a divergence between companies and economies that can attract that capital at sustainable costs and those whose future productivity is constrained by today's financing conditions.

FDK scenario probabilities frame the next 90 days: the Selective Capital Auction (50%), in which investors discriminate sharply between financing structures; the Productivity Dividend (30%), in which strong AI earnings and lower energy costs restore confidence; and the Sovereign–Corporate Capital Squeeze (20%), in which higher sovereign yields and greater corporate debt supply reinforce financing stress.

Five Takeaways

For investors, boards and policymakers

  • Duration is becoming a strategic scarcity — monitor the whole financing structure, not equity valuations alone.
  • Europe's equity rotation is an opportunity, not yet an industrial revival.
  • Boards must integrate financial resilience with operational resilience.
  • European policymakers must make sovereign credibility and financial intermediation reinforce one another.
  • The Great Split increasingly reflects the capacity to attract patient capital.

The next market regime will not be decided by the greatest promises of technological acceleration; it will be decided by the economic returns and financial resilience of the investments that make it possible. The next global hierarchy will be defined by who can finance its future — without mortgaging their own.
