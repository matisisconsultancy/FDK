The Morning View: Monday

AI is sustaining global growth, but capital markets are turning selective — the next competitive divide will be decided by who converts technological investment into economic productivity.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann October 12, 2026 9 min read Capital

“The next economy will be won not by those who invest the most, but by those who transform investment into productivity the fastest.”

The global economy is entering a decisive phase of the AI supercycle: technological investment is supporting growth, but the financial system is becoming increasingly selective about who can finance it.

Three developments this morning expose the emerging contradiction. First, the IMF has identified AI infrastructure investment as a critical source of global economic resilience — speaking in Bangkok today, Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva said AI investment is helping maintain global growth around 3%, despite the energy shock. Second, financial markets are beginning to distinguish AI infrastructure winners from capital-intensive aspirants. Third, global capital is becoming more defensive.

The AI supercycle is generating an investment boom. But investment-led growth is not yet equivalent to productivity-led growth. That distinction will increasingly determine the next great rotation of global capital.

THE GREAT SPLIT

The division is no longer simply between technological leaders and laggards. It is between those capable of converting AI investment into sustainable economic returns and those whose growth depends on the continued availability of increasingly expensive capital.

01

The Central Financial Signal

the numbers that matter

Three figures explain the emerging market regime: 3% global growth, US$153.8 billion moving into money-market funds, and more than 5% on benchmark U.S. government debt.

These figures do not describe a shortage of global liquidity. They suggest a change in investors' willingness to commit capital to long-duration risk. Capital remains available. Its allocation is becoming more selective. That distinction will increasingly separate the winners and losers of the Next Economy.

Market prices are early-Monday observations, not synchronized live quotes. TSMC profit is an analyst forecast, not a reported result. Sources: Reuters and LSEG, October 9–12, 2026.

02

Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy

from investment shock to productivity test

  1. At the opening of the IMF–World Bank meetings in Bangkok, Georgieva described AI infrastructure investment as a force supporting global demand. But the IMF also highlighted an extraordinary employment challenge: approximately 40% of jobs worldwide could eventually be augmented, downgraded or displaced by AI. Singapore's President Tharman Shanmugaratnam warned that AI is already reducing opportunities for some new graduates.

    Today, the strongest measurable benefits are concentrated in infrastructure construction, semiconductor production, data centres and electricity demand. The next phase must demonstrate that AI can raise output per employee, reduce operating costs and create new sources of revenue — otherwise the investment boom risks creating an unusually concentrated economic structure. The next capital rotation may increasingly favour companies demonstrating measurable productivity improvements rather than merely announcing AI adoption. signal: The next AI breakthrough will be financial: proving that intelligence generates returns above its cost.

  2. TSMC is expected to report third-quarter net profit of approximately NT$746.3 billion (US$23.4 billion), up 65% year on year, having already reported quarterly revenue growth of 50%. It has raised its 2026 capital expenditure forecast to US$60–64 billion. Meanwhile, Nvidia-backed Firmus withdrew its proposed US$5 billion Australian IPO after investors questioned the economics of its expansion.

    Two different AI economies are emerging. The first owns scarce technological capacity and generates substantial current earnings. The second must finance large-scale infrastructure before its expected revenues materialize. Advanced semiconductor manufacturers can benefit from customers' capital spending even when those customers face uncertain long-term returns; data-centre operators must manage construction risk, financing costs, utilization rates and depreciation. signal: The next market rotation will not necessarily be out of AI. It will be toward the most productive parts of its value chain.

  3. Brent retreated toward US$103.38 on Monday morning following announcements concerning Russian diesel supplies and a possible Russia–Ukraine energy ceasefire, which remains unconfirmed. World Bank President Ajay Banga disclosed discussions with 30–40 countries seeking assistance with energy and price shocks. Developing countries face approximately US$400 billion in external debt payments in 2026, with interest roughly one-third. The World Bank mobilized a record US$112 billion in private capital during its latest fiscal year.

    The global financial system is confronting three simultaneous demands for capital: AI infrastructure expansion, sovereign debt refinancing, and industrial and energy-system modernization. The transmission mechanism matters: higher energy costs → persistent inflation → higher financing costs → more selective investment → slower deployment of marginal projects. India illustrates this — RBI measures to reduce dollar demand from oil companies helped the rupee recover about 1% on Monday, but exchange-rate stabilization does not eliminate the underlying vulnerability. signal: Energy resilience is becoming credit resilience.

  4. French 10-year yields recently reached 4.994%, while the U.S. 10-year touched 5.3645%. The Italian banking consolidation is moving toward a decisive stage: Monte dei Paschi is expected to hold a board meeting late today following Intesa Sanpaolo's improved takeover proposal, with the October 29 shareholder vote central. Caltagirone has opposed MPS's acquisitions of Banco BPM and Banca Generali and its Mediobanca merger plan, while Crédit Agricole has rejected the attractiveness of MPS's Banco BPM offer.

    Europe's financial transformation is becoming inseparable from its industrial future. Intesa represents the consolidation of domestic financial depth; UniCredit represents continental financial reach. But banking consolidation alone does not create productive investment — Europe must connect larger institutions with deeper capital markets, infrastructure finance and credible industrial projects. A successful combination could accelerate investment in AI infrastructure, electricity networks, railways, advanced manufacturing and intelligent mobility. signal: Europe's next great opportunity is not simply banking consolidation. It is the creation of a continental capital-formation system.

  5. China's domestic passenger-car sales fell 28.1% year on year in September to 1.63 million units, while exports increased 73.2% to 923,000 vehicles. The EU and China have reached a preliminary understanding that could substantially reduce Chinese hybrid-vehicle exports to Europe, reportedly envisaging an initial annual ceiling of approximately 350,000 vehicles. Major Chinese manufacturers have already begun establishing production inside Europe, including Hungary and Spain.

    Competition may shift from exporting finished products toward building industrial capacity inside Europe. European production could benefit from additional investment, employment and technological transfer, but could also intensify pressure on established manufacturers. Industrial investment may increasingly favour locations combining competitive electricity, skilled labour, predictable regulation and efficient logistics — Spain, Central Europe and selected German regions could compete for production mandates. signal: The next industrial winners will not be those that protect their markets most aggressively, but those that attract the most productive investment.

03

The Hidden Pattern — The Capital Productivity Divide

three stages of the supercycle

The global economy is moving through three distinct stages of the AI supercycle. Stage I, technological discovery, rewarded innovation and access to computing capacity. Stage II, infrastructure expansion, accelerated investment across semiconductors, data centres, electricity and networking. Stage III, economic conversion, will reward businesses and economies capable of translating technological infrastructure into higher productivity, stronger cash flows and sustainable returns.

This third stage is the most consequential. It determines whether the AI supercycle becomes a broad-based industrial transformation or remains concentrated around a relatively small number of infrastructure owners. The financial return on AI investment will be distributed unevenly; financial conditions will influence the speed of technological diffusion; and national competitiveness will increasingly depend on the ability to combine technology, energy and finance.

THE MORNING VERDICT

Technological investment is supporting global growth. Financial conditions are increasingly selective. The premium on productive capital is rising. The next market rotation is likely to distinguish businesses generating measurable economic returns from those requiring continuous capital expansion.

05

Where Capital Is Moving — And Where It May Move Next

fund-flow evidence

During the week ended October 7, global investors allocated US$153.81 billion to money-market funds, US$5.37 billion to technology-sector funds, US$1.10 billion to utilities, US$1.03 billion to industrials and US$6.19 billion to European equity funds. These flows demonstrate simultaneous demand for liquidity and selective exposure to productive growth.

The next relative winners include profitable semiconductor suppliers with scarcity and earnings conversion, industrial automation with measurable productivity gains, electricity transmission and storage, infrastructure with contracted revenues, European engineering and industrial software, well-capitalized financial institutions, and select European manufacturing locations. The most exposed: highly leveraged AI infrastructure developers, speculative computing platforms, energy-intensive manufacturers without pricing power, weak sovereign borrowers and transport operators with significant refinancing requirements.

The market is beginning to reward the quality of growth, not simply its speed.

06

What to Watch Before the Close

five decisive tests for Monday

Equity leadership — can technology shares sustain market leadership as investors prepare for earnings? Watch Nvidia, Broadcom, TSMC-linked suppliers and European semiconductor-equipment companies. Energy and inflation — monitor whether Brent stabilizes around US$103 or resumes climbing. European sovereign spreads — watch whether investors differentiate individual sovereign risks or begin repricing eurozone conditions broadly.

Italian banking — today's MPS board meeting could influence expectations for the October 29 shareholder vote; monitor Intesa, MPS, Banco BPM, Mediobanca, Generali and UniCredit. The next earnings and inflation cycle — major U.S. banks begin reporting Tuesday, October 13; TSMC reports Thursday, October 15; the Reuters consensus anticipates headline CPI near 3.7% year on year. U.S. equity markets trade today, while the Treasury cash market is affected by the federal holiday.

Five Key Takeaways

from the source

  • For investors — follow productivity, not investment announcements. Prioritize companies with strong returns on invested capital, sustainable cash flows, pricing power and manageable financing requirements.
  • For corporate boards — reassess the economics of scale. Large AI, automation and infrastructure investments should be evaluated against utilization, energy costs, financing conditions and measurable operating benefits.
  • For financial institutions — prepare for greater capital selectivity. The ability to distinguish productive infrastructure from speculative capacity expansion will determine credit performance.
  • For European policymakers — turn savings into industrial productivity by connecting financial integration, competitive electricity, advanced manufacturing and digital infrastructure.
  • For global strategists — anticipate the next Great Split. Advantage is moving toward economies combining technological capability with financial resilience and productive deployment.

The Great Split is no longer simply about who possesses the most advanced technology — it is about who can convert that technology into sustainable economic power. The next economy will be won not by those who invest the most, but by those who transform investment into productivity the fastest.
