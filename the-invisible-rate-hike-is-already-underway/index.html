The Invisible Rate Hike Is Already Underway

The Great Split has entered a new monetary phase — AI capital formation is raising the global cost of capital while markets impose monetary restraint independently of central-bank policy.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann October 17, 2026 7 min read Macro
3.8%Euro-area headline inflation in September 2026.
2.5%Euro-area core inflation in September.
15%Yield reached this week on CoreWeave bonds originally issued at 9.675%.
The inflation figures are confirmed in Lagarde's October 17 statement. The bond-yield figures were reported by Reuters on October 16.

“The next monetary regime will not be determined by interest rates alone.”

The Great Split has entered a new monetary phase. In a consequential statement today, ECB President Christine Lagarde acknowledged that the global bond selloff is tightening European financial conditions more than the ECB anticipated in September. The resulting slowdown could reduce inflationary pressures even as energy prices remain elevated.

The development exposes a fundamental change in monetary transmission: AI capital formation is helping raise the global cost of capital, while financial markets are imposing monetary restraint independently of central-bank policy decisions.

For Europe, the consequences are particularly significant. The continent faces higher financing costs before capturing an equivalent share of the AI productivity dividend.

01

The ECB: Financial Markets Are Already Tightening Policy

Monetary signal

Lagarde's October 17 statement to the International Monetary and Financial Committee provides the most important new monetary-policy signal.

She explicitly recognised that long-term interest rates have risen substantially since the ECB's September meeting.

Higher financing costs will slow economic activity and reduce the transmission of energy-price increases into broader inflation more than previously expected.

This changes the policy calculation.

The ECB faces an inflationary energy shock while simultaneously confronting a contractionary financial shock.

HIGHER ENERGY PRICES → INFLATIONARY PRESSURE

HIGHER BOND YIELDS → LOWER INVESTMENT AND DEMAND → DISINFLATIONARY PRESSURE

The interaction matters more than either force considered independently.

Lagarde concluded that the energy shock remains too significant to ignore, but that a measured monetary response is appropriate.

Importantly, she reported no material evidence that energy inflation has yet generated additional wage and price pressures beyond those incorporated into the ECB's projections.

However, the ECB remains divided over the degree of additional tightening required.

Bundesbank President Joachim Nagel indicated that further action might be necessary.

Finnish central banker Olli Rehn emphasised continued vigilance but acknowledged that second-round inflation effects are not yet evident.

The emerging division is not between inflation hawks and doves. It concerns how much monetary tightening financial markets have already delivered.

For investors, the distinction is fundamental.

A central bank can maintain its policy rate while the effective cost of capital increases substantially.

No new ECB rate decision or formal neutral-rate revision was announced today.

02

The AI Credit Shock: Capital Formation Meets Capital Scarcity

Credit repricing

A second development makes the monetary implications more consequential.

The repricing of AI infrastructure credit is accelerating.

Reuters reported that CoreWeave bonds issued in June at a yield of 9.675% reached approximately 15% this week.

The increase reflects growing investor concerns over leverage, financing requirements and the time required for new infrastructure to generate revenue.

Separately, a Financial Times report cited by Reuters indicates that Anthropic is offering Blackstone a yield exceeding 10% on a proposed $9 billion chip-financing arrangement. Reuters has not independently verified that proposal.

These developments reveal a second-order mechanism.

AI CAPITAL EXPENDITURE → GREATER BORROWING REQUIREMENTS → HIGHER CREDIT RISK PREMIUMS → MORE EXPENSIVE FINANCING → GREATER PRESSURE ON FUTURE RETURNS.

The implications extend beyond individual companies. As AI financing becomes more expensive, highly leveraged infrastructure developers may reduce investment, seek additional equity or delay projects.

That could moderate demand for semiconductors, construction capacity and electricity infrastructure.

It could also reinforce market concentration by favouring companies capable of financing investment through internally generated cash flows.

The crucial distinction is between higher sovereign yields caused by economy-wide competition for capital and wider corporate spreads caused by deterioration in perceived credit quality.

Both raise financing costs, but their monetary-policy implications differ.

THE NEXT GREAT SPLIT

The next Great Split may therefore emerge inside the AI ecosystem itself: between capital-rich operators capable of sustaining investment and capital-dependent operators exposed to an increasingly unforgiving credit market.

03

Financial Stability: The ECB Identifies The Next Transmission Risk

Systemic risk

Lagarde's statement also contains an important financial-stability warning.

The ECB identifies concentrated AI exposures among non-bank financial intermediaries as a potential mechanism through which market corrections could amplify systemic stress.

More importantly, it confirms that private-credit funds have experienced outflows and that some have reactivated redemption restrictions.

The ECB does not currently consider private credit, in isolation, a likely source of systemic instability in the euro area because direct institutional exposures remain relatively limited.

Nevertheless, the combination of concentrated AI investment, liquidity mismatches and interconnected financing structures deserves attention.

The potential transmission chain is:

AI CREDIT REPRICING → PRIVATE-MARKET VALUATION PRESSURE → INVESTOR REDEMPTIONS → LIQUIDITY CONSTRAINTS → ASSET SALES → BROADER FINANCIAL TIGHTENING.

This remains a risk scenario, not evidence that AI-related credit losses have already triggered systemic contagion.

At the international level, the IMF's October 17 committee statement reinforces the concern.

The IMF's governing members explicitly identified rapid advances in intelligent technologies, rising sovereign yields and debt vulnerabilities as interacting risks capable of increasing macro-financial instability.

The significance is institutional.

The AI Supercycle is no longer being assessed independently from sovereign debt sustainability, monetary transmission and financial stability.

Those issues increasingly form a single policy problem.

04

The Next Rotation: Five Consequences For Investors And Europe

What to watch

05

Five Consequences

For investors and Europe

1

Monetary policy: the long end becomes decisive

Investors should monitor changes in sovereign yields, term premiums and corporate credit spreads alongside central-bank policy rates. The effective degree of monetary restraint increasingly depends on all three.

2

AI capital formation: financing resilience becomes a valuation factor

Companies financing investment through recurring cash flows should be distinguished from businesses dependent on continuous refinancing. The critical test remains whether realised returns on AI capital exceed its increasing cost.

3

Industrial-AI deflation: productivity versus contraction

A slowdown in AI infrastructure investment could reduce inflationary demand without generating sustainable productivity growth. That would constitute cyclical disinflation, not Industrial-AI deflation. The genuine productivity dividend requires measurable reductions in unit costs and sustainable improvements in output per worker.

4

Financial stability: credit stress as early-warning

Investors and supervisors should examine refinancing requirements, private-credit liquidity, infrastructure financing structures and concentrated exposure to AI-related assets. Operational resilience and the management of common technology-provider dependencies remain equally important.

5

European competitiveness: capital formation as priority

Europe risks absorbing higher global financing costs while maintaining insufficient domestic investment in accelerated computing, energy infrastructure and industrial AI. A stronger Savings and Investments Union, deeper risk-capital markets and competitive electricity costs are therefore becoming essential components of Europe's monetary and industrial resilience. The ECB can respond to monetary conditions. It cannot independently create the productive capacity needed to close Europe's technological gap.

06

The Final Take — The Great Split Reaches The Cost Of Capital

Closing analysis

The developments of October 17 expose a consequential evolution.

The first phase of the AI Supercycle rewarded the companies controlling computing capacity.

The second phase increasingly rewards companies capable of mobilising capital at scale.

The emerging third phase will distinguish those capable of generating economic returns from those whose financing costs consume the productivity gains they expect to create.

For central banks, this introduces a more complex monetary equation.

Higher AI investment may sustain inflationary demand and increase equilibrium real rates.

Higher market yields can simultaneously tighten financial conditions and moderate inflation.

A credit correction could accelerate disinflation while damaging productive investment.

These mechanisms do not necessarily move together.

The critical monetary-policy variable is increasingly the relationship between the cost of capital and the rate at which AI investment creates productive capacity.

For Europe, the challenge is particularly severe.

The continent cannot afford a prolonged economic regime in which it imports the higher interest rates generated by global AI capital formation while exporting savings that finance productivity elsewhere.

Its response must be to create the conditions for a larger share of that productive investment to occur within Europe.

The next monetary regime will not be determined by interest rates alone. It will be determined by which economies generate sufficient productivity to justify a structurally higher cost of capital.

The Great Split has reached the yield curve: the next winners will be those capable of converting AI capital formation into sustainable productivity faster than the rising cost of money erodes their economic returns.
