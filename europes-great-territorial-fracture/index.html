Europe’s Great Territorial Fracture

Europe's next divide may run not between North and South, but between territories that convert capital into productive capacity and those that use it to compensate for its erosion.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann October 18, 2026 5 min read Strategy

“Europe must finance its future, not its fragmentation.”

Europe is entering a new phase of economic divergence. The defining divide may no longer run between Northern and Southern Europe, or between fiscally disciplined and heavily indebted states. Increasingly it runs between territories capable of attracting productive capital, deploying artificial intelligence and sustaining industrial competitiveness—and territories progressively excluded from these processes.

Research highlighted by Andrés Rodríguez-Pose poses a critical challenge to European industrial policy: concentrating investment in established centers of excellence may raise aggregate productivity while deepening territorial inequality. That risk sharpens as three forces converge and interact to produce a self-reinforcing cycle of industrial concentration and political fragmentation.

Yet the outcome is not predetermined. AI can also lower barriers to industrial modernization, while integrated capital markets and infrastructure networks can extend productivity gains beyond existing hubs.

01

The three converging forces

What is reshaping Europe's economic map

01

The AI Supercycle

Concentrates initial infrastructure investment in locations with sufficient energy, computing capacity, skills and financing.

02

The Sovereign Risk Cycle

Raises the cost of productive capital, particularly for bank-dependent enterprises and financially vulnerable territories.

03

The Territorial Divergence Cycle

Reinforces differences in productivity, employment, fiscal capacity and political stability.

The strategic question is no longer how Europe can invest more—but how it can make capital more productive, more geographically scalable and more resilient to political and financial shocks.

02

Investors — Repricing Europe's economic geography

From sovereign risk to territorial capital productivity

Country exposure, sovereign spreads and corporate fundamentals remain essential, but may increasingly fail to capture the geographical distribution of productive capacity. A firm in a stable country can face deteriorating conditions if it depends on congested grids or scarce technical labor; a firm in a higher-debt country may thrive inside an advanced industrial ecosystem. The implication is a shift from national risk classification toward regional productive-capacity analysis.

Five investment decisions

What a forward-looking framework rewards

  • Introduce territorial exposure—grid availability, electricity costs, labor, specialization—into underwriting; a sovereign spread is not a sufficient proxy.
  • Distinguish AI infrastructure spending from productive AI deployment; the test is incremental return on invested capital, not more computing capacity.
  • Identify diffusion beneficiaries: automation, energy storage, grids, cybersecurity, engineering software, and SME-enablement firms.
  • Reassess European banks through regional SME credit, sectoral concentration and collateral values, not national aggregates.
  • Prepare for a rotation favoring firms combining financial resilience with measurable capital productivity.
03

Brussels — Redesigning the capital-formation architecture

From redistribution to productive integration

The challenge is not simply more investment, but an architecture reconciling global competitiveness, productive territorial convergence, and fiscal sustainability. Mission-oriented investment can deliver strong aggregate returns while concentrating benefits geographically; place-based investment aids convergence only where local capabilities allow. Europe must move beyond the false choice between excellence and cohesion—financing excellence while enabling its diffusion.

Five decisions for Brussels

Enabling a single productive ecosystem

  • Integrate competitiveness and cohesion into one investment architecture within the next MFF, appraised on productivity, employment, private capital mobilization and spillovers.
  • Accelerate the Savings and Investments Union to reduce SME dependence on concentrated banking systems.
  • Build a European AI diffusion infrastructure—shared computing, interoperable data, technical training—judged on downstream industrial benefit.
  • Make energy integration central to industrial policy: interconnections, grid modernization, storage, permitting coordination.
  • Create a European Territorial Competitiveness Observatory, complemented by an independently validated Territorial Velocity Index.
04

National governments — From fiscal management to industrial transformation

Turning regional capability into productivity growth

The critical mistake would be to meet industrial divergence with compensatory transfers alone. Transfers protect households; they cannot by themselves create sustainable productivity growth. Governments must map productive territories and bottlenecks, prioritize AI deployment in existing industrial systems, tie incentives to verifiable productivity outcomes, cut the cost and uncertainty of investment (permitting, grid connections, skills), protect fiscal credibility while shielding productive investment, and stand up accountable regional-productivity delivery units focused on execution over strategy proliferation.

05

Where governments must act

Six national tests

01

France

Restore fiscal credibility without sacrificing industrial renewal—shielding productive investment and energy affordability in vulnerable manufacturing regions.

02

Italy

Modernize industrial districts: scalable financing and technical support for SME AI adoption, linked to automation and international supply chains.

03

Spain

Convert renewable-energy and digital investment into industrial productivity that extends beyond metropolitan and technology hubs.

04

Germany

Rebuild the productivity advantage of its base through automation, energy-system modernization and supplier transformation.

05

Netherlands

Remove infrastructure constraints—grid capacity and industrial sites—so bottlenecks do not cap high-value growth.

06

Belgium

Overcome institutional fragmentation; coordinate regional governments on industrial investment, energy and skills.

06

The three-level operating model

A conditional productivity feedback loop

Brussels enables (capital markets, energy interconnections, standards). National governments execute (transformation, specialization, skills, permitting). Investors allocate and discipline (productive capital, risk, measurable returns). The loop—private investment → industrial productivity → employment and fiscal capacity → stronger investment conditions—works only when incentives align, projects are viable, and all three share comparable indicators: ROIC, AI-driven productivity, private capital mobilization, electricity availability, regional employment, and sovereign conditions, judged over three to five years.

Europe's next crisis may not begin with a bond-market collapse or a failing institution. It may develop gradually—through investment that never reaches productive enterprises, technologies that fail to diffuse, and regions that lose the capacity to compete. The decisive distinction is not between countries that spend more or less, but between economies that transform capital into productive capacity and those that use capital to compensate for its erosion.

Europe has spent two decades defending its monetary integration. The next decade will determine whether it can achieve productive economic integration.

Francesco De Leo Kaufmann · The Velocity Edge
