When the AI Boom Becomes a Monetary Shock

The IMF, Federal Reserve and Bank of Japan have exposed a new dilemma — AI investment may weaken before inflation is defeated, splitting the global monetary regime.

The AI Supercycle has entered a consequential new phase. Central banks must now contemplate the possibility that the investment boom supporting economic growth could reverse before its inflationary consequences have subsided.

Three developments on November 6 materially change the discussion: the IMF warned that an AI growth slowdown must enter policy calculations; the San Francisco Fed's Mary Daly indicated further tightening may still be necessary despite the tech correction; and the Bank of Japan's Kazuo Ueda argued AI labour substitution could strengthen Japan's capacity.

The same transformation may support productivity in labour-constrained economies, generate inflation through capital-intensive investment, and destabilise financial conditions if expected returns disappoint.

01

The IMF: AI Investment Has Become A Monetary-Policy Risk

Washington's warning

Speaking at IMF headquarters, Kristalina Georgieva warned that a slowdown in AI-driven growth would complicate efforts to bring inflation under control. AI investment has supported expansion and partially offset the Middle East energy shock — but it has also raised demand for capital, infrastructure and energy while contributing to financial-market vulnerabilities.

The IMF's October 2026 downside scenario estimated that a severe investment reversal, with financial spillovers, could leave global GDP 1.1% below baseline by 2028, and US GDP 1.8% below. These are modelled deviations, not predictions of contraction — but the magnitude shows how dependent the outlook has become on technological capital formation.

THE HIDDEN PATTERN

The AI Supercycle could generate financial disinflation before delivering Industrial-AI disinflation — falling asset prices are not productivity-led reductions in production costs.

02

The Federal Reserve: The Correction May Not Be Enough To Stop Tightening

Inflation still the priority

Daly described the tech-stock correction as painful but orderly, yet indicated further tightening would likely be required. Her remarks are not an FOMC decision — but they show financial weakness has not displaced inflation as the policy priority.

The November 6 employment report reinforced the case: October nonfarm payrolls rose 165,000, unemployment held at 4.1%, prior months were revised up by a combined 84,000, manufacturing added 19,000, and average hourly earnings rose 3.1% year over year. The picture was less uniform — involuntary part-time work rose by 339,000 and long-term unemployed remained near two million.

Markets then assigned roughly an 80% probability to a December hike, up from 67% — a market-implied figure, not a commitment. The second-order risk: higher discount rates lower AI valuations, tighten financing and weaken capital formation. Unlike earlier corrections, the next AI downturn may not immediately produce the monetary easing investors expect.

03

The Bank Of Japan: The Other Side Of The Great Split

A contrasting perspective

Ueda acknowledged AI is generating a positive demand shock for Japan and suggested labour-replacing technologies could benefit a country facing persistent labour shortages, while warning of cybersecurity weaknesses among smaller banks and businesses.

Where labour is abundant, automation raises displacement concerns; where workforces are shrinking, it can alleviate bottlenecks and support output. The Great Split is therefore becoming a divergence in the capacity to convert AI adoption into productivity — not merely in technological access.

04

The Great Split: The Next Market Rotation

Three forces, one question of sequencing

The AI Supercycle creates three interconnected forces: investment-driven inflation from building compute infrastructure; productivity-driven disinflation from AI diffusion; and financial repricing when expected returns no longer justify valuations.

If investment moderates while productivity accelerates, conditions improve without sacrificing growth. If valuations contract before productivity gains emerge, activity weakens while inflation stays elevated. The next rotation depends on whether AI capital expenditure delivers sustainable returns before financing conditions deteriorate.

05

The European Implication: A Second-Order Capital Shock

Weaker demand, costlier capital

A reassessment of American AI investment could weaken global demand for European industrial equipment, semiconductors and technology exports, while higher US rate expectations lift global financing costs. European firms could face weaker external demand without proportionate relief on the cost of capital. No new AI-specific policy change by the ECB, Bank of England, MAS, SNB or BIS was identified; existing frameworks remain the baseline.

Five signals for investors and boards

What to monitor

  • US interest-rate expectations: whether tightening odds stay elevated despite the correction.
  • AI capital-expenditure revisions: hyperscaler guidance, cancellations, equipment orders, utilisation.
  • Credit-market transmission: tech spreads, secured infrastructure lending, refinancing, non-bank leverage.
  • Productivity versus employment: distinguish real output gains from productivity driven by slower hiring.
  • European capital formation: industrial investment, electricity costs and financing for AI adopters.

The next Great Split may emerge when financial markets stop financing expected productivity before the productive economy has begun delivering it — the defining question is no longer how much intelligence can be built, but how much economic value can survive the repricing of its capital.
