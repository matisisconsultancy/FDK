Daily Intelligence Brief

Anthropic's $895 billion in computing commitments, Oracle's $20 billion raise and a $115 billion semiconductor merger are redefining the financial economics of the AI Supercycle.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann November 3, 2026 5 min read Capital

“The future productive capacity of intelligence is being secured long before its returns can be observed.”

The signal1 min
  1. Anthropic disclosed $895.4 billion in computing and infrastructure commitments, with $69.8 billion due within twelve months of September 30.
  2. Q3 revenue reached $16.96 billion (+47% sequentially), but rising compute costs returned the company to a $567 million operating loss.
  3. Oracle priced $20 billion of equity and debt, with bond coupons of 7.00%–8.65%, closing expected November 4.
  4. Marvell agreed to acquire Lumentum in a $115 billion all-stock deal, moving the bottleneck beyond computing into optical connectivity.
  5. Apple guided December-quarter margins to 45–46% from 47.9%, citing rising memory costs from AI-driven semiconductor scarcity.
  6. Europe's baseline is unchanged: no verified grid expansion, Gigafactory award or productivity acceleration this morning.
Read the full note ↓

The AI Supercycle is entering a phase in which the financial commitments required to produce intelligence are becoming as consequential as the technological capabilities they are intended to create.

The developments of November 2 provide unusually strong evidence. Anthropic's amended IPO filing reveals almost $900 billion of aggregate computing commitments. Oracle is asking investors for $20 billion to expand its cloud infrastructure. Marvell is proposing a $115 billion acquisition of Lumentum.

These are different financial decisions — securing capacity, raising capital, exchanging equity for capability — but together they illustrate a structural change: competition in AI increasingly depends on securing not only computing power, but also the capital, infrastructure and complementary technologies required to deploy it economically.

The most important question is not how large the AI Supercycle can become, but whether future revenues, margins and cash flows can support the financial architecture now being built around it.

01

Anthropic: The $895 Billion Commitment

Frontier AI becomes a long-term buyer of compute

Anthropic's amended S-1, filed November 2, gives exceptional visibility into frontier-AI economics. Third-quarter revenue reached $16.96 billion, up from $11.56 billion, but sequential growth slowed from 144% to 47%. Computing expenditure of $9.9 billion — 58% of revenue — moved the company from a $974 million operating profit to a $567 million operating loss.

Of the $895.4 billion in disclosed commitments, roughly $447.5 billion falls before December 31, 2030, with most of the remainder extending to 2045. These are aggregate contractual purchase and lease commitments, not an immediately payable liability; reported liquidity, including available credit, stands at $146.6 billion.

The asymmetry is fundamental: infrastructure commitments are contractually specified, while future customer demand is uncertain. The critical risk is a mismatch between contractual payments and customer cash flows — compounded by technological depreciation as newer architectures lower the cost of intelligence.

02

Oracle: The Cost of Capital Becomes Visible

A $20 billion test of market appetite

Oracle priced approximately $10 billion in common equity — 86.2 million shares at $116, an 11.2% discount to Friday's close — and $10 billion in senior notes maturing 2029–2046 with coupons of 7.00% to 8.65%. Net proceeds are about $19.9 billion, with closing expected November 4. Oracle shares fell roughly 8.8% on November 2.

Management expects capital expenditure to peak in fiscal 2027–2028, free cash flow to turn positive in late fiscal 2028 and annual free cash flow above $30 billion in fiscal 2029 — projections, not results. The new signal is the cost of capital: equity dilutes, debt adds fixed obligations, and both raise the incremental return new projects must clear. Where Microsoft recently priced $25 billion of debt backed by mature cash generation, Oracle is financing a far more demanding expansion relative to its revenue base.

03

Marvell–Lumentum: The Next Bottleneck

Beyond computing, toward connectivity

The proposed all-stock combination implies an enterprise value of roughly $115 billion for Lumentum, with holders receiving 4.5 Marvell shares each — a premium of about 23.9%. Marvell shareholders would own roughly 67% of the combined company, with completion expected in the first half of 2028, subject to approvals.

The deal joins semiconductor and electrical networking with optical connectivity and photonics — reflecting that as AI clusters grow, bandwidth and energy efficiency become decisive. The companies project over $1 billion in annual run-rate synergies and a combined addressable market near $550 billion, both management estimates. The economics of intelligence increasingly depend on moving information efficiently, not merely processing it — and Europe's strengths in semiconductor equipment, optics and engineering offer entry points without controlling the entire stack.

04

The Second-Order Shock and Europe

Scarcity propagates; the baseline holds

Apple's fiscal fourth-quarter revenue of $114.5 billion and 47.9% gross margin came with December-quarter guidance of 45–46%, citing rising memory costs. The same expansion that lifts advanced-memory suppliers raises costs for purchasers — a core feature of the Great Split: demand growth alone cannot identify the financial beneficiaries.

Europe's constraints remain. EU gas storage was 74.62% full on November 1 (Germany 61.03%), with benchmark prices above €100/MWh on October 28. The EU's AI Gigafactories plan — up to seven facilities, as much as €10 billion public and at least €20 billion private — now carries submission deadlines of December 3, 2026 (large-scale) and February 4, 2027 (medium-scale). The next European advantage depends on making infrastructure bankable, adaptable and economically productive.

Key takeaways

For investors, boards and policymakers

  • The financial commitments supporting frontier AI have reached exceptional proportions — Anthropic's filing provides unprecedented visibility into the sector's long-term capital needs.
  • The cost of capital is becoming a major source of competitive differentiation, as Oracle's pricing shows.
  • Competitive advantage is expanding beyond processors and models toward networking and optical connectivity.
  • The distribution of returns is uneven: semiconductor scarcity strengthens suppliers while squeezing downstream margins.
  • Europe's opportunity lies in combining technological specialization with disciplined capital allocation.

The first phase of the AI Supercycle was defined by the race for computing power. The next will increasingly be determined by the financial value — and the financial obligations — created by that race.
