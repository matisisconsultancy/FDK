China’s Army of One

China wants individuals to build AI-run companies — a diffusion engine to answer America's financing engine in the next phase of the supercycle.

China is asking individuals to do what once took a founding team, an engineering budget and a first round of capital: build a company, using AI agents to write the code, create the marketing, answer customers and automate the back office. Start with a problem small enough for one person to understand and important enough for someone else to pay to solve.

That is the call behind China's one-person company movement, as Christopher Beam's Bloomberg Businessweek reporting describes it. The United States has built a formidable engine for financing frontier AI. China is trying to build an equally formidable engine for putting it to work.

01

The New Front In The Supercycle

three fronts

Stanford's 2026 AI Index shows the U.S. leading on notable frontier models with roughly 23 times China's private AI investment in 2025 — yet leading models traded places on some benchmarks after early 2025. Capital raised and benchmarks won are incomplete measures of advantage.

The race is now fought on three fronts. Frontier capability determines which tasks become possible. Deployment determines how fast they become economical and reliable. Value capture determines whether returns accrue to model providers, cloud platforms, application companies or customers. A country can lead on one and surrender margins on another.

China's AI Plus plan makes diffusion explicit policy, targeting penetration above 70% by 2027 and 90% by 2030. The strongest opportunity may be where software meets the industrial base — 354,000 industrial robots installed in 2025, plus the inspection, maintenance and export workflows a small team can integrate and win trust in.

02

Why The Call To Build Matters

lower the cost of trying

AI reduces the minimum cost of attempting a business: a founder can prototype, translate, draft sales material and handle early support before hiring. The biggest opportunity may lie in narrow markets too small for a conventional software team.

It also shifts where competition happens. As more people can produce competent software and copy, the scarce resources become customer access, trust and dependable delivery. Beam reports a microdrama writer whose per-script fee fell by almost half as peers adopted AI — yet whose total income rose because she won more work. Prices can fall and output rise while pricing power weakens.

Beijing can spread the ability to produce while the returns concentrate in the platforms that supply compute, bring customers or handle transactions.

03

Historical Comparables

what each precedent explains today

01

Reform-era getihu

China began issuing permits to individual businesses in the late 1970s. The one-person AI company revives that logic with a new asset: software and rented compute alongside personal labor.

02

WTO accession

China joined in 2001; research found persistent employment effects in exposed U.S. labor markets. New low-cost supply benefits buyers while imposing durable adjustment costs — now on services and digital production.

03

EV expansion

China produced about 16 million electric cars in 2025 and exported over 2.5 million (IEA). Intense competition yields competitive products and squeezed margins — a pattern that could travel through model pricing and software.

04

Electrification

Paul David showed adoption can run ahead of productivity: firms had to redesign around electric power. Giving workers agents is the first step; reworking decisions and accountability determines whether gains persist.

04

Why This Time Is Different

structural shifts

A microenterprise can be global from inception, reaching an overseas customer with translated software before building a sales operation — though local rules on data and liability still bind. The supplier can become the competitor in a single release: an app viable on Monday can face a model provider bundling its core feature on Friday.

Capital requirements diverge at opposite ends of one industry. Frontier models draw on concentrated compute; the application layer lets ever smaller teams rent it. More founders can coincide with greater concentration of infrastructure rents.

The relevant unit of productivity is the completed, verified task. One customer-support study found a 15% average rise in issues resolved per hour; a separate early-2025 experiment found experienced developers took 19% longer with the tools then available. For a one-person company, the cost of checking and correcting an agent's work can decide whether an apparently automated business makes money.

Key Takeaways For Investors And Governments

where to look

  • Infrastructure investors: track cost and revenue per completed task, paid utilization and cash returns on incremental capex.
  • Application investors: underwrite retention, distribution and margin after inference and review — ask what survives platform bundling.
  • Established-industry investors: favor firms that keep part of the gain via faster delivery, lower defects or lower working capital.
  • U.S. government: pair frontier investment with adoption; help smaller firms test AI and preserve junior-worker experience.
  • Chinese government: judge the campaign by unsubsidized revenue, repeat customers, founder earnings and transferable skills.
  • European governments: use public procurement to give smaller suppliers a first customer and demand auditable, switchable systems.

The next AI advantage will belong to the economy that gets from one founder's prototype to a thousand paying customers fastest.
