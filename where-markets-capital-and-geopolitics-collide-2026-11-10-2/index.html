Where Markets, Capital and Geopolitics Collide

Anthropic's $1 trillion IPO ambition, SoftBank's $7 billion loss and a credit repricing mark the moment the AI Supercycle approaches a financial turning point, not a technological one.

Read the full note ↓

The most consequential development in global markets this morning is not the recovery in technology equities or the decline in oil prices. It is the widening divergence between the industrial expansion of artificial intelligence and the financial returns generated by the capital financing it.

Three developments on November 10 reveal the fault line. Anthropic is preparing an IPO of up to $64.4 billion, with a $26 billion private placement, at an implied valuation near $1.02 trillion — against nearly $900 billion in multiyear computing commitments. SoftBank reported a $7 billion quarterly net loss. And TSMC posted October revenue growth of 59.7%, with Infineon forecasting more than 20% growth in fiscal 2027.

The AI Supercycle is not approaching a technological turning point. It is approaching a financial one.

01

The First Trillion-Dollar Equity Test

Anthropic

Anthropic's proposed offering could raise up to $90.4 billion combined, marketed at $70–78 per share. Third-quarter revenue reached $16.96 billion, up 47% quarter-on-quarter — but the growth rate has slowed from the prior quarter's 144%.

A trillion-dollar valuation reflects expectations of future earnings; it does not establish that those earnings exist. The transmission runs: capital raised → computing capacity → commercialization → revenue → cash flow → profits. Financial vulnerability emerges when the first stages advance faster than the last. The crucial measure is whether the system generates cash flows from customers outside the financing network — genuine external demand, not mutually reinforcing commitments.

02

The Financial Cost of Acceleration

SoftBank

SoftBank's ¥1.1 trillion (~$7 billion) loss is the counterpoint to Anthropic's ambition. It included a $3.7 billion charge tied to revised American data-centre equipment plans, though procurement rebates could offset up to $2.1 billion. Financing costs more than doubled to ¥462 billion; the group raised $56.5 billion and has invested roughly $64.6 billion for a 13% OpenAI stake.

Infrastructure requirements can change faster than the commitments undertaken to satisfy them — exposing execution, financial and technological risk simultaneously. The Next Economy is beginning to distinguish the capacity to raise capital from the capacity to generate economic value from it.

03

Where the Revenues Actually Are

TSMC & Infineon

TSMC reported October revenue of NT$586.89 billion, up 59.7% year-on-year, with January–October growth of 43.3%. Infineon delivered fiscal 2026 revenue of €16.35 billion (+12%) and forecasts ~€20 billion for 2027, including nearly €4 billion from data-centre power supplies and ~€2.2 billion in adjusted free cash flow.

Both sit upstream of AI monetization, recognising revenue as components are delivered while customers must still prove profitability. This asymmetry lets suppliers monetise investment demand first. Europe can capture value here through power electronics and industrial engineering — but the opportunity lies in margins and free cash flow, not mere exposure to AI spending.

04

The Credit Repricing and Energy Paradox

Macro

U.S. high-yield spreads widened to 348 bps on Thursday before narrowing to 342 bps. Lower-rated infrastructure borrowers were hit hardest — Galaxy Helios Data Center bonds traded at 89.25 cents on November 5, with yields rising to ~13% from an initial 9.875%. A credit repricing affects productive capacity more directly than an equity correction.

Brent fell near $90.18, down ~23% from its October 30 high, after President Trump signalled no immediate further strikes on Iran — though Strait of Hormuz attacks continue. Yet Fed Governor Waller backs a December hike even if inflation moderates, and ECB Chief Economist Lane sees a December increase as potentially prudent. The paradox sharpens: energy costs fall, but capital may stay restrictive precisely when long-duration investment is required.

China adds a fifth dimension: a cumulative trade surplus of $1.04 trillion across ten months, with October exports up 35.2% and imports up 41.6% — financing industrial acceleration through external demand, but deepening its vulnerability to any slowdown in global AI capex.

Five Takeaways for Investors and Boards

The capital-conversion test

  • The AI Supercycle is entering its capital-conversion test — growth must be weighed against operating cash flow, utilization and returns on invested capital.
  • Separate infrastructure suppliers (TSMC, Infineon) from infrastructure financiers (SoftBank).
  • Watch credit markets as closely as technology equities; persistent financing premiums matter more than valuation volatility.
  • Anticipate the migration of value toward power management, manufacturing, reliable energy and enterprise productivity.
  • Reassess the Great Split through capital productivity — durable returns, not expensive financing, will decide the winners.
The Great Capital Conversion remains the central working scenario: capital is becoming increasingly differentiated by borrower quality.

The next great redistribution of global wealth will not be decided by who raises the most capital to build intelligence — but by who can turn intelligence into cash flow, cash flow into economic profit, and profit into the next competitive advantage.
