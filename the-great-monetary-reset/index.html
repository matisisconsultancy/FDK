The Great Monetary Reset

The AI Supercycle is dividing central banks over whether policy should restrain extraordinary investment demand or accommodate a rising productive frontier.

The AI Supercycle is beginning to divide central banks over a fundamental question: should monetary policy restrain an economy experiencing extraordinary investment demand, or accommodate the possibility that technological progress is increasing its productive potential?

Three consequential developments over the past 24 hours bring this question into sharper focus. On October 28, the Federal Reserve maintained interest rates at 3.75%–4.00%, despite two policymakers favouring an immediate increase. The Bank of Canada reached a different conclusion, raising its policy rate by 25 basis points to 2.50% as energy costs threatened to spread more broadly. And this morning, October 29, Christine Lagarde announced that she will leave the European Central Bank presidency on February 7, 2027—almost nine months before her mandate expires.

Europe must now manage an increasingly difficult monetary-policy environment alongside an unexpected leadership transition.

01

The Federal Reserve: Productivity Enters the Policy Calculation

Warsh and the inflation question

The Federal Reserve's October 28 decision represents the most significant new monetary-policy evidence concerning the AI Supercycle. The FOMC maintained its policy rate at 3.75%–4.00% by a 10–2 vote. Governors Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack favoured a 25-basis-point increase.

The committee simultaneously upgraded its description of economic activity, recognising strong growth, robust capital investment and improving productivity. Inflation nevertheless remains elevated.

Chair Kevin Warsh's subsequent remarks introduce an important distinction. He argued that the economy may be capable of sustaining stronger growth without generating excessive inflation because productive capacity is improving. Significantly, he acknowledged that much of the measured productivity improvement appears to precede the latest advances in frontier AI.

This is not evidence that Industrial-AI deflation has already arrived. It is evidence that the Federal Reserve is increasingly considering whether stronger productive capacity changes the inflationary significance of rapid economic growth.

The monetary implications are substantial. If productive capacity expands alongside demand, economic growth can accelerate without proportionate increases in inflation. But if AI capital formation generates immediate demand for electricity, construction, equipment and financing while productivity improvements remain delayed, inflationary pressures can persist. The Fed's disagreement reflects this unresolved distinction.

Two mechanisms

AI CAPITAL FORMATION → STRONGER INVESTMENT DEMAND → POTENTIAL INFLATION PRESSURE. AI PRODUCTIVITY DIFFUSION → HIGHER POTENTIAL OUTPUT → LOWER UNIT COSTS → POTENTIAL DISINFLATION.

02

The Second-Order Effect: The Neutral-Rate Question

The two mechanisms can operate simultaneously. Their relative importance determines the appropriate monetary response.

Sustained increases in productive investment and expected future income could support a higher equilibrium real interest rate. Yet weaker labour demand, increased savings and concentrated productivity gains could offset that pressure. Warsh's assessment does not constitute a new formal estimate of the neutral rate.

Nevertheless, it reinforces why conventional measures of economic overheating may become less reliable as the AI Supercycle advances. For investors, the critical question is whether higher growth expectations reflect sustainable productivity or capital expenditure whose returns remain uncertain.

03

Canada: Energy Inflation Overrides the Productivity Promise

The Bank of Canada's October 28 decision provides a revealing counterpoint. Governor Tiff Macklem's institution raised its benchmark rate to 2.50%, explicitly responding to the risk that elevated energy prices will increasingly affect the broader inflation process.

The central bank now expects inflation to average approximately 3% in 2027, returning toward its 2% target around the middle of 2028. Its assessment recognises that American economic growth remains supported by substantial AI-related investment. But Canada faces a different immediate monetary constraint: persistent energy inflation and the possibility of wider cost pass-through.

The distinction is consequential. The Federal Reserve is evaluating whether productivity improvements justify patience despite strong growth. Canada is acting to prevent an external inflationary shock from becoming embedded in domestic pricing behaviour. Neither position establishes a universal monetary-policy response to AI. Instead, the divergence demonstrates how the same global technological investment cycle interacts with different national production structures and inflation exposures.

For Europe, this is particularly relevant. Energy-import dependence can raise industrial costs precisely when investment in computing infrastructure and automation is becoming increasingly important. The resulting problem is not simply monetary. Higher energy costs can undermine the economics of the productive investment required to generate future disinflation.

04

The ECB: A Leadership Transition at a Critical Moment

The most significant development this morning is Christine Lagarde's announcement that she will leave the ECB presidency on February 7, 2027. The decision accelerates a leadership transition previously expected only at the conclusion of her mandate in October 2027. Former Dutch central-bank governor Klaas Knot and BIS General Manager Pablo Hernández de Cos are among the potential successors discussed by market observers. No appointment has been announced.

The immediate significance is institutional rather than a demonstrated change in monetary policy. However, the timing matters. Europe faces persistent energy-driven inflation, rising sovereign financing costs and increasingly uneven credit conditions. France's fiscal and sovereign-market difficulties further complicate monetary transmission.

The ECB is expected to announce its October 29 interest-rate decision at 14:15 Central European Time, after this assessment. The decision has therefore not yet been incorporated into this report.

A leadership change does not automatically alter the ECB's mandate or policy framework. Nevertheless, the next president will inherit an economic environment increasingly shaped by the AI Supercycle. Three strategic questions will become more consequential. First, how should the ECB distinguish inflation caused by investment and energy constraints from disinflation generated by sustained productivity improvements? Second, how should monetary policy respond when a common interest rate produces increasingly different financing conditions across member states? Third, how can Europe's financial architecture support AI capital formation without amplifying sovereign and private-credit vulnerabilities?

The next ECB presidency will help determine how Europe interprets the monetary consequences of technological transformation. That is a more consequential responsibility than merely managing the next interest-rate cycle.

05

The Great Split: A New Financial Transmission Mechanism

Three possible paths

The developments of October 28–29 suggest three possible paths for the next phase of the AI Supercycle.

Productivity-led expansion would allow output and corporate earnings to increase while unit costs gradually decline. This could support valuations without requiring proportionately higher inflation or interest rates.

Investment-led overheating would occur if capital expenditure continues accelerating faster than productive capacity. Energy demand, equipment shortages and financing requirements could sustain inflation and higher interest rates.

Credit-driven adjustment would emerge if expected AI returns disappoint, causing valuations to decline, financing spreads to widen and investment to slow.

The third path introduces a critical distinction. A decline in inflation caused by reduced investment is not equivalent to productivity-driven disinflation. The former can weaken future productive capacity. The latter strengthens it. The Great Split is increasingly a divergence between economies that can sustain investment until productivity materialises and those forced to constrain investment before the returns arrive.

Five Takeaways for Investors and European Policymakers

Where the debate now leads

  • The Fed is opening the productivity debate. Stronger growth need not automatically imply stronger inflation, but markets should avoid attributing existing productivity improvements entirely to frontier AI.
  • Energy remains the immediate constraint. Canada's decision demonstrates that persistent energy inflation can require tighter policy even when technological progress offers substantial longer-term supply-side benefits.
  • Europe faces a monetary leadership transition. Lagarde's departure creates an opportunity for strategic reassessment, but also introduces uncertainty while sovereign financing conditions remain fragile.
  • Capital allocation will determine the next market rotation. Investors should distinguish businesses with measurable AI-driven improvements in returns on invested capital from those relying on continued infrastructure spending and favourable refinancing conditions.
  • The EU must accelerate productive investment without compromising financial stability. Deeper capital markets, competitive electricity costs, industrial AI deployment and coordinated supervision are increasingly complementary requirements.
06

The Final Take: The Price of Money in the Age of Intelligence

The past 24 hours establish a consequential new dimension of the global monetary debate. The Federal Reserve is acknowledging the possibility that productive capacity is expanding faster than conventional economic models can confidently measure. Canada is demonstrating that persistent inflation can still require monetary restraint. Europe is entering a leadership transition at a moment when its monetary framework must accommodate greater technological, financial and sovereign divergence.

The central challenge is increasingly clear. Monetary policy must distinguish economic growth that consumes scarce resources from technological progress that expands the economy's productive frontier. For investors, the next decisive variable will be the relationship between expected productivity gains, the cost of capital and realised financial returns. For European policymakers, the priority is to ensure that financial fragmentation and energy constraints do not prevent productive investment from taking place. And for central banks, the challenge is to recognise that AI can simultaneously raise investment demand, alter potential growth and create new sources of financial instability.

Primary documents: Federal Reserve — October 28 FOMC decision · Bank of Canada — October 28 policy decision · Reuters — October 29 ECB leadership announcement.

The next monetary regime will be defined not simply by the level of interest rates, but by central banks' ability to distinguish the inflationary cost of building the AI economy from the disinflationary value of operating it.
