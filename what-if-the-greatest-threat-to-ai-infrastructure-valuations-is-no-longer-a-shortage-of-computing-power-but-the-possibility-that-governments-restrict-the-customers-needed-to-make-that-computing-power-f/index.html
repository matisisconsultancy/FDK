What if the Greatest Threat to AI Infrastructure Valuations Is No Longer a Shortage of Computing Power: But the Possibility That Governments Restrict the Customers Needed to Make That Computing Power F

Washington extends the AI cold war from semiconductors to cloud computing as Wall Street enters correction territory and the ECB warns of a $5.5 trillion financing challenge.

  2. Markets corrected: the Nasdaq closed −2.49%, now more than 10% below its record, while the semiconductor index fell 5.3%.
  3. The ECB warned of $5.5tn in US technology financing needs through 2030, competing with sovereign borrowing for capital.

  5. The EBRD flagged that nearly half the economies it covers lack grid capacity for even one data centre.
  6. The Great Split is shifting from who owns intelligence to who can lawfully and profitably deploy it.
Read the full note ↓

The most consequential development in global markets this morning is not Nvidia's approaching earnings. It is Washington's decision to extend restrictions on advanced computing from the physical ownership of semiconductors to remote access to their computational power.

A data centre can possess advanced chips, electricity connections and long-term financing. But its financial value ultimately depends on lawful market access, contractual revenues and utilization. If restrictions alter those assumptions, the economic value of infrastructure can change before its technological capabilities deteriorate.

01

Five Signals Shaping the Next Economy

Access, financing and utilization

  1. Washington's interim rule extends restrictions to remote computing services and adds licensing for covered chip shipments through Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. Overseas data centres supplying affected Chinese firms have a 90-day window; US-operated centres gain specified exemptions. The measures separate physical ownership from commercially permissible access — a new exposure running from export controls through customer eligibility to infrastructure valuations. signal: Regulatory access to computing is becoming a determinant of infrastructure creditworthiness.

  2. Nvidia reports fiscal Q3 today, with ~$109.1bn revenue and 90.4% year-on-year earnings growth expected. Colette Kress, interim CEO since Jensen Huang's November 12 resignation, addresses investors at approximately 22:20 CET. A revenue surprise alone may no longer restore confidence amid uncertainty over leadership continuity, export exposure and ecosystem financing. signal: Focus on forward guidance, margins, cash generation and China exposure — not the headline beat.

  3. ECB Vice President Vujčić warned that AI financing and sovereign indebtedness are increasingly reinforcing financial vulnerabilities, citing $5.5tn in tech financing needs, opaque debt structures and competition for capital between governments and companies. Markets assign a 95.7% probability to a December Fed +25bp move; the US 10Y yield sits near 5.13%. signal: The next hierarchy of competitiveness depends on financing productive investment without undermining stability.

  4. Brent crude fell to roughly $91.66 on expectations of an expanding supply surplus, though ANZ estimates Hormuz throughput at only 49% of pre-war levels. The EBRD warns that many emerging economies lack the grid, financing and industry to own frontier infrastructure — but can still benefit through less capital-intensive AI applications. signal: The most efficient AI opportunity in many economies is application, not a full domestic computing ecosystem.

  5. Ahead of the ECB's November 18 Financial Stability Review, French fiscal and growth risks persist. Campaign for Better Transport projects Channel Tunnel passenger journeys could rise from 11.8m today to 28m by 2040 if investment and institutional barriers are addressed — a conditional projection, not secured demand. signal: Europe's advantage will depend on the productivity of capital, not the scale of new announcements.

02

The Central Thesis

The Great Split enters its most consequential phase

The decisive divide will no longer be simply between those who possess intelligence and those who do not. It will increasingly separate those capable of deploying intelligence under secure regulatory conditions, at competitive financing costs and with sustainable economic returns.

The first divide concerned who possessed advanced technology; the second who could scale it; the third who could attract the capital to finance it. The fourth will distinguish those capable of converting technological capacity into sustainable economic returns from those whose access, financing or operating conditions prevent them from doing so.

Technology creates capability. Geopolitics determines access. Capital finances deployment. Productivity determines value. The next great market rotation will follow the companies and economies capable of mastering all four.

The world has spent extraordinary amounts of capital building intelligence. It is now discovering that the right to use intelligence may become as economically consequential as the ability to produce it — and the future of financial power will belong to those who can make intelligence economically sovereign, commercially productive and financially sustainable.

Francesco De Leo Kaufmann · The Velocity Edge
