Record bond issuance proves capital is abundant, but financial resilience is becoming the decisive variable of the AI Supercycle.

  2. Five hyperscalers now move credit markets: Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle raised over $370.7B, roughly 20% of issuance.

The AI Supercycle is entering a phase in which the availability of capital remains extraordinary, but the financial conditions attached to that capital are becoming increasingly unequal. The events of November 6 reveal a fundamental paradox.

US investment-grade issuance has reached a record $1.88 trillion, yet SoftBank's credit-default-swap spread hit a four-year high and OpenAI closed financing below its target. These developments do not show collapsing demand for artificial intelligence. They reveal that the cycle is differentiating between companies that finance expansion through strong balance sheets and those dependent on increasingly demanding external financing.

01

Capital Formation

A record year for corporate debt

According to LSEG data reported by Reuters, investment-grade issuance reached $1.88 trillion through November 4, exceeding the 2020 record of $1.86 trillion. Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle together accounted for more than $370.7 billion. Goldman Sachs expects bonds to cover roughly 35% of hyperscaler capital expenditure in 2027.

AI-related financing is no longer a sector phenomenon; it has become a major component of credit-market activity. The data demonstrate substantial capacity to absorb debt, not a generalized shortage of capital. But when one sector accounts for a growing share of issuance, shifts in investor appetite can influence pricing and availability across other borrowers — particularly with government borrowing and interest rates elevated.

02

SoftBank and OpenAI

The market prices concentration

SoftBank provides the clearest evidence of differentiated risk. On November 6 its shares fell 10.8% and its five-year CDS reached approximately 466 basis points, the highest since October 2022. Its exposure includes an ~$64.6 billion investment in OpenAI, a major Arm position and SB Energy commitments. The widening spreads are not a forecast of default; they reflect a reassessment of concentrated, interconnected AI exposure — the relationship between liquid resources, obligations, asset valuations and the timing of realizable cash flows.

OpenAI, meanwhile, reportedly closed an initial $11 billion tranche at a $1.25 trillion valuation, with SoftBank and MGX participating — below the $1.5 trillion previously sought. Substantial private capital remains available to leading developers, but valuation discipline is increasing. Access to financing and the valuation attached to it are now distinct measures of financial strength.

03

Markets and Macro

Volatility is not demand destruction

South Korea's KOSPI fell 6.02% on Friday, triggering a temporary trading halt; Samsung declined 6.4% and SK Hynix 10.4%, with foreign investors selling roughly ₩2.8 trillion. These are market observations, not evidence of falling semiconductor orders. The US session delivered a partial reversal — the Nasdaq gained 1.1% and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index rose 4.2% — yet the Nasdaq still fell 3.4% on the week, its fourth consecutive decline.

The macro backdrop tightened the hurdle. US nonfarm employment rose 165,000 in October, unemployment eased to 4.1%, and the ten-year Treasury yield reached ~4.86%. A higher risk-free rate raises the threshold for investments whose returns are concentrated far in the future — precisely the profile of frontier AI and infrastructure.

04

Industrial AI and Europe

Displacement and the social licence

Teleperformance's Q3 disclosure shows the financial complexity of enterprise AI: like-for-like revenue fell 1.2% even as AI projects more than doubled to 1,400 and over 35% of clients adopted TP.ai solutions. Adoption and disruption are occurring inside the same enterprise; the relevant measure is net value creation after revenues displaced by automation — though offshoring and industry headwinds also contributed.

In Amsterdam, demonstrators entered a Microsoft data-centre construction site developed by Pure Data Centres (a >$1 billion investment), amid a coordinated campaign across 16 countries. Public acceptance now shapes permitting, delays and returns. EU data centres consumed ~68 TWh in 2024, potentially 114 TWh by 2030. Europe's challenge is to connect technological specialization, productive capital, reliable energy and efficient implementation — its €525 million Chip Design Platform and EuroHPC AI Gigafactories programme remain active, but operating-capacity and productivity baselines are unchanged.

Key takeaways

For investors, boards and policymakers

  • The AI Supercycle has become a major force in credit markets, raising the importance of monitoring concentration.
  • Financial resilience is becoming a competitive advantage; SoftBank's repricing highlights concentrated-exposure risk.
  • Market volatility must not be confused with deteriorating physical demand.
  • Industrial AI creates winners and displacement risks simultaneously, as Teleperformance shows.
  • Europe must integrate technological ambition with financial and infrastructure execution.

The AI Supercycle is not running out of capital. It is entering a phase in which the price, structure and productivity of capital will determine the distribution of its rewards.
