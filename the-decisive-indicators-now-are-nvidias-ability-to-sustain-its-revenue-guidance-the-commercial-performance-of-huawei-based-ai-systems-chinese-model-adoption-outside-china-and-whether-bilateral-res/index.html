The Decisive Indicators Now Are Nvidia’s Ability to Sustain Its Revenue Guidance, the Commercial Performance of Huawei-based AI Systems, Chinese Model Adoption Outside China, and Whether Bilateral Res

How Nvidia's $130 billion revenue forecast is redrawing the financial geography of the AI Supercycle.

  2. Chinese data-center compute is assumed at zero in Q4 guidance; Nvidia stopped shipping U.S.-licensed Hopper chips on November 18.

Read the full note ↓

What happens when the world's leading AI computing company can sustain extraordinary growth while planning for no data-center computing revenue from the world's second-largest economy? This is today's consequential development.

On November 17, Nvidia reported record quarterly revenue and issued fourth-quarter guidance excluding Chinese data-center computing sales. On November 18, Reuters confirmed that Nvidia has stopped shipping U.S.-licensed Hopper processors to China, although the company has not explained whether the suspension is permanent.

01

The Big Take — Decoupling Without Demand Destruction

frontier leadership, less dependent on China

Nvidia sold $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper data-center products to China in the quarter ended October 25. That represented approximately 2.1% of total quarterly revenue.

Despite excluding that business from its next-quarter outlook, Nvidia expects continued substantial growth. Its Vera Rubin NVL72 accelerated-computing platform has also begun shipping.

Three implications follow. First, U.S. frontier computing leadership is becoming less dependent on Chinese demand. Nvidia's earnings trajectory demonstrates the scale of investment being mobilized elsewhere, although it does not establish that every AI infrastructure investment will deliver attractive returns.

Second, China's incentive to develop domestic computing capacity is strengthening. Beijing has itself restricted imports of Nvidia's H200 chips, with Reuters previously reporting approximately 600,000 processors awaiting approval in Hong Kong. Supply constraints and industrial policy are now reinforcing each other.

Third, the AI Supercycle may increasingly divide into distinct computing ecosystems without producing an equivalent division in AI applications. China can continue distributing comparatively inexpensive models even as the infrastructure supporting them becomes more domestically oriented.

THE HIDDEN PATTERN

The geography of computing production is fragmenting faster than the global market for intelligence.

02

The Second-Order Financial Effects

investment exposure and emerging implication

01

Nvidia and American AI infrastructure

Greater reliance on allied and domestic demand; stronger sensitivity to capital expenditure discipline.

02

Huawei and Chinese accelerator developers

Greater opportunity for domestic substitution, but execution and manufacturing constraints remain.

03

Chinese frontier-model developers

Greater pressure to improve inference efficiency and hardware portability.

04

Global data-center operators

Potential geographic reallocation of capacity, customers and financing.

05

Semiconductor supply chains

Increasing geopolitical costs and duplication of infrastructure.

03

What It Means for The Velocity Edge

strengthening the first half of the thesis

The geopolitical dimension is intensifying. On November 17, China's Commerce Ministry warned of countermeasures against the new American restrictions on remote access to advanced AI chips. No retaliatory measure was confirmed as implemented in that announcement.

Today's results strengthen the first half of our governing thesis: America continues to dominate key elements of frontier computing and is demonstrating the capacity to finance its expansion without relying on Chinese data-center sales.

They do not independently prove the second half — that Chinese developers have achieved superior inference economics. That proposition still requires independently validated cost-per-task comparisons and evidence of profitable industrial deployment.

04

The Final Take

a more financially consequential phase

The U.S.–China AI competition is entering a more financially consequential phase. The United States is seeking to preserve the scarcity value of advanced computing. China is being pushed toward technological self-sufficiency and more economical deployment.

For investors, the emerging distinction is between control over the infrastructure of intelligence and the ability to generate sustainable returns from its widespread use.

The AI Supercycle may remain global in its economic consequences while becoming increasingly divided in its technological foundations.

The next great capital rotation will depend on which side converts that structural change into enduring profitability.
