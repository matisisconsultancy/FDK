The Great Productivity Arbitrage

Why the timing and distribution of the AI productivity dividend could redefine monetary policy, financial stability and global capital allocation.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann November 25, 2026 5 min read Macro

What happens when financial markets capitalise a decade of expected productivity gains before central banks can identify them in the real economy?

A consequential intervention comes from John C. Williams, President of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, in an interview published on November 24 by the ECB's Euro Matters podcast. The emerging monetary dilemma is therefore not simply inflation versus disinflation. It is the asymmetry between the speed of financial capital formation and the speed of economic transformation.

01

The Big Take

Three observations stand out

AI could sustain significantly higher productivity growth for a decade or more, although acceleration is not yet apparent in aggregate US productivity data.

Demand for semiconductors and memory chips is already contributing to consumer-goods inflation.

The migration of AI financing towards less transparent structured debt warrants increasing scrutiny.

02

The Hidden Pattern — The Three Clocks Of The AI Supercycle

FDK conceptual framework

Financial markets anticipate. Infrastructure absorbs capital. Productivity requires organisational transformation. Financial expectations, physical investment and realised productivity move at different speeds.

Central banks must navigate the interval between these three processes. The longer that interval, the greater the risk that inflation, restrictive monetary policy and refinancing costs undermine the investment required to deliver the productivity dividend itself.

THE THREE CLOCKS

Central banks must navigate the interval between financial expectation, physical investment and realised productivity — and the longer that interval, the greater the risk.

03

The Historical Precedent

1995–2005

Williams draws an important parallel with the American productivity acceleration between 1995 and 2005.

04

The Second-Order Effect — Who Captures The Productivity Dividend?

Distribution of value

Perhaps the most consequential question raised by Williams concerns the distribution of AI-generated economic value.

If productivity gains are retained predominantly through higher corporate margins rather than passed through to consumers, the disinflationary effect could be significantly weaker than markets anticipate. Industrial AI can lower production costs without proportionately lowering consumer prices.

Competition, pricing power and the distribution of income therefore become increasingly relevant to monetary policy.

05

The Great Split — Financial Market Implications

Divergent outcomes across the capital structure

01

Equities

Rotation towards companies demonstrating sustainable AI-driven returns on capital.

02

Duration risk

Persistent inflation and potentially higher neutral real rates.

03

Spread risk

Repricing of leverage, concentration, liquidity and earnings uncertainty.

04

Default risk

Delayed monetisation, refinancing pressure and insufficient debt-service capacity.

05

Infrastructure

Premium for contracted revenues, competitive electricity and financing resilience.

06

Private credit

Greater scrutiny of structured financing and opaque leverage.

THE DANGEROUS ASSUMPTION

A transformative technology can coexist with widespread capital misallocation — technological success does not automatically guarantee financial success.

06

The European Policy Imperative

Adoption over replication

Europe does not necessarily need to replicate the entire American AI infrastructure ecosystem to capture substantial productivity gains. Its opportunity lies in accelerating Industrial AI adoption across existing productive assets, while developing sufficient sovereign technological capacity and competitive energy infrastructure.

In a separate November 24 speech in Vienna, ECB Vice-President Boris Vujčić emphasised the importance of European integration, diversified financing and structural adaptability in building economic resilience. This reinforces the broader case for deeper European capital markets.

07

What's Next

Two monetary and supervisory checkpoints

NOV 25 — Bank of England, Parliamentary testimony. 14:15 GMT. Governor Andrew Bailey and MPC members face questions following the increase in Bank Rate to 4%, with energy inflation and productivity among the anticipated topics.

NOV 26 — Bank of England, Financial Stability Report. 10:30 GMT. The critical issues to monitor are AI-related leverage, structured finance, private credit and cross-market contagion.

Today's parliamentary testimony has not yet taken place at the time of this assessment. No comparably consequential new AI-specific policy decision was identified from MAS, SNB or BIS. Williams's intervention represents an important analytical signal, not a formal change in monetary policy.

Key Takeaways For Investors And Policymakers

Five mandates

  • Investors: distinguish productivity expectations from realised free cash flow.
  • Bondholders: separate risk-free duration exposure, credit-spread sensitivity and actual default probability.
  • Central bankers: monitor who captures productivity gains, rather than assuming automatic disinflation.
  • Supervisors: investigate structured AI financing, off-balance-sheet commitments and interconnected exposures.
  • EU policymakers: prioritise the speed of Industrial AI adoption, competitive electricity, market competition and capital-market integration.

The next great market rotation will be determined not simply by how much productivity AI creates, but by who captures its economic value — and how long capital must wait to realise it.
