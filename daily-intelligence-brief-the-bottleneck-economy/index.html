Daily Intelligence Brief the Bottleneck Economy

The AI Supercycle is becoming a system of interdependent bottlenecks — and the next excess returns may sit where scarce complementary capabilities constrain the productive deployment of capital.

The AI Supercycle is entering a phase in which the scarcity of complementary capabilities may become as important to financial returns as the scarcity of computing power itself.

The competition for advanced processors is increasingly extending into semiconductor packaging, specialized equipment, electricity infrastructure and the financing needed to turn individual technologies into commercially productive systems. As investment removes one constraint, the economic value associated with scarcity may simply migrate toward another.

The next source of excess returns may therefore be found where scarce capabilities constrain the productive deployment of capital across the AI value chain — and the central question is which constraints are binding, who holds pricing power, and how long it lasts.

01

Advanced Packaging Becomes the Binding Constraint

Accelerated computing

Reuters' November 6 investigation identifies advanced packaging — the integration of processors, memory and components into high-performance assemblies — as a critical production limit. Industry research projects roughly $28 billion of advanced-packaging capex in 2026, with advanced spending expected to overtake conventional packaging that year. Powertech and Broadcom plan a $400M Singapore venture, with production from 2028; TSMC's CoWoS remains the principal high-end technology.

The critical limit is no longer making individual chips but integrating them into usable systems — so a single packaging facility can govern the effective output of many upstream suppliers. The financial value of a bottleneck depends not on its existence but on its persistence and its owners' ability to capture the associated rents.

02

Strategic Capital Is Blurring the Lines

The great split

On November 6 Alphabet disclosed a ~$548M Qualcomm stake, accumulated in Q3; Qualcomm targets roughly $15B of data-centre chip revenue by 2029 — a management ambition, not realized revenue. Technology platforms now participate as customers, operators and investors at once, improving revenue visibility but concentrating exposure to the same cycle.

On November 7 Berkshire Hathaway reported $12.705B operating earnings (−5.8% YoY), $22.112B net earnings, ~$9.6B of Q3 buybacks and ~$356.9B in cash. Its discipline offers a contrast to extraordinary AI capital commitments. Neither model is inherently superior; the test is identical — will incremental returns exceed the cost of capital?

The financial winners of the AI Supercycle may change as the location and persistence of scarcity evolve.

03

The Physical and Productivity Constraints Remain

Energy and returns

EU data centres consumed ~68 TWh in 2024 and could reach 114 TWh by 2030 — over 3% of total demand. Announced megawatts are not operational megawatts: projects need electricity, grid connections and commissioning before generating revenue. Coordinated November 6–7 demonstrations, including an incursion at a Microsoft-associated Amsterdam site, underline public-acceptance risk, though no moratorium has been adopted.

Meanwhile the productivity dividend stays unproven. ECB data show euro-area digital investment rising to ~3.3% of GDP, and AI adoption in EU strategic sectors reached 18% in 2025 (34% in energy, 30% in electronics) — but no verified economy-wide acceleration in realized returns.

04

Europe's Test Moves to Execution

Policy watch

The Commission's November 4 European Chip Design Platform mobilizes €525M, in a segment where European companies hold under 1% of global revenues. The EuroHPC AI Gigafactories programme still envisages up to seven facilities, as much as €10B public and at least €20B private funding, with Lot 2 due December 3, 2026 and Lot 1 February 4, 2027 — ambitions, not completed investments.

Europe's objective is not to raise its share of AI capital expenditure, but to raise its share of the economic value that expenditure generates.

Key takeaways

For investors, boards and policymakers

  • AI economics increasingly depend on complementary capabilities; packaging shows one scarce process can constrain far larger investments.
  • Strategic capital relationships — like Alphabet's Qualcomm stake — deepen interdependence among platforms, suppliers and infrastructure.
  • Financial discipline is a competitive advantage; Berkshire's results model capital allocation amid vast AI commitments.
  • The productivity dividend remains insufficiently established at the aggregate level.
  • Europe's opportunity is to capture more value across the complete AI production system.

The AI Supercycle is creating extraordinary demand. The next financial advantage will belong to those who anticipate where scarcity moves — and invest before its economic value is fully priced.
