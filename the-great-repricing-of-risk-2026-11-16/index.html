The Great Repricing of Risk

The next phase of the AI Supercycle will be accompanied by a fundamental reallocation of financial risk—between public markets, private capital, asset managers, credit providers and end investors.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann November 16, 2026 9 min read Capital

“The next great market rotation may be from financial promises to financial resilience.”

The signal1 min

  2. BlackRock is seeking approval for an exchange-traded interval fund combining ETF trading with private-market exposure, targeting a ~$16 trillion US ETF industry.
  3. The AI Supercycle is entering a capital-discipline phase distinguishing technological leadership from sustainable earnings and financing capacity.
  4. A structural mismatch is forming: long-duration transformation financed through structures whose investors may expect much shorter-duration liquidity.
  5. Base case is disciplined AI repricing at 50%, renewed acceleration at 30% and a liquidity-led correction at 20%.

What if the most consequential development in financial markets is not the prospect of another AI correction, but the simultaneous transformation of how risk is priced, distributed and ultimately absorbed by investors?

Two developments deserve to be considered together. The first is the growing sensitivity of global equity markets to AI-related earnings, valuations and expectations surrounding the next generation of technology companies. The second is the expansion of asset-management products that seek to combine the accessibility of public-market investment vehicles with exposure to private-market assets.

At first glance these appear unrelated. But their intersection reveals a deeper structural change: the financial system is increasingly financing long-duration economic transformation through investment structures whose investors may expect much shorter-duration liquidity. This mismatch does not necessarily produce a crisis. It does change where financial fragility accumulates, how corrections propagate and which institutions ultimately bear the consequences.

THE DECISIVE QUESTION

The question is no longer merely which companies will benefit from AI. It is which assets can justify their valuations, which investment structures can withstand liquidity shocks, and which institutions possess the balance-sheet capacity to finance the transition.

01

The Market Signal: AI Is Entering Its Capital-Discipline Phase

AI valuations

On November 16, Nasdaq futures declined as investors prepared for Nvidia's November 17 earnings and Anthropic's anticipated public-market debut later in the week. Nvidia's leadership disruption has intensified uncertainty, while Anthropic's offering represents an unusually large test of institutional demand for AI exposure.

For several years, financial markets have rewarded AI companies primarily for their perceived capacity to capture future economic growth, capitalizing distant earnings expectations at exceptionally high valuations. That regime is becoming more demanding.

The market is increasingly distinguishing between three different sources of value: technological leadership, sustainable earnings generation and the capacity to finance future expansion without excessive dilution or balance-sheet deterioration. These are not equivalent. A company may possess extraordinary technological capabilities and still deliver disappointing shareholder returns if its valuation already discounts decades of exceptional performance. Conversely, a company with less technological prominence may generate superior risk-adjusted returns through predictable cash flows, disciplined investment and resilient margins.

The second-order effect is a potential rotation within the AI Supercycle itself—from the ownership of technological expectations toward the ownership of demonstrable economic returns. This does not necessarily imply the end of the AI bull market. It implies a more discriminating market in which earnings quality, governance, financing costs and capital intensity become increasingly decisive.

02

The BlackRock Signal: The Boundaries of Financial Markets Are Being Redrawn

ETIF

BlackRock's proposed exchange-traded interval fund, or ETIF, represents a different but potentially equally consequential development. According to Bloomberg's November 16 report, BlackRock is seeking regulatory approval for a structure intended to combine elements of exchange-traded investing with access to private-market assets. The initiative targets a U.S. ETF industry of approximately $16 trillion.

The important distinction is between trading liquidity and underlying asset liquidity. A vehicle may permit investors to trade shares without providing an unconditional right to redeem underlying private investments at their reported valuations. Secondary-market liquidity, redemption rights and portfolio liquidity remain separate characteristics—a distinction that becomes critical during periods of financial stress.

BlackRock's broader private-markets outlook already identifies expanding access for wealth investors, evergreen structures and the integration of public and private investments as major industry trends. The implications are substantial. First, private markets may increasingly become a mainstream component of investment portfolios rather than an allocation reserved principally for institutional investors. Second, the distribution power of large asset managers could become as important as their investment-selection capabilities. Third, the pricing of illiquid assets may become more closely connected to the daily volatility of public financial markets. And fourth, the competitive boundary between asset managers, banks, private-equity firms and credit providers may progressively erode.

The hidden pattern is the industrialization of private capital distribution. Financial institutions are not merely developing new investment products. They are competing to control the channels through which long-term capital is mobilized, packaged, priced and distributed. That creates substantial commercial opportunities. It also introduces a new question: whether increasingly accessible investment structures can preserve financial resilience when the underlying assets remain difficult to value or liquidate.

03

The Five Second-Order Effects That Matter Most

mechanisms

1. The rotation from growth expectations to capital efficiency. Higher discount rates and greater scrutiny of AI spending could favor companies that convert investment into operating cash flow. The next leadership group may include selected infrastructure operators, enterprise software providers and industrial adopters—not only semiconductor designers.

2. The convergence of public and private valuations. As private assets become accessible through more widely distributed vehicles, investors may demand greater transparency and more comparable valuation methodologies. Public-market corrections could increasingly influence private-market fundraising, secondary discounts and financing conditions.

3. Liquidity becomes a strategic asset. Investors may increasingly differentiate between the expected return on an asset and the ability to exit that position during market stress. This could increase the premium commanded by genuinely liquid securities, robust balance sheets and vehicles with transparent redemption mechanisms.

4. Financial intermediation shifts toward large capital platforms. The integration of private credit, infrastructure, wealth management and exchange-traded distribution could strengthen global asset-management platforms. Traditional banks may increasingly compete with institutions capable of originating, financing and distributing assets across multiple markets.

5. Correlations rise when investors need liquidity. During a significant correction, investors may sell liquid public holdings to meet obligations or rebalance portfolios containing less-liquid private assets. The resulting selling pressure can spread beyond the original source of financial stress.

The fifth mechanism deserves particular attention. BlackRock previously highlighted how private-market exposure increases the importance of liquid portfolio holdings. Reports earlier this year also described redemption pressures in private-credit funds and the use of bond ETFs as portfolio liquidity reserves. This creates a potential paradox: the greater the allocation to private assets, the more important the liquidity of the remaining public portfolio becomes. Consequently, private-market expansion can amplify public-market selling pressure even when the underlying private assets are not themselves being liquidated.

04

The Hidden Transmission Mechanism

illustrative causal chain

The potential causal chain is illustrative, not inevitable. An AI valuation and earnings shock lowers expected returns or raises required risk premiums. In public markets, that drives equity repricing and portfolio deleveraging. In private markets, it means higher financing costs and valuation uncertainty. A liquidity and capital-allocation adjustment follows—asset sales, repricing of credit and reduced risk capacity—producing new market leadership as capital shifts toward resilient earnings, financing capacity and credible valuations.

The mechanism is conditional. It becomes more powerful when leverage is elevated, liquidity buffers are inadequate or investors are forced to reduce exposure simultaneously.

05

Three Scenarios for the Months Ahead

analytical judgments, not statistical forecasts

50%

Disciplined AI Repricing

AI investment continues, but valuation dispersion increases. Quality earnings and cash generation outperform speculative growth.

30%

Renewed AI Acceleration

Strong earnings and successful capital raising restore confidence. AI leadership broadens, supporting equities and investment activity.

20%

Liquidity-Led Correction

Earnings disappointments, tighter financing and private-market redemption pressures reinforce one another. Credit spreads widen and correlations rise.

06

The Central Scenario

transition, not collapse

The central scenario is not a collapse in AI investment. It is a transition toward a market that requires greater evidence of returns. Technological adoption can accelerate even as valuations decline. Indeed, a correction in the cost of capital and a redistribution of investment opportunities could ultimately improve the economic sustainability of the AI Supercycle. The principal downside risk is that the adjustment becomes disorderly because financing structures and investor liquidity expectations are insufficiently aligned.

07

What to Watch This Week

three tests

Three developments will help determine whether the current weakness remains a contained repricing or develops into a broader risk-reduction cycle.

Nvidia's November 17 earnings will test confidence in the scale and durability of AI infrastructure spending, as well as management's ability to maintain strategic and operational continuity.

Anthropic's anticipated November 19 debut will test the willingness of investors to allocate substantial new capital to AI growth. A successful offering would not necessarily be positive for existing technology stocks: investors may sell established holdings to finance their participation in the new issue.

Federal Reserve communications and the October meeting minutes will provide another test of the interest-rate outlook. Higher expected policy rates would increase the discount-rate pressure on long-duration growth assets and raise financing costs across public and private markets.

The BlackRock initiative operates on a longer horizon. Its significance will depend on regulatory approval, the final product structure, valuation arrangements, redemption provisions and actual investor adoption. The most important indicators to monitor are therefore not confined to equity prices. They include Treasury yields, investment-grade and high-yield credit spreads, private-credit redemption activity, secondary-market discounts and the breadth of equity-market participation.

Five Takeaways for Investors and Boards of Directors

the board-level read

  • Remain constructive on the AI Supercycle, but more selective on valuations. Technological leadership alone is insufficient to justify an investment premium.
  • Treat liquidity as a source of financial value. Asset liquidity, redemption rights and the ability to finance positions through a correction should be assessed separately.
  • Watch the migration of risk into private markets. Greater access creates opportunities, but also introduces valuation, governance and liquidity challenges.
  • Identify the beneficiaries of financial-market convergence. Large asset managers, capital-market infrastructure providers and institutions with strong distribution capabilities may capture an increasing share of financial intermediation.
  • Prepare for a change in market leadership. The next phase could reward those capable of delivering sustainable returns on invested capital rather than simply expanding assets, revenues or commitments.
08

The Closing Perspective

inflection point

The two Bloomberg stories are more closely connected than they first appear. Nvidia and Anthropic represent the increasingly demanding economics of technological transformation. BlackRock represents the transformation of the financial architecture being developed to fund, distribute and ultimately absorb the risks of that transformation.

The AI Supercycle is creating extraordinary demand for long-term capital. Meanwhile, the financial industry is developing new structures to mobilize that capital across a wider investor base. The opportunity is substantial. So is the challenge of ensuring that financing structures remain consistent with the economic characteristics of the assets they hold.

The next great market rotation may not be simply from technology to traditional industries, or from growth to value. It may be from financial promises to financial resilience. And that is where the next generation of investment leadership will be decided.

In the economy of accelerating intelligence, capital will remain abundant for credible opportunities. What will become increasingly scarce—and increasingly valuable—is the capacity to absorb risk without surrendering liquidity, discipline or control.
