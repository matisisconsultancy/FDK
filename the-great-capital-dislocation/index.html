The Great Capital Dislocation

Friday's rebound masks an emerging dislocation between equity valuations, corporate financing conditions and the geography of capital.

Global equity markets closed the week with a powerful rebound. But beneath the recovery, a more consequential financial development is unfolding: investors withdrew $30.57 billion from US equity funds while European equity funds attracted $7.29 billion in the week ending November 25.

The Nasdaq recovered 2.31% today. Yet Treasury yields remain close to 5%, credit markets are questioning the sustainability of AI infrastructure investment, and France has entered a technical recession. The next phase of the AI Supercycle may be defined less by technological acceleration than by the financial system's increasingly selective willingness to fund it.

01

The Anatomy of a Fragile Recovery

Five signals, three systems

Wall Street rebounded on the shortened Black Friday session — Nasdaq +2.31% — but thin volumes weaken the signal. Beijing's pledge of state-backed support lifted the CSI 300 by 4.20%. Treasury yields rose even as equities recovered, with futures pricing an almost certain December Fed hike.

France's revised Q3 GDP confirmed a −0.2% contraction, complicating fiscal consolidation, while Spanish HICP eased to 4.1% and French to 3.2% — both still above the ECB's target. Three systems, three dynamics: the United States absorbing the consequences of a vast private investment cycle, China mobilizing state-backed capital, Europe attracting portfolio inflows despite deteriorating fundamentals. Technological, financial and industrial leadership are becoming distinct sources of power.

02

Follow the Capital

The first evidence of a geographical rotation

Fund-flow data give a sharper signal than the rebound alone. US technology funds lost $9.27bn; short-to-intermediate government bonds drew $5.21bn; global money-market funds shed $49.42bn. One week does not establish a structural rotation, but these figures reveal growing sensitivity to US concentration, technology valuations and the cost of corporate financing.

The same supercycle affects balance sheets very differently. Dell reported a $132.3bn AI backlog and $10.8bn year-to-date operating cash flow; CoreWeave guides to $37–39bn of capex — near three times projected 2026 revenue; Oracle faces roughly 9% long-dated yields; Siemens Energy generates €8.8bn of pre-tax free cash flow from physical infrastructure. The distinction is between businesses monetizing today's cycle and those financing capacity whose returns will materialize over years. Credit markets are already warning: CoreWeave's 5-year CDS stands at 1,314 bps against SoftBank's 501 bps. The next major AI valuation adjustment could begin in the bond market, not the stock market.

The next industrial revolution must become a balance-sheet revolution.

03

The Next Market Inflection

Catalysts for Monday, November 30

The weekend concentrates political, energy and monetary risk: S&P's scheduled review of France's sovereign rating; Spain's general election; OPEC+ production policy, with expectations favouring unchanged targets; Bank of Japan Governor Ueda; and China's official manufacturing PMI, where October's 49.3 is the reference and a reading above 50 would strengthen the stabilization thesis.

The decisive question is whether Friday's recovery survives the return of normal volumes. A rebound accompanied by narrower credit spreads would strengthen the stabilization thesis; a rebound alongside further credit deterioration would confirm that markets remain vulnerable to another major repricing.

The Velocity Insight

Five strategic takeaways

  • Investors: follow capital flows and credit spreads, not equity momentum alone — falling prices are not cheap valuations when financing risk is rising.
  • Boards: reassess capex, acquisitions and refinancing under more demanding cost-of-capital assumptions.
  • Industrial executives: deploy AI where it releases working capital, improves asset utilization, reduces energy intensity and expands margins.
  • Central bankers: recognize the divergence between the near-term inflationary effects of the AI cycle and its long-term productivity benefits.
  • European policymakers: integrate debt sustainability, industrial competitiveness, energy independence and capital-market development — Europe cannot finance its renaissance through fiscal expansion alone.

Friday's recovery should not be mistaken for resolution. The next market leaders will not be those commanding the largest budgets, but those generating the greatest incremental value from every unit of capital — the age of capital abundance is giving way to the age of capital selection.
