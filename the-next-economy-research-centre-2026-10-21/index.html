The Next Economy Research Centre

The AI supercycle is producing record revenues — but electricity delays are beginning to destroy financial value, and the market is learning to tell the difference.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann October 21, 2026 5 min read Energy

“Capital determines what can be financed. Electricity determines what can be delivered.”

The most consequential development in the AI supercycle is no longer the extraordinary amount of capital flowing into computing infrastructure. It is the growing financial divergence between infrastructure that can generate revenue and infrastructure that cannot be delivered on schedule.

Three developments over the past 24 hours make that distinction unusually clear. Technology suppliers are monetizing demand. Operators with functioning capacity are beginning to monetize utilization. Developers dependent on future electricity and construction face increasing financial risk.

The AI supercycle is not experiencing a uniform demand reversal. It is undergoing a more discriminating financial selection process.

When Electricity Delays Destroy Shareholder Value

A physical electricity delay has directly affected published revenue guidance, earnings expectations, capital expenditure and market valuation — not merely construction costs, but near-term revenue from infrastructure for which customer demand already exists.

01

Capital Formation and the Great Split

NextDC

Australia's NextDC disclosed that electricity distributor Jemena suspended network upgrades required to power its M3 Melbourne facility, following community opposition. The next increase in grid supply is not expected until fiscal 2028.

The company cut FY2027 net revenue guidance to A$475–500M (from A$615–640M) and underlying EBITDA to A$245–265M (from A$385–410M) — reductions of roughly 22% and 36% at the midpoints. Capital expenditure guidance fell to A$4.50–5.00B. Approximately A$140M of contracted revenue is being deferred, and NextDC has suspended new projects and land acquisitions while evaluating asset disposals.

Affected contracts include service-level credits for late or under-powered delivery, and in certain cases customer termination rights. The transmission is now explicit: grid delay → deferred commissioning → deferred contracted revenue → weaker EBITDA → equity repricing. Yet A$8.675B of reported liquidity at September 30, with covenant compliance, shows why a large equity decline is not imminent distress.

02

The Demand Cycle Remains Strong

Supermicro

Supermicro raised preliminary Q1 FY2027 revenue to $18.5–19.5B from $14.5–15.5B, with gross margins now expected at 12–12.5%, citing AI platform demand. Final results are due November 3.

The contrast with struggling developers is not contradictory. Server manufacturers receive revenue as customers purchase systems; operators must subsequently convert those systems into commercially utilized capacity. Hardware demand can accelerate even as the economics of financing and operating capacity become more demanding. The semiconductor baseline is unchanged — TSMC at $46.72B revenue (66.4% margin), ASML at €11.66B (57.5% margin).

03

The Second AI Economy

SpaceX

SpaceX reported Q3 consolidated revenue of $14.58B, up 161% year over year, with its AI business — largely renting computing capacity to third-party developers — generating $7.56B, up from ~$813M. Consolidated net income reached $4.4B, against a $1.1B loss a year earlier.

But quarterly capital expenditure reached $19.6B, including ~$16.4B toward AI infrastructure, and is expected to rise further. One company is realizing rapid revenue from existing capacity; the other is deferring contracted revenue because electricity cannot be delivered. Even rapid growth does not establish durable returns: incremental margins, depreciation, customer concentration and free cash flow remain the decisive questions. SpaceX and Tesla have also formed special committees to study a possible combination — no terms agreed, and management notes a merger may never occur.

04

Electricity as a Gatekeeper of Capital

IEA

The IEA's October 14 report, examining 34 jurisdictions covering ~90% of global data-centre capacity, estimates hyperscalers will spend more than $700B in 2026 CapEx. It warns that grid-connection queues in some jurisdictions exceed existing peak demand — though these include speculative and duplicated applications.

Financial capital moves faster than physical infrastructure. The critical variable is increasingly the probability that a project receives usable electricity by its contractual commissioning date — distinguishing a requested connection, a confirmed agreement, a financed upgrade and a completed, energized connection. The market may progressively demand evidence of the final stages before assigning value to future computing revenue.

05

Europe's Position

Earnings and Implementation

The Siemens–Nvidia Erlangen demonstration reported up to 85% reductions in engineering effort and up to 20% production-planning savings — specific workflows, not economy-wide gains. Nvidia's Barcelona Supercomputing Center AI Factory Accelerator is now operational, though its productivity contribution is unquantified. Eurostat shows Eurozone industrial production down 0.5% month-on-month and 1.1% year-on-year in August.

LSEG I/B/E/S estimates put STOXX 600 Q3 earnings up 20.7% year over year, with technology near 33% — partly from energy profits and data-centre suppliers. Europe may capture significant value from the supercycle without leading every frontier category, via ASML, ABB, Schneider Electric and Siemens. But the EU's Gigafactories ambition — more than €30B mobilized — should emphasize electricity readiness, financing credibility and implementation timetables, not announced scale alone.

Five Key Takeaways

For investors, boards and policymakers

  • Electricity availability is becoming a measurable financial variable — NextDC's cut directly hit contracted revenue and shareholder value.
  • Strong AI demand does not guarantee attractive infrastructure returns; Supermicro and SpaceX growth coexists with heavy capital needs and developer stress.
  • The financial system is becoming more selective, not uniformly distressed — South Korea's Elice fell ~32% on debut; CoreWeave 2031 bonds yield nearly 16%.
  • Europe's opportunity extends beyond building infrastructure, into industrial AI, electrical equipment and engineering software.
  • The next rotation may reward deliverable capacity over announced capacity — infrastructure that can be powered, commissioned and commercially utilized on credible timelines.

Capital determines what can be financed. Electricity determines what can be delivered. Cash flow determines what can survive. Productivity determines what creates wealth.
