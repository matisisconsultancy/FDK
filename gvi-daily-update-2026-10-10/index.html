GVI Daily Update

The next Great Split is moving from financial markets into the industrial economy, where capital and intelligence are abundant but the ability to convert them into productive output is becoming sharply uneven.

FdK Francesco De Leo Kaufmann October 10, 2026 5 min read Macro
108.0Headline GVI (last published baseline, retained)
+12.0%Dutch manufacturing output, year-on-year
+45.1%Dutch machinery production
−1.3%Italian industrial output, August

“Capital creates potential. Intelligence creates capability. Industrial productivity creates wealth.”

The next Great Split is moving from financial markets into the industrial economy, and the evidence from October 9 reveals a striking divergence.

The Netherlands recorded 12.0% annual manufacturing-output growth, its strongest in more than four years, with machinery production surging 45.1%. Sweden reported a 5.8% annual increase in industrial production and a preliminary 1.1% monthly GDP rebound. Italy moved the opposite way: industrial output contracted 1.3% in August, with the three-month trend declining 1.1%.

Meanwhile, China announced new guidelines to accelerate industrial AI while explicitly discouraging speculative investment. In the United States, preliminary consumer sentiment deteriorated as inflation expectations rose, even as equity markets recovered.

Capital is available. Intelligence is expanding. But the ability to transform both into productive industrial output is becoming increasingly uneven. The divergence is measured; its connection to AI-driven productivity remains a hypothesis to test rather than an established causal relationship.

THE NEXT ADVANTAGE

The next competitive advantage will not be measured by the intelligence an economy can acquire, but by the industrial productivity it can generate from that intelligence.

Five Signals Defining Global Velocity

October 9 evidence

  1. The Dutch manufacturing index reached a new high in August: output expanded 3.3% month-on-month and 12.0% year-on-year, across more than 60% of sectors. Machinery production rose 45.1%, transport equipment 8.3%. This is the strongest evidence of sustained acceleration — but higher output is measured, while AI productivity gains still require company-level evidence. signal: Advanced machinery and engineering economies are positioned to supply the next phase of industrial modernisation.

  2. Industrial production rose 5.8% year-on-year, new orders 2.0% month-on-month and 5.1% annually, export orders 9.0% year-on-year. The preliminary GDP indicator suggests a 1.1% monthly rebound. However, services production fell 0.8% month-on-month and the estimate remains subject to revision. signal: Improving momentum justifies a limited upgrade rather than a broader reassessment.

  3. Production declined 1.3% in August, with the three-month trend down 1.1%. The deterioration was broad: intermediate goods −1.8%, consumer goods −1.2%, capital goods −0.9%, with energy the lone gain at +2.8%. Year-on-year production was unchanged, limiting the case for a larger downgrade. signal: Substantial industrial capabilities are not yet translating into sustained acceleration.

  4. China's October 9 guidelines reaffirm commitment to AI infrastructure and industrial applications, but warn against speculative investment, duplication and financial excess. Beijing may increasingly differentiate between companies building strategic capabilities and those expanding capacity without sustainable demand. This is a policy signal; China's score is unchanged. signal: Industrial AI remains a strategic priority, but capital efficiency is becoming an explicit policy concern.

  5. The University of Michigan's preliminary October sentiment index fell to 46.3 from 48.1. One-year inflation expectations rose to 4.7% and longer-term to 3.5%. Yet the S&P 500 gained roughly 0.6% on October 9, with the 10-year yield around 5.25%. signal: America's technological leadership is intact; the distribution of its benefits is the more important question.

04

Second-Order Effects — What Comes Next

How the divergence compounds

The industrial productivity gap could become a capital-allocation gap. If higher production translates into operating cash flow, companies can finance further modernisation; weaker producers face lower utilisation — raising the possibility of cumulative divergence.

The AI premium moves toward industrial implementation. The next phase depends on embedding intelligence into productive systems, favouring machinery, industrial software, automation and asset optimisation.

Europe could experience an internal Great Split. The Netherlands and Sweden accelerate, Germany recovers unevenly, Italy deteriorates. Capital-market integration alone will not suffice; investment must reach enterprises able to use it productively. The winners will not be those announcing the largest AI expenditures, but those converting investment into output fastest and most profitably.

Five Key Takeaways

From signals to decisions

  • Investors: follow industrial conversion, not announcements — prioritise rising production, profitable utilisation and cash generation.
  • Boards: measure productivity per unit of capital — assess AI through incremental margins and returns.
  • Executives: shorten the time between adoption and measurable operational improvement.
  • European policymakers: address the internal industrial divide through targeted investment finance and faster implementation.
  • Capital allocators: maintain discipline — capital, capacity, utilisation, productivity, cash flow, with ROIC > WACC the critical test.

The next Great Split may emerge not between economies that invest in intelligence and those that do not, but between those capable of converting technological investment into sustained industrial productivity — and the next excess return belongs to those that convert capital, intelligence and productivity into cash flow.
