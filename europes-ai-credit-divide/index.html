Europe’s AI Credit Divide

The AI Supercycle is accelerating banking productivity precisely as Europe's productive economy faces increasingly restrictive financing conditions.

The Big Take — The AI Supercycle is accelerating banking productivity precisely as Europe's productive economy faces increasingly restrictive financing conditions.

Two consequential developments on October 26 expose a new dimension of the Great Split. The European Central Bank's latest enterprise survey confirms deteriorating credit availability, higher borrowing costs and increasingly cautious lending across the euro area. Meanwhile, Deutsche Bank reports that artificial intelligence has reduced some corporate lending decisions from weeks to minutes.

The contradiction is consequential: Europe is developing the technological capacity to allocate capital faster while its companies are finding that capital increasingly difficult to obtain. This distinction goes directly to the monetary-policy challenge confronting the ECB. Industrial-AI deflation requires productive investment. If financing conditions prevent companies from adopting AI, Europe may experience the inflationary costs of the Supercycle without capturing its productivity dividend.

01

The ECB: Monetary Restraint Is Reaching the Productive Economy

Signal 1

The ECB's October 26 Survey on the Access to Finance of Enterprises provides the most consequential new macroeconomic evidence. Businesses report that borrowing costs continue to increase while credit availability deteriorates.

The deterioration is particularly important because firms are simultaneously experiencing pressure on working capital from higher energy and other input costs. The survey's financing-gap indicator remains positive, although it declined slightly to 2% from 3%. Credit availability nevertheless fell more sharply than financing needs.

The results demonstrate tightening credit conditions, but do not establish that an economy-wide credit crunch is underway. One further qualification matters: substantial changes in the survey's respondent composition complicate comparisons of numerical inflation and price expectations. The ECB reports much smaller changes among firms participating in consecutive surveys.

The monetary transmission mechanism. Energy and input-cost inflation → higher working-capital requirements → tighter credit conditions → lower productive investment.

This creates a potential conflict between short-term inflation control and long-term productivity formation. Restrictive monetary conditions can help contain demand and inflation. But persistent financing constraints can also postpone investment in automation, advanced computing and industrial modernisation.

The second-order consequence is critical. Europe could reduce inflationary demand today while simultaneously delaying the productivity improvements needed to sustain disinflation tomorrow. That is a central risk in the transition from AI infrastructure inflation to Industrial-AI deflation.

02

Deutsche Bank: AI Is Transforming the Velocity of Capital Allocation

Signal 2

Today's Reuters interview with Deutsche Bank technology chief Marie-Jeanne Deverdun supplies the corporate counterpart. The bank reports that AI has reduced decision times for certain smaller corporate loans from weeks to minutes. For some multinational corporate lending decisions, processing times have declined from months to weeks.

Deutsche Bank plans technology expenditure exceeding €2.5 billion between 2026 and 2028. It has also developed an internal platform, ADA, designed to supervise AI agents, manage operational risks and enable switching between AI models. The bank is preparing for interactions between its own agents and those deployed by corporate clients.

The second-order effect. This development creates opportunities for banking productivity. Faster processing can reduce administrative costs, accelerate customer responses and potentially improve capital allocation. But faster credit decisions do not automatically mean greater credit availability. AI can accelerate the allocation of capital without increasing the quantity of capital available for investment.

This distinction is fundamental. The next competitive divide in European banking may therefore emerge between institutions using AI primarily to reduce operating costs and institutions capable of combining technological efficiency with superior credit assessment, risk management and productive lending.

There is also a supervisory dimension. If banks increasingly rely on similar AI models and data, their credit decisions could become more correlated during periods of economic stress. That remains a prospective systemic risk, not a demonstrated consequence of Deutsche Bank's deployment.

03

The BIS: Artificial Intelligence May Change Monetary Transmission Itself

Signal 3

A BIS intervention delivered on October 20 and published on October 21 provides additional analytical context. Tao Zhang, the BIS Chief Representative for Asia and the Pacific, identified three interconnected risks: faster algorithmic price adjustments, correlated financial decisions and increasingly sophisticated cyber threats.

The first is particularly important for monetary policy. AI-enabled pricing systems allow companies to respond more rapidly to changes in energy costs, exchange rates and demand. As adoption broadens, these capabilities could accelerate the transmission of inflationary shocks.

The second concerns financial stability. Institutions relying on similar analytical models may respond simultaneously to changing market conditions, potentially amplifying portfolio adjustments and credit contraction. The BIS advocates better supervisory visibility, improved data and international coordination. It has not announced a new binding regulatory framework through this intervention.

The emerging monetary paradox. AI may eventually lower production costs. Yet its deployment could also accelerate the transmission of inflation and financial shocks. The same technology that increases productive efficiency may increase the velocity of economic instability. Central banks must therefore distinguish productivity improvements from changes in the speed and amplification of monetary transmission.

04

The Great Split: Europe's Productivity Dividend Is Constrained by Capital Formation

Signal 4

The new evidence becomes particularly consequential when combined with earlier ECB findings. In July, the ECB reported that 72% of surveyed firms anticipated using internal funds to finance AI investment, while approximately 16% expected to use bank loans. These are proportions of firms identifying funding sources, not shares of total investment expenditure.

This suggests a possible structural divide. Companies generating substantial internal cash flows may be better positioned to accelerate AI deployment. Smaller and more financially constrained firms may struggle to make comparable investments. If financing conditions continue deteriorating, the gap could widen.

Strong cash flows → greater AI investment → higher productivity → stronger financial capacity. Conversely: limited financing → delayed AI adoption → weaker productivity → lower future investment capacity.

These are potential reinforcing mechanisms, not proven causal outcomes of today's survey. Nevertheless, they identify a critical European policy challenge. AI adoption is not simply a technological decision. It is increasingly a capital-allocation decision. And the structure of European financial markets may determine how broadly the productivity dividend is distributed.

Five Takeaways for Investors and EU Policymakers

Signal 5

  • Monetary policy — Credit conditions are becoming the critical transmission variable. The ECB must assess whether higher lending costs are restraining inflationary demand or beginning to damage productive investment. The distinction will matter at its October 29 meeting.
  • Banking — AI productivity and credit availability must be evaluated separately. Faster lending decisions can improve efficiency and operating leverage without necessarily expanding lending volumes. Investors should focus on sustainable cost reductions, credit quality and risk-adjusted returns.
  • Industrial-AI deflation — Financing constraints can delay the productivity dividend. The transition to lower unit production costs requires investment in technology, skills and organisational transformation. Tighter credit may postpone those gains.
  • Financial stability — Algorithmic convergence creates a new supervisory challenge. The ECB, BIS and national supervisors should examine whether common AI models and automated risk controls increase correlated lending decisions during periods of financial stress.
  • European competitiveness — Capital-market integration becomes an industrial imperative. Europe's Savings and Investments Union should facilitate the financing of productive AI adoption, particularly among businesses unable to rely exclusively on internal cash generation. The objective must be to turn technological opportunity into broad-based productive capacity.
05

What's Next — Three Monetary Tests

The calendar

The immediate calendar is consequential. On October 27, the ECB publishes its next Bank Lending Survey, providing evidence from the banking side of the credit market. On October 28, the Federal Reserve announces its monetary-policy decision. On October 29, the ECB Governing Council reaches its next monetary-policy decision.

The central question is whether policymakers increasingly acknowledge that restrictive financial conditions can suppress investment before the AI productivity dividend materialises. No new policy-rate decision or formal neutral-rate reassessment is established by today's developments.

06

The Final Take — The Next Great Split Will Be Decided in the Credit Market

Conclusion

The October 26 developments expose an important distinction in the economics of artificial intelligence. Banking technology is advancing rapidly. Credit decisions are becoming faster. Financial institutions are developing more sophisticated capabilities to assess risk and allocate capital. Yet European businesses face increasingly restrictive financing conditions.

The financial system is becoming more intelligent without necessarily becoming more capable of financing economic transformation. For investors, the critical distinction is between productivity improvements that strengthen sustainable cash flows and technological efficiencies that cannot overcome deteriorating financing conditions. For central banks, the challenge is to maintain monetary credibility without unnecessarily constraining the productive investment required for future disinflation. For European policymakers, the imperative is to ensure that access to productive capital does not become the defining constraint on Industrial-AI adoption.

The Great Split is no longer simply about access to artificial intelligence. It is increasingly about access to the capital required to turn intelligence into economic value.

And that distinction will shape Europe's productivity, competitiveness and financial returns throughout the next phase of the AI Supercycle.

Francesco De Leo Kaufmann · The Velocity Edge
