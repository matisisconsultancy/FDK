When the AI Boom Sets the Price of Money

Central banks now face a widening split between economies that can finance AI's inflationary investment and those where the cost of capital prevents productivity from emerging.

Read the full note ↓

The central-bank response to the AI Supercycle has entered a more consequential phase. The question is no longer whether artificial intelligence will influence monetary policy, but which economies can absorb its inflationary investment costs while generating sufficient productivity to justify them.

Three developments over the past 24 hours establish this emerging divide. On October 30, the Bank of Japan explicitly incorporated AI-related demand and rising semiconductor prices into its inflation outlook. The ECB's latest Survey of Professional Forecasters simultaneously raised euro-area inflation expectations for 2027. And new data revealed a striking contrast: the American economy expanded at a 3.4% annualised rate in the third quarter, while France contracted by 0.1%.

The Great Split is now visible in growth, inflation, capital formation and monetary-policy choices. The implications extend beyond interest rates: they concern how international capital will be allocated between economies financing technological expansion and those struggling to preserve their existing productive capacity.

01

The Bank Of Japan: AI Inflation Enters The Official Outlook

AI appears in both the inflation and the risk scenarios

The most consequential new institutional development comes from Tokyo. At its October 30 meeting, the Bank of Japan maintained its policy rate at 1.25%, with one policymaker favouring an increase. But its accompanying outlook contains an important change in emphasis.

The BOJ explicitly identifies rising semiconductor prices, growing AI-related investment demand and higher prices for industrial inputs as potential sources of consumer inflation. It warns that AI demand is already raising prices for semiconductors and durable goods, while higher copper, machinery and other industrial costs could transmit additional pressure. At the same time, it recognises that extraordinary AI investment could generate financial instability if subsequent profits fail to justify the capital committed. AI is therefore appearing simultaneously in the BOJ's inflation assessment and its financial-risk scenarios.

The monetary transmission: AI capital formation → semiconductor and materials demand → higher industrial input prices → consumer-price pass-through → potential monetary tightening.

Industrial-AI deflation requires productivity improvements sufficient to lower unit production costs. Yet the construction of the infrastructure supporting AI can initially increase those costs. The BOJ's assessment provides concrete evidence that the inflationary investment phase is already influencing monetary analysis. Japan also faces imported energy inflation and a weaker yen. The central bank now considers underlying inflation broadly consistent with its 2% objective, although Governor Kazuo Ueda cautions that the durability of this achievement still requires confirmation. The implication is clear: stronger AI demand can bring monetary tightening forward even when the technology's productivity benefits remain uncertain. The BOJ also announced it will accelerate sales of its exchange-traded fund holdings from approximately ¥330 billion to ¥860 billion annually, at book value, beginning in January 2027. It is not specifically an AI-policy measure, but it introduces an additional consideration for Japanese equities while technology valuations remain sensitive to global AI investment expectations.

02

The ECB: Inflation Expectations Rise As European Growth Fractures

A common policy, divergent trajectories

The ECB's fourth-quarter Survey of Professional Forecasters raised inflation expectations for 2027 from 2.2% to 2.6%. Core inflation expectations rose to 2.5%. Longer-term expectations remain anchored at approximately 2%, offering reassurance that the energy shock has not displaced the ECB's objective. Forecasters also expect limited wage-price second-round effects despite higher energy prices.

The significance sharpens alongside today's growth figures. France's economy contracted 0.1% in the third quarter, with investment down 0.6% and exports down 1.7%. Spain expanded 0.6% over the same period, and the euro area as a whole grew approximately 0.4%. Europe is experiencing increasingly differentiated growth conditions under a common monetary policy.

France's weakness is particularly consequential because it coincides with sovereign-market tensions and restrictive financing conditions. The ECB maintained its deposit rate at 2.50% on October 29 and reiterated that the Transmission Protection Instrument remains available to address unwarranted and disorderly market dynamics. This is not an announcement of activation, nor a guarantee against sovereign repricing arising from fundamentals, but it highlights the ECB's sensitivity to financial fragmentation.

The potential transmission mechanism: weaker French growth → greater fiscal pressure → higher sovereign risk premiums → tighter bank financing → lower private investment.

The critical second-order consequence is that weaker investment reduces future productive capacity. This could make France less competitive just as AI-intensive economies accelerate capital formation. The resulting divergence would extend beyond cyclical growth into a structural difference in productivity and expected returns on capital. A common monetary policy cannot guarantee a common trajectory of technological investment.

03

The Federal Reserve: American AI Capital Formation Supports Growth

Investment-driven demand or sustainable productivity?

The Bureau of Economic Analysis reported on October 29 that American GDP expanded at a 3.4% annualised rate in the third quarter, up from 2.2%. Investment was a major contributor; within equipment spending, information-processing equipment provided particularly strong support. Real final sales to private domestic purchasers increased 4.9% annualised. These figures demonstrate strong domestic demand and technology-related capital formation, although they do not isolate AI's contribution or prove that frontier AI has already raised economy-wide productivity.

The distinction is crucial. Investment can strengthen growth while increasing demand for scarce resources; productivity improvements can permit stronger expansion without proportionate inflation. America must determine how much of its acceleration is sustainable productivity growth and how much remains investment-driven demand. Europe must determine how to preserve productive investment while managing energy inflation and financial fragmentation. These are fundamentally different monetary challenges.

Sustained investment demand, higher expected productivity and stronger prospective returns could increase the equilibrium real interest rate, but only if investment translates into persistent gains in productive capacity. An investment boom financed at elevated valuations, followed by disappointing returns, could produce the opposite adjustment. No new AI-specific neutral-rate estimate has been announced by the Fed, ECB or BOJ. The important change is that the forces capable of influencing neutral rates are becoming more visible.

04

The Great Split: Three Monetary Regimes Are Emerging

Analytical characterisations, not permanent classifications

01

United States — Investment-Led Expansion

Strong technology capital expenditure is supporting growth, but policymakers must remain alert to inflation and valuation risks.

02

Japan — AI-Supported Industrial Demand

Stronger technology exports and investment can reinforce underlying inflation, encouraging continued tightening amid normalisation.

03

Europe — Constrained Investment

The ECB must manage price pressures without allowing tighter financial conditions to deepen differences in productive capacity among member states. The next monetary divergence may be driven less by differences in inflation targets than by differences in how economies translate investment into productive growth.

05

Financial Stability: The Supervisory Challenge Accelerates

Preventing technological concentration from becoming financial concentration

On October 30, Reuters reported that India's Reserve Bank is increasing scrutiny of cybersecurity vulnerabilities among financial institutions, including risks associated with AI and third-party technology, following cyber incidents and vulnerabilities in application programming interfaces. The RBI had not publicly confirmed the reported intervention at the time.

The implication is relevant to the ECB, Bank of England, MAS and BIS. As institutions expand AI adoption, they may reduce operating costs while increasing exposure to common technology providers, cyber vulnerabilities and correlated automated decisions. Technological efficiency and operational resilience must therefore be assessed together. The challenge is to prevent technological concentration from becoming financial concentration.

Five Key Takeaways For Investors And European Policymakers

What the October 30 developments mean

  • AI-related inflation is becoming measurable. The BOJ's outlook connects semiconductor prices, industrial inputs and AI investment demand to potential consumer inflation. Monitor cost pass-through before assuming productivity-led disinflation.
  • Europe's monetary challenge is increasingly asymmetric. France's contraction, Spain's growth and elevated euro-area inflation expectations create different financing needs under a common rate. Sovereign spreads and private-credit conditions deserve attention.
  • The next rotation will depend on realised productivity. American technology investment is supporting growth, but valuations ultimately require evidence that capital expenditure generates sustainable cash flows, productivity gains and returns on invested capital.
  • Neutral rates remain an unresolved strategic question. Sustained AI investment could raise equilibrium real rates; disappointed expectations could reverse the effect. Central banks have not established a definitive AI-driven change in their estimates.
  • Europe must protect productive capital formation without suppressing monetary discipline. Integrated capital markets, resilient electricity infrastructure, broader Industrial-AI adoption and stronger supervision are needed to prevent today's growth differences from becoming permanent competitive disadvantages.
06

What Comes Next — Three Decisive Tests

More informative than any single rate announcement

The first is inflation persistence: the ECB must assess whether higher energy prices are generating sustained domestic price pressure. The euro area's October flash inflation estimate is scheduled for November 4.

The second is AI investment profitability: corporate earnings, data-centre commitments, semiconductor orders and credit spreads will indicate whether the current investment expansion remains financially sustainable.

The third is financial fragmentation: French sovereign spreads, bank lending standards and cross-border capital flows will determine whether tighter financing conditions are beginning to impair Europe's ability to invest. These indicators will be more informative than any single interest-rate announcement.

07

The Final Take — Monetary Policy At The Frontier Of Productivity

The deeper meaning of the Great Split

The developments of October 30 reveal a fundamental asymmetry. The inflationary consequences of AI capital formation are becoming increasingly visible. The productivity gains required to justify that investment remain unevenly distributed and difficult to measure. Meanwhile, financial markets are beginning to distinguish economies capable of sustaining extraordinary investment from those confronting tighter credit, higher energy costs and deteriorating growth.

For investors, the critical question is shifting from how much capital is being invested in AI to how effectively that capital can generate economic returns. For central banks, the challenge is to avoid both premature restraint of productive capacity and excessive accommodation of investment-driven inflation. For European policymakers, the stakes are particularly high: Europe risks entering the next productivity cycle with higher financing costs precisely where it most urgently needs productive investment.

That is the deeper meaning of the Great Split. It is not merely a difference in technological capabilities. It is an emerging difference in the ability of economies to finance their future.

The defining monetary divide of the AI Supercycle will separate economies in which productivity determines the cost of capital from those in which the cost of capital prevents productivity from emerging.
